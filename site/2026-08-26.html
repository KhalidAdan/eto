Dolly Parton, country music icon, dies at 80

Dolly Parton, a legendary country singer and songwriter, died at the age of 80 after a brief battle with cancer, according to her team. She was celebrated for her music, which spanned decades and included hits like "I Will Always Love You" and "9 to 5." Parton was also known for her philanthropy, including her work with the Dollywood Foundation, which aimed to improve education in her hometown. She maintained a public stance of neutrality in politics, often emphasizing her role as an entertainer rather than a politician.

Parton's career was marked by her songwriting and performances, with over 3,000 songs attributed to her. She was recognized for her contributions to music, earning numerous awards, including 10 Grammy Awards. Her work extended beyond music, as she also ventured into film and television, and she was known for her distinctive personality and style.

Where the accounts differ

FOX News reports that Parton declined the Presidential Medal of Freedom twice, first due to her husband's illness and second due to the pandemic, and that she was approached by the Biden administration. The Daily Wire does not mention the Medal of Freedom or her health issues. CBS News states that Parton had health issues leading up to her death, including kidney stones and digestive problems, and that her sister Freida Parton had previously raised concerns about her health. The Federalist highlights her songwriting and the emotional depth of her music, including her song "I Will Always Love You," which she wrote for a business partner. The Free Press focuses on her early life and the impact of her music, while the BBC provides detailed information about her early career, including her first performance at the Grand Ole Opry and her family background.

Darline Graham Nordone defeats Ralph Norman in South Carolina Senate GOP runoff

Sen. Darline Graham Nordone (R-SC) won South Carolina’s Republican Senate special primary runoff, defeating Rep. Ralph Norman (R-SC) with 52.3% of the vote compared to Norman’s 47.7%, according to the Associated Press. The race followed a crowded primary where neither candidate secured a majority, forcing a runoff. Graham, appointed by Gov. Henry McMaster (R-SC) to fill her late brother’s Senate seat, received roughly 33% of the vote in the initial primary, while Norman finished second with about 25%. Trump endorsed Graham early in the campaign and campaigned for her in the closing days of the runoff.

Graham’s victory was bolstered by Trump’s support, which became a central theme of the campaign. During a debate, Graham admitted she was “not that informed on national security” when asked about Taiwan and the South China Sea, a comment that drew criticism from Norman and others. Trump defended her, praising her honesty and reiterating his support. Graham will now face Democratic nominee Annie Andrews in November, where she is expected to be the favorite in a state that has not elected a Democratic senator in decades.

Where the accounts differ

The Washington Examiner and The Daily Wire both report Graham received 52.3% of the vote, while Axios states she won with 53% of the vote. The Daily Wire and Straight Arrow News mention Graham’s debate gaffe about national security, while the Washington Examiner and Axios attribute the same comment to her. The Daily Wire and Straight Arrow News note that Norman criticized Graham’s lack of experience, while the Washington Examiner and Axios attribute the criticism to Norman and others. The Washington Examiner and Straight Arrow News mention Trump’s campaign in Myrtle Beach, while the Daily Wire and Axios attribute the same event to Trump. The Washington Examiner and The Daily Wire report Graham’s family ties to South Carolina politics, while the Straight Arrow News and Axios do not mention this detail.

Lindsay Clancy trial: psychologist says he doubts claim she was hallucinating

A forensic psychologist called by the prosecution testified that he does not believe Lindsay Clancy was experiencing acute psychosis when she killed her three children. Kirk Heilbrun, who evaluated Clancy in April, told the court he questioned her claims of hearing a voice that commanded her to commit the act. Heilbrun testified that Clancy killed her children because she wanted to kill herself but did not want them to suffer after she died. Clancy, who is paralyzed from the waist down, is facing murder charges in the deaths of her children, and her defense argues she was suffering from postpartum psychosis. The trial is nearing its conclusion, with the outcome depending heavily on expert testimony.

Prosecutors say Clancy planned the killings and manipulated her husband to leave the house. The defense claims she was in a severe mental state and should not be held criminally responsible. The prosecution has called multiple expert witnesses to challenge the defense's claims, including Dr. Phillip Resnick, who testified that Clancy was clearly psychotic and not in control of her actions. The trial has drawn significant public attention, with supporters of Clancy forming a visible presence in court.

Where the accounts differ

US removes Syria from list of state sponsors of terrorism

The US has removed Syria from its list of state sponsors of terrorism after 47 years, lifting what it said was the "final major barriers" for private sector investment in the country and its reintegration into the global economy. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio stated the move was in recognition of the Syrian government's positive actions to fully distance itself from acts of international terrorism. Syria had been on the list since 1979, with the designation in force throughout Bashar al-Assad’s rule. The removal leaves Cuba, North Korea, and Iran as the remaining countries designated as sponsors of terrorism by the US.

The decision was announced following a 45-day congressional notification period, as part of a process initiated by an executive order signed by President Donald Trump in June 2025. Syria’s new president, Ahmed al-Sharaa, a former militant who once had an American bounty on his head, is credited with leading the rebel offensive that overthrew Assad in late 2024. The US Treasury Department also removed Hay’at Tahrir al-Sham (HTS), the group that led the rebel offensive, from its list of specially designated global terrorists. HTS, formerly known as al-Nusra Front, was al-Qaeda’s affiliate in Syria until Sharaa severed ties with the jihadist network in 2016.

Syrian Foreign Minister Asaad Hassan al-Shaibani welcomed the news as a "historic step," according to state-run news agency SANA. He said the move would help restore Syria’s links to the global financial system and encourage investment following nearly 14 years of war. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the move would "help foster additional investment in Syria to promote political and economic stability."

Where the accounts differ

BBC states that HTS was removed from the US list of global terrorist organisations and that Syria had been on the list since 1979. Washington Examiner and UPI also mention the removal of HTS from the list of specially designated global terrorists. FOX News and The Daily Wire include details about the 45-day congressional notification period and the June 2025 executive order. New York Post and The Daily Wire mention the removal of the Nusrah Front as a specially designated global terrorist organization. UPI notes that Syria was among the first countries designated by the US as a state sponsor of terrorism in 1979 over its hostility to Israel, fighting with Lebanon, partnership with Iran, alignment with Russia, support for terrorist groups and development of weapons of mass destruction, according to a Congressional Research Service report.

Sources BBC · Washington Examiner · FOX News · New York Post · The Daily Wire · UPI

Target apologizes for ‘offensive’ Halloween costume after backlash

Target, the US retail corporation, pulled a controversial Halloween costume from its shelves after criticism on social media that it resembled a racist caricature. The costume, sold as the “Kids’ Glows Under Blacklight Circus Clown Halloween Costume,” depicted a Black child in a bright orange and black top with solid black gloves and a mask with a wide, toothy smile. Both the child’s pose and the costume’s gloves and mask were likened to caricatures of Black people used in minstrel shows. Target issued an apology, stating the costume was offensive and should never have been part of its assortment. The company said it is looking into how the costume ended up in its stores and what changes are needed to prevent such issues in the future.

The costume controversy is part of a series of challenges for Target, including backlash over its decision to scale back diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) initiatives after President Donald Trump returned to the White House. The company has faced financial and reputational hits from these decisions, including a significant drop in stock value following a boycott led by activists. Target’s recent stock has shown signs of recovery, with shares rising to a 52-week high in late August.

Trump cuts U.S.-South Korea military drills, citing tensions with North Korea

President Donald Trump ordered the Pentagon to reduce the annual U.S.-South Korea Ulchi Freedom Shield (UFS) military exercise from 11 days to five, framing the move as a goodwill gesture to North Korean leader Kim Jong Un. Trump claimed the drills sent a “totally inappropriate and hostile” signal to Pyongyang and linked the decision to Seoul’s refusal to join the U.S. war effort in Iran. The reduction was announced in late August 2026, with Trump stating he had a “very good relationship” with Kim Jong Un.

North Korea’s response was cautious, with Kim Yo Jong, Kim Jong Un’s sister, stating she was “unaware” of any communication between the two leaders, contradicting Trump’s claim of a “very positive” exchange. However, she reaffirmed her brother’s “personally good memories and feelings” towards Trump, indicating openness to engagement on terms that include North Korea’s nuclear status. Meanwhile, South Korea’s government expressed concern over the reduced drills, with some analysts suggesting the move could accelerate calls for South Korea to develop its own nuclear capabilities.

Where the accounts differ

Al Jazeera reports that China’s Foreign Minister Wang Yi urged South Korea to pursue “genuine strategic autonomy” and avoid “bloc confrontation,” while omitting any reference to denuclearisation in his remarks. The New York Post notes that South Korea is stockpiling advanced interceptors and munitions, including the Cheongung-II, and that Seoul has been hesitant to share some of these with the U.S. The Christian Science Monitor highlights growing public support in South Korea for nuclearization, citing a recent survey showing 80% of South Koreans believe the country should invest in becoming a nuclear state. The Dispatch provides minimal detail on the event, focusing instead on the broader implications of U.S. influence over North Korea.

Illegal immigrant accused of fentanyl dealing linked to Virginia high school overdoses

An illegal immigrant from El Salvador, Isaac Melendez-Carranza, 31, was arrested on August 20 for allegedly distributing fentanyl within 1,000 feet of a school. He is being held without bond at the Loudoun County Adult Detention Center. Federal authorities have lodged a detainer with the Loudoun County Jail, requesting his deportation. Melendez-Carranza was previously released on bond in 2016 after being arrested by U.S. Border Patrol near Hebbronville, Texas, and had a final deportation order issued by an immigration judge on July 18, 2024. The Loudoun County Sheriff’s Office linked him to overdoses involving students at Park View High School in May, though the school system stated it was unclear if the incidents were connected.

Melendez-Carranza was encountered in Sterling, Virginia, on August 16, 2024, but no action was taken, according to DHS. During a search of his home, deputies found 100 pills and about $1,000 in cash. Multiple students were hospitalized following suspected overdoses at Park View High School in May, with two suffering nonfatal overdoses. The sheriff’s office warned that one pill can be deadly and emphasized the dangers of fentanyl.
