Trump hosts Xi Jinping at White House with red carpet welcome and state dinner

President Donald Trump welcomed Chinese President Xi Jinping to the White House on Thursday with a red carpet ceremony and a state dinner, marking Xi's first U.S. state visit in over a decade. Trump described the meeting as a "truly great friendship" and highlighted progress on trade and security issues. The visit included a military flyover, formal meetings, and a ceremonial welcome at Joint Base Andrews. During the state dinner, Trump presented Xi with a bald eagle sculpture and praised their personal relationship. Xi, in turn, called for continued cooperation between the two nations, emphasizing the importance of their relationship for global stability.

The White House event followed a controversial decision by Trump to ban CNN, MS NOW, and Politico from covering White House events, which a federal judge later temporarily restored. The visit also raised questions about press freedom and access, with some outlets noting the absence of U.S. journalists during the formal meetings.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian and Al Jazeera both mention the red carpet welcome at Joint Base Andrews, but only The Guardian notes that Trump referred to Xi's wife as "Madam Xi" instead of "Madame Peng," which is the correct title. The Guardian also highlights the absence of U.S. journalists during the formal meetings, a detail not included in Al Jazeera or ABC News. NPR and CBS News both report on the judge's decision to restore press access, while The Guardian and Al Jazeera do not mention this legal development. Axios focuses on AI competition between the U.S. and China, a topic not covered in the other accounts.

Sources The Guardian · NPR · Al Jazeera · ABC News · CBS News · Axios

Senate Rejects Iran War Powers Resolution Despite GOP Support

The U.S. Senate voted down a resolution calling for President Donald Trump to end the war with Iran, with four Republican senators joining all Democrats except Pennsylvania's John Fetterman. The measure, which failed 49-50, was previously passed by the House of Representatives in July. The resolution aimed to compel Trump to end the conflict under the War Powers Act of 1973, which allows Congress to direct the president to halt military actions without congressional authorization. The war, launched in February as Operation Epic Fury, has faced growing public discontent, with polls showing most Americans believe the U.S. is not winning. Some Republicans, including Thom Tillis, Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski, and Rand Paul, have expressed concerns about the war's economic impact and lack of clear objectives.

Republican candidates in key races have also distanced themselves from Trump's Iran policy, with figures like Mike Rogers and Ashley Hinson criticizing the war's cost to families and calling for its end. These statements mark a shift from previous support for Trump's military actions, though they did not directly mention him. Meanwhile, the White House has not yet commented on the resolution, and the conflict continues to strain the economy and public opinion.

Where the accounts differ

The Daily Wire reports that Bill Burr has criticized CEOs for receiving security and supported Luigi Mangione's actions, while the other accounts do not mention Burr or Mangione. The Daily Wire also claims a poll by the Center for Strategic Politics and Liberal Currents found 32% of Democrats would be happy if Trump were assassinated, a claim not made by other outlets. The New York Post and UPI mention Thom Tillis voting for the resolution for the first time, while the Daily Wire does not reference this. The Slate and NPR accounts focus on Republican candidates distancing from Trump's Iran policy, while the Newsweek and UPI accounts detail the Senate's rejection of the war powers resolution and the involvement of specific senators.

Sources The Daily Wire · Slate · NPR · Newsweek · UPI · New York Post

Iran's president tells Trump it will never 'bend the knee'

Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian addressed the UN General Assembly on Wednesday, accusing the U.S. and Israel of attacking Iran and portraying his country as the victim of terrorism. He presented photos of civilian casualties, including over 175 people, two-thirds children, killed in missile strikes on a primary school in Minab and a sports complex in Lamerd. The U.S. has denied striking Lamerd and said it is still investigating what happened in Minab. Pezeshkian also mentioned an attack on a cargo ship in the Strait of Hormuz that killed an Indian sailor, which was condemned by India's foreign ministry. He reiterated Iran's stance that it will not give up its right to a civilian nuclear program and will not seek nuclear weapons. Trump, in a separate address, threatened to "annihilate" Iran unless it agreed to a deal to end the war and reopen the Strait of Hormuz. Pezeshkian said any deal must include the end of the U.S. blockade of Iranian ports and ships.

Where the accounts differ

BBC and Axios both mention the U.S. denying striking Lamerd and still investigating Minab, while Axios adds that a UN report found "reasonable grounds to believe" the U.S. committed a war crime in the strike. NPR and Straight Arrow News report that Trump met with Iranian officials for three hours on the sidelines of the UN, but Axios and BBC state that the talks were indirect and mediated. FOX News attributes the claim about the three-hour meeting to Trump, while the other accounts do not specify who made the claim. Christian Science Monitor and BBC mention that Iran's foreign ministry spokesman Esmail Baqai said the talks were "not anything new" and that Araghchi maintained Iran's conditions, while Straight Arrow News and FOX News do not include this detail.

Sources BBC · Straight Arrow News · Christian Science Monitor · FOX News · NPR · Axios

Netanyahu takes UN stage with one eye on Israel’s election

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu addressed the United Nations General Assembly on Thursday, a speech that coincided with growing domestic political pressure as his Likud party faces elections. The address, which included references to Israel’s military actions against Hezbollah and Iran, was met with significant public and diplomatic backlash. Dozens of UN delegates walked out before the speech began, and protests outside the UN headquarters in New York drew hundreds of participants, including Jewish, Palestinian, and Iranian activists. Netanyahu also directly criticized New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani, accusing him of supporting Hamas and failing to condemn Israel’s actions. The speech was also marked by Netanyahu’s display of a Hezbollah pager, which he used to highlight Israel’s military campaign against the group.

Netanyahu’s address was framed by some as a defense of Israeli settlements in the West Bank, with claims that only a small number of settlers had committed violence against Palestinians. However, international reports and Palestinian activists disputed these claims, citing numerous documented incidents of settler violence. The speech also came amid international condemnation of Israel’s actions in Gaza, with multiple countries and organizations calling for sanctions against Israeli settlements. The UN has repeatedly criticized Israel’s conduct in the occupied territories, including accusations of genocide and war crimes, which Netanyahu has dismissed as biased.

Where the accounts differ

Al Jazeera and The Nation describe Netanyahu’s speech as part of a broader context of Israel’s strained relationship with the UN, including accusations of genocide and war crimes. The Daily Wire and Mother Jones provide more specific details about the content of the speech, including Netanyahu’s display of the Hezbollah pager and his direct criticism of Mayor Zohran Mamdani. The Guardian and NPR focus more on the public protests and the broader geopolitical context, including the Russia-Ukraine war and the U.S.-Iran tensions, which are not covered in the other accounts. The Nation also highlights the decline in American public support for Israel, a point not mentioned in the other accounts.

Sources Al Jazeera · The Daily Wire · Mother Jones · The Guardian · NPR · The Nation

Man wounded during 2024 Trump rally shooting in Butler has died, House Republicans confirm

Jim Copenhaver, a man wounded during the 2024 Trump rally shooting in Butler, Pennsylvania, has died, according to House Republicans. Copenhaver was one of two people gravely injured in the shooting, where volunteer firefighter Corey Comperatore was killed. President Donald Trump was also injured in the shooting after a bullet grazed his ear. David Dutch was the other person seriously injured. Copenhaver, who was 76 at the time of his death, was shot in the triceps and had a bullet lodged in his body after it took chunks of his colon, leaving him with nerve damage. He was discharged from the hospital one week after the shooting.

Copenhaver's death was confirmed by his family and reported by multiple outlets. His lawyer, Joseph Feldman, stated that Copenhaver died surrounded by loved ones. The family's statement described him as a devoted husband, father, and grandfather who instilled values of hard work and service in his children. Trump also expressed condolences, calling Copenhaver "a true American Patriot" and praising his bravery and perseverance after being wounded.

Where the accounts differ

Just The News and CBS News both state that Copenhaver was shot in the triceps and that a bullet took chunks of his colon, leaving him with nerve damage. UPI and The Daily Wire mention that Copenhaver was shot in the triceps and abdomen, with bullet fragments remaining in his body. FOX News adds that Copenhaver had bullet fragments still lodged in his body. NPR states that Copenhaver was struck in the arm and abdomen, with lingering injuries and walking with a cane. The Daily Wire also notes that Copenhaver's family described his injuries as "life-altering" and that he lived with surgeries and pain for two years.

Sources Just The News · NPR · CBS News · UPI · FOX News · The Daily Wire

Dolly Parton’s Business Empire Feuds With Her Nephew Over Explosive Texts

Dolly Parton’s estate has filed a temporary restraining order against her nephew Bryan Seaver, accusing him of sending threatening messages and attempting to extort money from the Parton legacy. The order seeks to keep Seaver at least 1,000 feet away from employees, attorneys, and business partners of the estate. Seaver, who was Parton’s head of security and announced her death, has denied the allegations, claiming the messages were taken out of context and were private conversations with his longtime friend and manager Danny Nozell. The court documents include text messages and emails allegedly sent by Seaver, which include threats to destroy the brand and references to his military experience. The estate claims Seaver’s actions have led to the resignation of employees and lawyers, and the hiring of private security.

Where the accounts differ

The Daily Wire and CBS News both report that Seaver allegedly sent a message on September 1 stating, “I’m going to set up a podcast dedicated to ruining Dolly’s brand partnerships and telling the violations these people have done on us. It’s going to be great. Or f*cking pay me.” The Daily Caller and BBC also include this message, but the BBC adds that Seaver allegedly described himself as a “killer” and claimed to have sold $27m in arms to the Haitian police. The Daily Wire and CBS News do not mention the arms sale, while the Daily Caller and BBC attribute the claim to Seaver. The Daily Wire and CBS News state that Seaver referred to himself as an “international arms dealer” after Parton’s death, while the Daily Caller and BBC attribute this to Seaver as well. The Daily Wire and CBS News mention that Seaver’s company, Squadron Augmented Protection Services, is named in the complaint, while the Daily Caller and BBC do not specify the company’s name. The BBC and CBS News note that Seaver’s company did not respond to comment requests, while the Daily Wire and Daily Caller do not mention this.

Sources The Daily Wire · The Daily Caller · CBS News · BBC

Trump’s FDA Pick Refuses to Say IUDs Don’t Cause Abortions

Dr. Heidi Overton, nominated by President Donald Trump to lead the FDA, faced questions during her Senate confirmation hearing about abortion and birth control. Senator Patty Murray of Washington asked Overton if she agrees that an IUD is an abortifacient, referencing Trump’s claims that some forms of birth control can cause abortions. Overton refused to answer directly, instead stating she would follow FDA standards if confirmed. Murray also pressed Overton on her 2023 claim that the abortion drug mifepristone is “dangerous to women,” which Overton said she no longer holds. Overton stated that mifepristone was approved by the FDA as safe and effective, but she did not confirm whether she personally believes it is safe.

Overton, currently serving as a White House domestic policy advisor, has a history of anti-abortion statements. Her past comments on mifepristone, including calling it “dangerous to women,” have drawn criticism from Democrats, who argue they conflict with scientific evidence. Overton cited an ongoing FDA review of mifepristone’s safety, which she said is “very important.” The FDA has faced legal challenges over its regulation of mifepristone, with five active lawsuits involving both abortion-hostile states and abortion providers.

Where the accounts differ

Mother Jones reports that Overton refused to answer whether an IUD is an abortifacient and that she suggested the FDA could change its stance. UPI states that Overton sidestepped questions about IUDs and mifepristone, but does not mention her refusal to answer directly. ABC News notes that Murray asked Overton if she believes an IUD is an abortifacient, and Overton did not answer directly. FOX News does not mention the IUD or mifepristone questions at all, focusing instead on a separate Senate race in Maine.

Sources Mother Jones · UPI · FOX News · ABC News
