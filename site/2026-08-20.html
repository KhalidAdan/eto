Trump Vows Unprecedented Economic Measures Against Iran Amid Stalled Talks

President Donald Trump announced on Wednesday that the United States is launching a new economic campaign against Iran, calling it "the MOST CRUSHING ECONOMIC OPERATION EVER TAKEN AGAINST ANY COUNTRY." The move comes after a 60-day ceasefire expired without a diplomatic resolution to the war that began in late February. Trump warned that any country aiding Iran financially would face "TREMENDOUS Economic Consequences," though he did not specify the measures. The administration has been applying economic pressure on Iran since April, aiming to cut off its revenue streams and isolate the country. The U.S. has also imposed a naval blockade on the Strait of Hormuz, a critical oil route, which has significantly impacted Iran's economy.

The economic measures are part of a broader strategy to weaken Iran's ability to sustain its military and economic operations. Reports indicate that Iran's oil production has dropped by nearly 23 percent since the start of the conflict, reducing its ability to generate foreign currency. The U.S. and its allies are seeking to maintain control over the Strait of Hormuz, which is vital for global oil trade. Trump claimed the U.S. has "complete control" of the strait, despite Iran's continued efforts to restrict maritime traffic through the region.

Where the accounts differ

Newsweek reports that Trump's economic measures are part of "Operation Economic Fury," which includes targeting oil smuggling, swap lines, and front companies. The Daily Wire adds that Trump's actions are aimed at dismantling Iran's "shadow economy," including illicit financial networks in China, Dubai, and Malaysia. BBC notes that the UAE has severed financial ties with Iran following a missile threat, while UPI mentions that China has been a major buyer of Iranian oil and has opposed U.S. sanctions. Washington Examiner highlights the financial impact of the naval blockade, estimating it could cost Iran $18 billion annually if continued.

Sources Newsweek · BBC · UPI · Washington Examiner · The Daily Wire · Axios

South Korea Wants Its Military Back

On August 27, 2025, ROK Army 7th Corps Engineer Brigade and U.S. Army 11th Engineer Battalion of the 2nd Infantry Division soldiers participated in a joint bridging drill in Yeoju, South Korea. South Korean President Lee Jae Myung reiterated calls for the country to regain full control of its military operations from the U.S. following Donald Trump’s order to cut back on annual joint training drills in preparation for North Korean threats. Trump cited his "very good relationship with Kim Jong Un" and South Korea’s refusal to support the U.S. war effort in Iran as reasons for reducing the drills. Lee responded by urging Seoul to plan a transfer of control over the Combined Forces Command, which oversees both South Korean and U.S. forces during wartime. South Korea has sought to regain wartime operational control since the 1950s Korean War, with regaining control seen as a key goal of Lee’s administration. Lee also signaled rearmament, including accelerating the development of nuclear-powered submarines.

Where the accounts differ

Mother Jones and ABC News both mention Trump’s claim that South Korea refused to support the U.S. war effort in Iran, while NPR and UPI attribute this claim to Trump. Al Jazeera and BBC attribute the claim to Trump as well, but BBC adds that Trump linked the decision to South Korea’s refusal to "give us a little hand" with Iran. Mother Jones and ABC News state that Trump cited his "very good relationship with Kim Jong Un" as a reason for reducing the drills, while NPR and UPI attribute this claim to Trump. Al Jazeera and BBC also attribute this claim to Trump, but BBC adds that Trump said Pyongyang had been "unthreatening and respectful" during his presidency. UPI and ABC News mention that Trump wrote on Truth Social that the drills "send a signal that is totally inappropriate and hostile" to North Korea, while Mother Jones and NPR attribute this statement to Trump. Al Jazeera and BBC also attribute this statement to Trump, but BBC adds that Trump said the drills were "costly" and "send a signal that is totally inappropriate and hostile" to North Korea.

Sources Mother Jones · NPR · Al Jazeera · ABC News · BBC · UPI

Prince Harry and Meghan leaving US for Britain after six-year royal feud

Prince Harry and Meghan Markle are relocating from the United States to the United Kingdom later this month, according to multiple outlets. The couple, along with their two children, Prince Archie and Princess Lilibet, are expected to move to a private, non-royal residence outside London. The children are enrolled to start school in September. The move comes after a period of strained relations with the royal family, which began when the couple stepped down from their royal duties in 2020 and moved to California. The couple has not resumed their roles as working royals, and their status as private individuals remains unchanged.

The decision to return to the UK follows a meeting between Harry and King Charles III at Highgrove House in July, which marked the first time the king had seen his grandchildren in person in over four years. King Charles was reportedly informed of the couple’s plans on Sunday, and he has expressed willingness to see them in a private capacity. The move also comes after Harry expressed a desire for reconciliation with his family, particularly following his father’s diagnosis with an unspecified form of cancer.

Where the accounts differ

The Washington Examiner and The Guardian both state that King Charles was informed of the couple’s decision on Sunday, while The Daily Wire and BBC report that the king was made aware of the plans on Sunday as well. The Guardian and The Daily Wire mention that the couple’s return was not discussed during their July visit to Highgrove, whereas The Washington Examiner and BBC suggest that the visit was a key development in their relationship. The Daily Wire and The Guardian note that Harry has remained estranged from his brother, Prince William, while The Washington Examiner and BBC do not explicitly state this. The Daily Wire includes a quote from GB News commentator Patrick Christys, which is absent from other accounts.

Sources Washington Examiner · The Daily Wire · The Guardian · NPR · Just The News · BBC

Trump to tap Heidi Overton as FDA commissioner

President Trump has nominated White House aide Heidi Overton to lead the Food and Drug Administration, according to multiple outlets. Overton, currently deputy director of the White House Domestic Policy Council, was one of several candidates considered for the role. The nomination comes after former FDA commissioner Marty Makary resigned in May following clashes with the Trump administration over the safety of flavored vapes. Overton is expected to face Senate confirmation, with some senators expressing concerns about her qualifications and policies.

Overton has been involved in recent executive actions, including the signing of an order to reduce the number of childhood vaccines recommended for children and to split the MMR vaccine into separate shots. She has also been vocal about her opposition to abortion, describing it as "corrosive" to women and society. Her nomination has drawn criticism from Democrats and some Republicans, who argue that her policies could undermine public health and scientific integrity.

Where the accounts differ

Axios reports that Overton may not offer the reassurance the pharmaceutical industry was looking for and that industry analysts have criticized her nomination. The Guardian and FOX News attribute similar concerns to Overton's stance on abortion and vaccine policies. UPI and Washington Examiner note that Overton's nomination has caused tension within the MAHA movement, with some activists viewing her as too aligned with the traditional public health establishment. The Guardian and FOX News also highlight Overton's role in Trump's executive order on vaccines, which has been criticized by the American Academy of Pediatrics as "dangerous" and "disheartening."

Sources Axios · BBC · UPI · Washington Examiner · FOX News · The Guardian

Five Americans among seven killed in Kenya helicopter crash

A helicopter crash in northern Kenya killed seven people, including five Americans, according to multiple reports. The crash occurred on Wednesday morning while the aircraft was flying from the Loisaba Conservancy to the Ewaso Nyiro area in Samburu County. The Kenya Civil Aviation Authority confirmed the crash and stated an investigation was underway. The helicopter, owned by Lady Lori Helicopters, was operating a charter flight for the travel company &Beyond. The aircraft was a Eurocopter EC130 B4. Rescue and recovery efforts faced challenges due to difficult terrain and an active fire at the crash site.

The U.S. State Department confirmed that five of the deceased were American citizens, including a Florida-based Telemundo TV station manager and a Miami restaurateur. The State Department said it was in contact with local authorities and providing consular assistance. The identities of the victims were not immediately released, though some outlets reported that José Alberto Suárez, a Telemundo executive, and Ecuador's intelligence chief Michele Sensi-Contugi, along with his wife, were among those killed.

Where the accounts differ

CBS News and The Guardian both reported that five Americans were killed, including José Suárez and Roger Duarte. The Guardian also named Michele Sensi-Contugi and his wife, Stephany Holliham Yeaza, as victims. UPI and Just The News reported five Americans were killed but did not specify which individuals. FOX News and BBC also mentioned five Americans, but FOX News referred to José Suárez as a Telemundo executive, while CBS News and The Guardian provided more details about his role. The Guardian and BBC noted that Sensi-Contugi was visiting Kenya as a tourist, a detail not included in CBS News or UPI. The Guardian and BBC also mentioned the death of Sensi-Contugi's wife, while CBS News and UPI did not. The Guardian and BBC provided more context about the helicopter's route and the crash location, while UPI and Just The News omitted some details about the victims.

Sources CBS News · The Guardian · UPI · Just The News · BBC · FOX News

Disney, ABC file suit against Trump's FCC for retaliation against it

The Walt Disney Company and ABC have filed a lawsuit against the Federal Communications Commission (FCC), alleging the Trump administration is engaging in a retaliatory campaign against the network for its programming. The suit claims the FCC ordered an early license renewal for Disney’s eight owned-and-operated stations, which are not due for renewal until 2028. The lawsuit argues this action is an "existential threat" and part of a broader effort to punish ABC for its content. The suit was filed in a Washington, D.C., federal court, requesting an immediate block on FCC actions related to the early renewal process and a speedy hearing. The complaint also references President Trump’s social media posts criticizing ABC and its late-night programming, including a 2025 post where he suggested revoking broadcast licenses for networks that criticize him.

Where the accounts differ

UPI and The Daily Caller both mention Trump’s 2025 social media post about revoking licenses for networks that criticize him, while Straight Arrow News and Slate refer to a similar post from December 2025. The Daily Caller and The Daily Wire include details about FCC Chairman Brendan Carr’s investigation into Disney’s diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) practices, which UPI and NPR do not mention. The Daily Wire and Slate provide more context about the suspension of Jimmy Kimmel’s show in September 2025, which is omitted by UPI and The Daily Caller. Straight Arrow News and The Daily Wire both note that Carr launched an investigation into ABC’s DEI programs, while UPI and NPR do not specify the nature of the investigation.

Sources UPI · Straight Arrow News · The Daily Wire · Slate · NPR · The Daily Caller

Russian missile barrage kills at least 13 people in Ukraine’s Kyiv

Russian forces launched ballistic missile attacks on Kyiv, killing at least 13 people and wounding 33, according to Ukrainian officials. Explosions were reported across several districts, including Sviatoshynskyi, Solomianskyi, and Shevchenkivskyi, with a nine-storey residential building collapsing and catching fire in Solomianskyi. A children’s hospital in the district was also damaged. Kyiv Mayor Vitali Klitschko reported that parts of the districts were without electricity and that people were trapped in another residential building. Ukraine’s Emergency Services confirmed at least 33 people were wounded in the attack. In the Brovary district of the Kyiv region, one person was killed and another wounded. Russia’s Ministry of Defence claimed it shot down 726 Ukrainian UAVs between Wednesday evening and Thursday morning.

The attacks prompted security responses in neighboring NATO countries, with Poland mobilizing aircraft and Romania scrambling jets after detecting aerial targets near the Ukrainian border. Ukraine’s new defence minister, Yevhenii Khmara, was confirmed by parliament, with Zelenskyy calling the appointment a sign of increased strength. Zelenskyy also appealed for more anti-ballistic missile support, citing a shortage of Patriot interceptors and the need for Ukraine’s FREYJA program to be operational by 2027.

Where the accounts differ

Al Jazeera and The Guardian both report that at least 13 people were killed, while ABC News and BBC report at least 12 and 13 respectively. Al Jazeera and The Guardian mention the collapse of a nine-storey residential building and damage to a children’s hospital in Solomianskyi, while ABC News and BBC do not specify which buildings were hit. ABC News and BBC report that Zelenskyy called the attack “massive” and described it as one of the most brutal, while Al Jazeera and The Guardian do not include this characterization. The Guardian and BBC mention the use of hypersonic missiles and specific types of Russian weaponry, which are not detailed in Al Jazeera or ABC News. ABC News and BBC report that Ukraine’s air force did not specify how many ballistic missiles were intercepted, while Al Jazeera and The Guardian do not mention this detail.

Sources Al Jazeera · ABC News · The Guardian · BBC
