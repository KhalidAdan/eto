Two Dan Sullivans on Alaska Senate ballot amid legal and political controversy

Alaska's Senate race features two candidates named Dan Sullivan, with incumbent Republican Sen. Dan S. Sullivan facing Democrat Mary Peltola and Republican challenger Daniel J. Sullivan. The race, which includes a fourth candidate, Gerald Heikes, is under scrutiny due to the similarity in names. Alaska's unique ranked-choice voting system requires voters to rank candidates, with the winner determined through multiple rounds of elimination. The state's election system, which changed in 2020, has led to a four-candidate race. The controversy over the duplicate names has been ongoing, with election officials initially ruling against Daniel J. Sullivan's candidacy due to concerns about voter confusion, but the state Supreme Court later allowed him to remain on the ballot.

The New York Times reported that Democratic operatives linked to Peltola's campaign allegedly recruited Daniel J. Sullivan to split the Republican vote. The report cited texts, interviews, and documents suggesting Peltola's team was involved in the effort. The Department of Justice has filed a lawsuit seeking to block certification of the election if the incumbent loses by a margin potentially attributable to voter confusion. The state's election officials and the DOJ are involved in legal battles over the validity of the second Sullivan's candidacy.

Where the accounts differ

CBS News and Axios both report that the Alaska Supreme Court upheld the decision to allow Daniel J. Sullivan on the ballot, but CBS News adds that the court did not determine whether his candidacy was intended to mislead voters. Axios states that the court did not determine this, while CBS News notes that the court's decision was based on the state's lack of authority to disqualify candidates based on their motives. The Free Press and Straight Arrow News both mention that the New York Times reported Peltola's campaign was aware of the effort to recruit Sullivan, but The Free Press attributes this to the Times, while Straight Arrow News states that Peltola was not involved but was "aware" of it. The Washington Examiner and Axios both mention the DOJ's lawsuit, but the Washington Examiner adds that the DOJ is seeking "expedited discovery" in the case, while Axios states that the DOJ is pressing the campaign to reveal more details about the timing of their request.

Sources CBS News · Axios · The Hill · Straight Arrow News · Washington Examiner · The Free Press

Judge rejects DOJ's push to force California to set aside ballots

A federal judge on Wednesday rejected the Trump administration's bid to require California election officials to separate out mail ballots received from roughly 20,000 people it claims are not U.S. citizens. The decision from U.S. District Judge David Carter is the latest setback for the Trump administration in its efforts to exert more federal control over election administration just ahead of the Nov. 3 midterm elections. Carter said that the Justice Department's request for California to segregate and exclude more than 20,000 ballots violates "longstanding constitutional principles" giving states the authority to administer their own elections. The judge warned that requiring California election officials to set aside mail ballots received from those alleged noncitizens would likely lead to disenfranchisement.

The Trump administration sued California earlier this month as it alleged the state was sending 20,000 likely noncitizens ballots for the midterm contests. California has a universal mail-in voting program where all active voters are sent a ballot. Attorneys for California and Los Angeles County condemned the administration for not sharing who supplied its list. They argue they’re not required to prove voters’ citizenship merely because a sitting administration alleges issues, saying it’s not practical within the current timeline.

Where the accounts differ

CBS News and The Hill both report that the Justice Department formed its list by checking a commercially-bought dataset with a government list of people who had been assigned numbers in the federal immigration system. Mother Jones and The Guardian add that the list was created by checking a commercially-bought dataset containing partial California voter rolls with a government list of people who had been assigned numbers in the federal immigration system. The Guardian also notes that the Department of Homeland Security identified up to 190,000 non-citizens on California voter rolls in July, but did not provide more details to the state until after ballots had already gone out. Al Jazeera reports that the Trump administration issued more than 100 subpoenas across 13 states as part of its search for alleged noncitizen voters. NPR states that the DOJ has charged dozens of people for registering or voting without being a U.S. citizen in the last two months.

Sources CBS News · The Hill · Mother Jones · The Guardian · NPR · Al Jazeera

Senate race in South Carolina tied in final stretch – poll

Graham, appointed to the seat after her brother, Lindsey Graham, died in July, entered the race with President Trump's endorsement. Trump returned to South Carolina to rally for Graham, attacking Andrews with anti-trans slurs and calling her a "radical left lunatic." The Senate is still a toss-up with six states to decide the majority. On a good night for the Democrats, their ceiling rises to 54 seats. The outcome of the South Carolina race is seen as critical to the overall Senate balance, with both parties pouring significant resources into the state.

Brewers End Historic Drought, Defeat Rays For First World Series Title

The Milwaukee Brewers secured their first World Series title by defeating the Tampa Bay Rays 4-1 in Game 5 on October 28. The victory ended a 56-year championship drought for the Brewers, who had previously reached the World Series only once in 1982. The Rays, seeking their first title, fell short after a thrilling 12-inning Game 4 win. The Brewers' dominant postseason run included 11 wins and two losses, with standout performances from rookie Cooper Pratt and Jacob Misiorowski, who pitched five scoreless innings before leaving due to a calf injury. Trevor Megill closed the game with a save, and the Brewers' bullpen was nearly unhittable, posting a 1.38 ERA and 0.86 WHIP in 13 playoff games.

The Brewers' victory was marked by strong offensive output, with Pratt and Jackson Chourio each batting over .400 in the series. Pratt contributed seven RBIs, while the Rays managed only 11 RBIs as a team. The Rays' ace, Drew Rasmussen, allowed just one run in four innings but struggled against the Brewers' bullpen, which extended the lead to 4-0 after six innings. The Rays' players watched in stunned silence from the dugout as the Brewers celebrated in the middle of Tropicana Field, awaiting their own parade for the first time in their 28-year history.

Where the accounts differ

Newsweek reports that the Rays fell short on both sides of the ball, while The Guardian and BBC attribute the Rays' failure to their inability to capitalize on opportunities, such as a fourth-inning error by Sal Frelick. NPR and ABC News mention the Rays' 12-inning Game 4 win, while CBS News refers to it as a "dramatic come-from-behind 4-3 victory in the 12th inning of Game Four." The Guardian and BBC provide more detail on William Contreras' MVP performance, including his .381/.435/.429 line and his role in containing Tampa Bay's running game, which is not explicitly mentioned in Newsweek or CBS News. The Guardian and BBC also note Contreras' World Baseball Classic win with Venezuela, which is omitted in other accounts.

Three killed in midair helicopter collision near Oregon airport, authorities confirm

Two helicopters collided midair near McMinnville Municipal Airport in Oregon on Thursday afternoon, resulting in the deaths of all three occupants, according to multiple outlets. The collision occurred around 2:15 p.m. local time, involving a Robinson R22 and an Airbus H125. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) confirmed the incident, and the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) is leading the investigation. The crash site was near Oregon Route 233, approximately one mile west of Dayton, with nearby roads closed for emergency response. The airport remained open, though operations were not disrupted.

The victims included 22-year-old Texas flight instructor Maddie Aasbo, a British student pilot, and 38-year-old Oregon pilot Tyler Delane Sturdevant. The British student pilot's identity has not been publicly released, pending notification of next of kin. One of the helicopters was operated by Jerry Trimble Helicopters, an aviation school based at the airport, which expressed condolences and stated it was cooperating with authorities.

US judge blocks Trump’s Medicaid rule forcing ill to prove impairment

A federal judge ruled against enforcing a new rule by the Trump administration that would require chronically ill people on Medicaid to prove themselves “medically frail” to qualify for work exemptions. US District Judge Richard Stearns blocked the rule, siding with 25 states and the District of Columbia, which had sued the administration in June over the change. The states argued the rule unlawfully limits exemptions for people with serious medical conditions. The law defines “medically frail” to include people who are blind or disabled, have substance use disorders or disabling mental disorders, have certain disabilities that significantly impair daily activities, or have serious or complex medical conditions. The administration’s rule required people in those categories to show their conditions also “significantly impair” their ability to comply with work requirements. The plaintiffs argued the stricter rule would prevent eligible Americans from accessing the care they need and overstep the law’s text.

Starting January 1, 2027, Medicaid expansion enrollees aged 19 to 63 must show they work or do community service at least 80 hours a month, or are in school at least half the time. Exceptions include those considered medically frail or in addiction treatment programs. While the ruling makes it easier for some Medicaid recipients with qualifying medical conditions to obtain an exemption from the new work requirements, it does not strike down the work requirements as unlawful. Stearns, who serves in the District of Massachusetts and was appointed by President Bill Clinton, upheld two other federal restrictions on how states can verify medical frailty that the states and the District of Columbia had contested.

Where the accounts differ

Al Jazeera states the rule requires people in certain categories to show their conditions “significantly impair” their ability to comply with work requirements, while Washington Examiner does not mention this specific requirement. Al Jazeera also notes that the ruling does not strike down the work requirements as unlawful, whereas Washington Examiner does not explicitly state this. Al Jazeera mentions that the law defines “medically frail” to include people with certain disabilities that “significantly impair” daily activities, while Washington Examiner does not include this detail.

Sources Al Jazeera · Washington Examiner
