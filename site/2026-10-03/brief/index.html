Jane Doe’s Allegations of Gang Rape at Cornell Fraternity House Spark New Investigation

A former Cornell University student, identified as Jane Doe in a lawsuit, alleges she was drugged and gang-raped by multiple men at a Chi Phi fraternity house in October 2024. According to CBS News, Jane Doe consumed alcohol until she was intoxicated at a sorority house and later went to the fraternity house, where she claims she was drugged and sexually assaulted. She alleges the men pushed her to take ketamine until she was incapacitated and then forced sexual acts upon her. Jane Doe later filed a civil lawsuit against seven fraternity members, the university, and others she claims failed to protect her. Prosecutors initially did not charge anyone but have since reopened the criminal investigation. The White House has also taken an interest in the case, with New York Governor Kathy Hochul appointing state Attorney General Letitia James as a special prosecutor.

The case has prompted political reactions, including the cancellation of an event by House Speaker Emerita Nancy Pelosi, who cited “serious concerns about Cornell’s response” to the allegations. The lawsuit also highlights disputes over the initial investigation, with Jane Doe’s attorneys arguing that the original account provided to police was inadequate.

Where the accounts differ

The Daily Wire attributes the sexual misconduct allegations to a broader generational trend and frames the issue as a societal crisis involving Gen Z, while CBS News and The Hill focus on the specific incident at Cornell and the legal and institutional responses. The Daily Wire does not mention the specific allegations of drugging or gang rape, instead focusing on the cultural and generational context. The Washington Examiner provides a timeline of Jane Doe’s allegations but does not detail the specific events of the night in question. The Dispatch and The Guardian do not cover the Cornell incident at all.

Republican Senate Leadership Fund Pauses Ad Spending in North Carolina, Shifts Focus to Kansas

The Senate Leadership Fund (SLF), a major Republican super PAC, paused its ad spending in North Carolina and redirected resources to Kansas, according to multiple reports. The move comes as the party faces challenges in key Senate races, including North Carolina, where Republican nominee Michael Whatley trails former Democratic Gov. Roy Cooper, and Georgia, where Rep. Mike Collins faces Sen. Jon Ossoff. The SLF had previously spent over $30 million in North Carolina and is now focusing on supporting Sen. Roger Marshall in Kansas, who faces Democratic nominee Adam Hamilton. The shift reflects the PAC’s strategy to allocate resources to races where Republicans are more likely to hold their seats.

The decision to pause ad spending in North Carolina was confirmed by Semafor and other outlets, with the SLF redirecting funds to Kansas. The move has drawn attention from Democratic leaders, including Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, who cited the shift as an indicator of the Senate majority’s direction. Meanwhile, Whatley’s campaign remains confident, citing potential late-breaking voter support and the ability to highlight Cooper’s record.

Where the accounts differ

The Daily Caller and The Guardian both report that the SLF paused ad spending in North Carolina and redirected funds to Kansas, but The Daily Caller attributes the decision to the SLF’s focus on maintaining the Senate majority, while The Guardian notes the SLF’s previous ad campaign linking Roy Cooper to a crime. The Guardian also mentions that the SLF had planned to spend an additional $30 million in North Carolina, which is not explicitly stated in The Daily Caller. The Daily Wire and Just The News both mention the SLF’s shift to Kansas, but Just The News adds that the PAC is also concerned about its chances in Georgia, a detail not included in other accounts.

Judge halts contentious border construction in and near Texas’s Big Bend National Park

A federal judge on Friday halted Trump administration border construction plans in Texas’s Big Bend region. Judge Kathleen Cardone ruled that the administration cannot build border barriers or related infrastructure, or engage in any construction or ground disturbing activities in the area. The ruling was based on the likelihood that plaintiffs would succeed on the merits of their claims and that they would suffer irreparable harm without an injunction. The construction has drawn criticism from Democrats and Republicans, who have warned of adverse effects on Big Bend National Park, the local economy, and landowners. The administration had waived environmental laws to expedite construction earlier this year, but has stated it will not build a large wall inside the park.

The ruling blocked the use of Department of Homeland Security waivers that suspended environmental and historical protection laws. The administration has said it will not build a large wall inside the park but is pursuing other construction activity there. A Customs and Border Protection spokesperson stated the agency remains confident in its legal authorities to secure the border while preserving the unique landscape of the Big Bend region. Opponents of the construction celebrated the ruling, calling it a historic victory for Big Bend and its environmental protections.

Where the accounts differ

The Hill and The Guardian both mention that the administration waived environmental laws to expedite construction, while the New York Post and ABC News refer to the waivers as a "swath of waivers" and "a swath of waivers allowing it to bypass numerous regulations." The Guardian and The New York Post attribute the claim about the administration pausing construction after bulldozers were spotted in August to Rodney Scott, the CBP commissioner, while The Hill and ABC News do not attribute this claim to a specific individual. The Guardian and The New York Post also mention that the administration has awarded $6 billion in contracts for the "smart wall," while The Hill and ABC News refer to the $46 billion effort without specifying the $6 billion figure. The Just The News account does not mention the $46 billion figure or the $6 billion contracts.

Cyclospora outbreak linked to lettuce sickens hundreds, prompting FDA recall and threatening public health

Cyclospora found at Mexican farm linked to lettuce outbreak, FDA says

The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) announced on Friday that cyclospora was detected at a Mexican farm associated with a large foodborne illness outbreak. Inspectors found the parasite in a wastewater tank and a drainage ditch used for lettuce cultivation. The FDA stated that while the exact method of contamination is unclear, the samples provide “strong epidemiological data” linking the outbreak to the Taylor Farms site. The agency’s findings follow an earlier CDC declaration that the multi-state outbreak had ended, which affected nearly 13,000 people across 21 states and resulted in 570 hospitalizations and two deaths. Taylor Farms attributed the contamination to an independent grower no longer part of its supplier program and stated its facility in central Mexico remains closed.

The FDA concluded its investigation into the Cyclospora outbreak tied to recalled Taylor Farms iceberg lettuce, finding “strong” evidence linking the infections to the recalled products. The agency identified multiple farms and a processing facility in Mexico that supplied the recalled shredded lettuce. Two environmental samples tested positive for Cyclospora, one from a wastewater tank and another from a drainage ditch. The FDA noted that while the contamination source remains unclear, the data supports a link to Taylor Farms de Mexico. Taylor Farms’ spokesperson stated the positive sample came from discharged waste and sewer water outside its processing facility and emphasized that there is not enough evidence to determine how contamination occurred.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian reports that Mexico’s own food safety authority did not detect cyclospora in the lettuce or water at the facility, and that the FDA had to retract a faulty test result. Fox Business does not mention this Mexican authority’s findings or the retraction. The Guardian states the CDC declared the outbreak over, while Fox Business notes that the CDC previously declared the outbreak over on Sept. 11. The Guardian attributes the contamination to an independent grower, while Fox Business reports that Taylor Farms’ spokesperson said the sample came from discharged waste and sewer water outside its processing facility.

Sources The Guardian · Fox Business
