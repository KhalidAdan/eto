Republican Senator Discloses Her Husband Committed Suicide

Republican Senator Deb Fischer (R-NE) announced on Friday that her husband, Bruce Fischer, died by suicide. Initially, the family had stated he had "passed away peacefully" on November 22, but further investigation revealed he took his own life. Fischer described Bruce as her "partner in life, my greatest source of strength, and the heart of our family." Bruce Fischer was 76 years old and a lifelong rancher in the Nebraska Sandhills. The couple, married since 1972, had three sons and six grandchildren. The family said they would not provide further details or comment on Bruce’s death.

Bruce Fischer made national headlines in January 2025 for refusing to shake the hand of then-Vice President Kamala Harris during his wife’s swearing-in ceremony. The incident, captured on video, showed Bruce holding a Bible and a cane, and responding only with a "thank you" when Harris extended her hand.

The Daily Wire and The Hill mention Bruce was a "lifelong rancher" and "conservationist," while The New York Post and The Daily Caller describe him as a "rancher" and "conservationist dedicated to responsible resource stewardship." NY Senate leader Andrea Stewart-Cousins, first woman in the role, dies at 76

New York State Senate Majority Leader Andrea Stewart-Cousins, the first woman to lead the chamber, died Friday, her office confirmed. She was 76. Stewart-Cousins was reelected by her constituents on Nov. 3 and retained as Senate Democratic leader mid-month. Her death was announced by Mike Murphy, a spokesperson for the Senate's Democratic majority, though the cause and location were not disclosed. Stewart-Cousins was a prominent advocate for progressive policies, including legalizing marijuana, strengthening gun control, and advancing voting rights and police accountability measures. She also led efforts to secure voter approval of a state constitutional amendment protecting abortion rights. Her leadership marked a significant shift in New York's political landscape, breaking the so-called "three men in a room" tradition of decision-making.

Stewart-Cousins, a resident of Yonkers, had a long career in public service, including roles as a teacher, sales and marketing professional, and local government official. She was the first African American woman to lead the New York State Senate and the first woman to hold the position. Her leadership was widely praised by political figures across the spectrum, including Governor Kathy Hochul, former Governor Andrew Cuomo, and New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani. Tributes highlighted her impact on reproductive rights, education, and civil rights, as well as her role in advancing legislation such as the Eric Garner Anti-Chokehold Act.

Where the accounts differ

FOX News reports that Stewart-Cousins was found dead in her Yonkers home on Friday morning and that her daughter tried to contact her on Thursday night before requesting a welfare check. This detail is not included in ABC News, Newsweek, The Hill, Washington Examiner, or Just The News. Additionally, FOX News states she was first elected to the state senate in 2007, while ABC News and The Hill note she was first elected in 2006. Newsweek and The Hill mention she was the first African American woman to lead the Senate, while FOX News and Washington Examiner refer to her as the first Black woman to hold the role. ABC News and The Hill attribute her role as the first woman and first Black woman to the Senate to her own leadership, while FOX News and Washington Examiner attribute it to her being the first Black woman.

Zeldin: No airborne lead detected in Iowa a day after Army blast

The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) reported no airborne lead was detected in the region following the Army’s controlled detonation of lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa. EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin stated that air monitors recorded zero detect of airborne lead since the detonation. The agency collected over 1,000 samples of air, soil, and water after the blast, with results expected as early as Friday. The operation was conducted to neutralize deteriorating explosives deemed too dangerous to transport. Federal officials declared the detonation a success, lifting a shelter-in-place order and allowing residents to resume normal activities.

The EPA also reported that no increase in lead levels was found in drinking water sources tested after the detonation. The agency emphasized that it is continuing its environmental assessment, comparing samples collected before and after the event. President Trump praised the operation, calling it a success and stating residents were “100% safe.” The Army cited the need to prevent an uncontrolled explosion involving the unstable material, which had been stored for over two decades.

Where the accounts differ

CBS News and the New York Post mention that the EPA collected over 1,600 samples before and after the detonation, while the New York Post also notes that the Army had planned the detonation for November and had communicated with the EPA and local officials. Holiday scammers target older Americans, often returning to exploit them multiple times, experts warn

Scammers are increasingly targeting older Americans during the holiday season, often returning to exploit the same victims multiple times, according to recent reports. Federal authorities and consumer protection groups warn that the holidays are a particularly lucrative time for fraud, as older adults engage in more online shopping, travel, and charitable giving. A November 2026 AARP survey found that 84% of U.S. adults had encountered a scam or fraud attempt ahead of the holidays, with nearly 39% of those who purchased something through an online advertisement reporting they were scammed. The Federal Trade Commission reported that Americans ages 60 and older lost around $3 billion in fraud losses in 2025, with older victims losing as much as $119.5 billion last year.

Experts note that scammers often return to the same victims, using tactics such as posing as officials promising to recover funds or sharing personal information on the black market. The Treasury Department's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network said that after a successful scam, some fraudsters share victims' personal information, allowing other criminals to target them again with different schemes. AARP's Amy Nofziger stated that criminals target older adults because they hold the majority of wealth in the United States.

FOX News also cites a November 2026 U.S. Secret Service public advisory and includes quotes from USSS Investigative Analyst Bre Whalen and AARP's Amy Nofziger.
