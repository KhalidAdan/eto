Senate Democrats want answers on reported suicide attempts on warship

Senate Democrats are seeking details about the status of the USS Abraham Lincoln aircraft carrier following reports of attempted suicides by sailors. The ship has been at sea since November and is currently in the Middle East. Families of sailors have reported several crew members attempting to jump overboard or being stopped from doing so. Senate Armed Services Committee member Richard Blumenthal, D-Conn., raised concerns about the length of the deployment, supply shortages, contaminated water, and deteriorating mental health. He also questioned whether the conflict with Iran is pushing deployments far beyond planned durations. Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth have not provided clear timelines for the ship's return to the U.S. and have disputed claims of misrepresented conditions.

The Lincoln has been at sea for over 250 days, with only one brief port call. Reports of deteriorating living conditions, including moldy showers, bad food, and a lack of personal hygiene supplies, have sparked outrage on Capitol Hill. Sen. Ruben Gallego, D-Ariz., called for an official bipartisan visit to the ship. The Navy has denied claims of increased suicidal ideation or attempts, stating that mental health professionals are available to address concerns. However, some families report not receiving responses from the Navy, raising further concerns about the situation.

Where the accounts differ

UPI and Washington Examiner both report that the Lincoln has been at sea for over 250 days, but UPI states the deployment started in November, while Washington Examiner specifies November 21, 2025. UPI mentions that the ship has been in the Middle East since November, while Washington Examiner notes it is in the Iran war zone. UPI and Washington Examiner both report that sailors have attempted to jump overboard, but UPI includes a quote from Sen. Chris Coons, D-Del., criticizing Hegseth for dismissing concerns as "fake news," which is not mentioned in Washington Examiner. UPI also reports that Hegseth claimed conditions on the Lincoln have been "completely misrepresented," while Washington Examiner states that the Navy has denied claims of increased suicidal ideation or attempts.

Sources UPI · Washington Examiner · NPR · The Daily Wire · CBS News · Just The News

Luigi Mangione Lays Out Chilling Step By Step Plot To Kill Brian Thompson

Luigi Mangione pleaded guilty to federal stalking charges in connection with the killing of UnitedHealthcare CEO Brian Thompson. In court, Mangione admitted to planning the attack, using a 3-D printer to make a gun, and shooting Thompson in Manhattan on December 4, 2024. His guilty plea could impact his upcoming state murder trial. Mangione's family and prosecutors have commented on the plea, with the family calling it an important step toward justice. Mangione faces a maximum sentence of life in prison for the federal charges and additional state charges, though his lawyers are seeking to dismiss some of those under double jeopardy laws.

Where the accounts differ

The Daily Wire and BBC both report that Mangione described his motivation as being driven by his experience with the healthcare system and his severe back pain. FOX News and NPR do not mention this specific motivation, instead focusing on the legal implications of the plea deal and double jeopardy. The Daily Wire and CBS News both state that Mangione admitted to using a 3-D printer to make a gun, while FOX News and NPR do not specify the method of gun creation. The Daily Wire and BBC mention that Mangione stated he emailed UnitedHealthcare posing as an investor, while FOX News and NPR do not include this detail. The Daily Wire and CBS News report that Mangione's family called the plea "an important step towards justice," while FOX News and NPR do not include this specific family statement.

US missionary kidnapped in Niger is released after 9 months in captivity

An American missionary, Kevin Rideout, was kidnapped in Niger's capital, Niamey, on October 21, 2025, by three armed individuals, according to multiple accounts. His organization, SIM International, confirmed his release after nine months in captivity, stating he is in good health and in the care of U.S. officials. The U.S. Embassy in Niamey issued a security alert following the kidnapping, warning of heightened risks for American citizens. No armed group claimed responsibility for the abduction. The incident occurred in a country ruled by a military junta that overthrew the democratically elected president in 2023. The junta has distanced itself from Western partners, including the United States, and formed a new alliance with Russia.

The U.S. government has been involved in Niger's security efforts, including the withdrawal of troops in 2024 and the donation of military equipment in 2025. The release of Rideout was highlighted by President Donald Trump, who claimed to have helped secure his freedom and described the region as the "epicenter of Islamist terrorism."

Where the accounts differ

ABC News and Associated Press report that Rideout was kidnapped by three armed individuals and that no group claimed responsibility. The Guardian and CBS News add that Trump claimed to have helped secure his release, a claim not mentioned in ABC or Associated Press. UPI states that Rideout was in FBI custody and in good health as of Thursday, a detail not included in other accounts. NPR notes that the U.S. State Department has not yet confirmed his release, while CBS News mentions that the kidnapping occurred approximately 100 yards from the presidential palace, a detail absent in other reports. The Guardian also includes information about Trump's social media post and the broader context of regional security issues, which are not detailed in other accounts.

Sources ABC News · Associated Press · The Guardian · UPI · NPR · CBS News

Judge dismisses Trump administration lawsuit alleging antisemitism at Harvard University

A federal judge in Boston dismissed a Trump administration lawsuit that accused Harvard University of failing to protect Jewish students from harassment. The lawsuit, filed in March 2026, alleged that Harvard officials did nothing as Jewish students were "harassed, physically assaulted, stalked, and spat upon" during pro-Palestinian demonstrations following Hamas' Oct. 7, 2023, attacks on Israel. The judge ruled that the incidents cited were "too isolated and episodic" to prove an ongoing violation of Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964. The government sought to recover billions of dollars in federal research grants awarded to Harvard since October 2023, arguing that the university had failed to protect Jewish students. Harvard President Alan M. Garber stated the university formed a task force to combat antisemitism and made administrative changes to its policies.

The lawsuit is part of a broader dispute between Harvard and the Trump administration, which has also sought to cut Harvard's research funding and limit its ability to enroll foreign students and U.S. military members. In a separate case, a federal judge ordered the Trump administration to reverse more than $2.6 billion in federal funding cuts to Harvard, finding that the White House used antisemitism as a "smokescreen for a targeted, ideologically-motivated assault" on universities. Despite the legal battles, Harvard and the Trump administration have held some negotiations, though the Justice Department's lawsuit marked an extraordinary impasse.

Where the accounts differ

NPR and New York Post both state that the judge ruled the incidents were "too isolated and episodic," but the New York Post adds that only three incidents occurred after the 2023-24 academic year, all in March 2025. The Daily Wire and CBS News also mention the three incidents in March 2025, while UPI and FOX News include additional details about specific incidents, such as protesters breaching barricades outside an event featuring former Israeli Prime Minister Naftali Bennett and a "die-in" at Widener Library. UPI also notes that the administration sought $1 billion in damages from Harvard, a claim not mentioned in other accounts. FOX News attributes the claim about seeking $1 billion in damages to President Trump, while CBS News and NPR do not specify who made the claim.

Sources NPR · New York Post · The Daily Wire · CBS News · UPI · FOX News

Ukraine’s drones hit a major Russian refinery 800 miles from the border, sparking a fire

Ukrainian drones struck a major refinery deep inside Russia, military officials said, the fourth in three days in Kyiv’s monthslong campaign to choke Moscow’s vital oil sector. The refinery targeted is one of Russia’s biggest facilities, according to Ukrainian General Staff. The attack caused a fire at the facility, which processes up to 74 million barrels of oil annually. Russia’s Bashkortostan governor, Radiy Khabirov, said a drone attack wounded two civilians and started a fire in an industrial zone of Salavat. Wildberries, Russia’s biggest online retailer, said a fire started at one of its facilities in the Salavat industrial zone after the overnight attack. The attack came a day after Ukrainian anti-ship missiles and drones blitzed a major Russian naval base on the Black Sea coast.

Ukraine’s recent drone campaign has included strikes that cut electricity, cell phone service and water for extended periods. The daily attacks have also targeted Russian ships and ferries that deliver food, fuel, and other essential supplies from Russia. Ukraine’s military says in the past month it's hit more than 130 Russian vessels in the Sea of Azov and more than 80 in the Black Sea. The strikes have dented Russia’s refining capacity, bringing shortages at gas stations.

Where the accounts differ

AP reports that the refinery targeted is one of Russia’s largest oil refining and petrochemical facilities, located about 800 miles from Ukraine’s border. NPR does not mention the refinery but focuses on drone strikes in Crimea, including attacks on Russian ships and ferries. Straight Arrow News reports that Ukrainian drones struck a major Russian refinery about 800 miles from Ukraine’s border, but also highlights the U.S. military exercise in Germany where Ukrainian drone operators demonstrated their effectiveness. Washington Examiner and The Daily Caller do not mention the refinery attack but focus on Ukraine’s missile and interceptor shortages.

Sources Associated Press · NPR · Straight Arrow News · Washington Examiner · The Daily Caller

Polls suggest GOP could gain traction on health care, energy, and voter ID in midterms

A national survey by the Tyson Group found broad support for policies aimed at lowering energy and grocery costs, preparing for flu season, regulating artificial intelligence, and protecting healthcare access for seniors. Energy topped the list with 88% of likely voters supporting expanding U.S. energy infrastructure to lower gas and electricity prices. Nearly 82% said ensuring flu shots are available, accessible, and affordable was a priority. The Tyson Group also found 71% supported creating a uniform national standard for ingredient labeling to prevent a potential 12% increase in grocery costs.

President Donald Trump’s top political advisers, including James Blair, Tony Fabrizio, and Alex Latcham, urged Republican candidates to focus more on health care and less on voter integrity. Polling showed Republicans had a 46-point advantage when messaging about greater transparency around health insurers’ profits, claim denials, and wait times while accusing Democrats of “lining insurers’ pockets.” Banning congressional stock trading and cracking down on fraud also performed well, with 44- and 38-point advantages for Republicans, respectively. The SAVE America Act, which would require proof of citizenship to register to vote, produced a smaller 14-point Republican advantage.

Where the accounts differ

The Washington Examiner and Axios both report that Trump’s team advised Republicans to focus on health care, with the Tyson Group survey showing strong support for health care transparency and flu shot availability. The Daily Wire and Axios also note that the SAVE Act had a smaller advantage for Republicans. FOX News focuses on Rep. Nicole Malliotakis criticizing Democrats for opposing voter ID measures, which is not mentioned in the other accounts. The Daily Caller does not cover the polling or messaging strategy but instead discusses Shane Gillis as a bellwether, which is not covered by the other outlets.

Sources Washington Examiner · The Daily Wire · Axios · FOX News · The Daily Caller

'Unprecedented' rain kills four in Japan as thousands stranded at airport

‘Unprecedented’ rain kills four in Japan as thousands stranded at airport

Heavy rainfall in Chiba prefecture, east of Tokyo, led to at least four deaths and stranded thousands. The Japan Meteorological Agency issued its highest level-5 warning for heavy rains and landslides, marking the first time this level was used for the prefecture. Over 7,000 passengers spent the night at Tokyo’s Narita airport, while 1,700 people sought shelter at the Chiba prefectural government building. The rain, which exceeded the monthly August average, caused power outages, evacuation orders for over 400,000 people, and flooded roads and rail lines. Soldiers were deployed to assist with relief efforts.

The rain, which reached up to 115mm per hour, caused significant disruption, including flooded roads and damaged infrastructure. A man in his 60s or 70s was found floating in a flooded road, and a 66-year-old woman died after her car was trapped in floodwaters. Two others are reported to have died after being unable to escape their cars. The Chiba governor described the situation as “extremely unusual,” noting he had never experienced such a disaster before. Officials warned of ongoing flood risks and urged people to follow evacuation advisories.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian and BBC differ in the number of deaths reported. The Guardian states at least four people died, while the BBC reports at least eight. The BBC also mentions two additional deaths from collapsing on the streets. Al Jazeera reports four confirmed deaths and mentions a fifth person feared dead and a sixth unaccounted for. The Guardian and Al Jazeera both mention the man found floating in a flooded road and the woman killed in her car, but the BBC adds two more deaths from collapsing on the streets. The BBC also states that all flights were scheduled to operate normally at Narita, while The Guardian and Al Jazeera note that some flights may be delayed.

Sources The Guardian · BBC · Al Jazeera
