Brazil heads to a runoff election after Bolsonaro and Lula fail to garner a majority of votes

Brazil’s presidential election resulted in neither candidate securing a majority, leading to a runoff between incumbent President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and Flávio Bolsonaro, the son of former President Jair Bolsonaro. The electoral court confirmed that both candidates will face off in a second round on October 25. Lula, 80, is seeking a fourth non-consecutive term, while Bolsonaro, 45, is running as the leading right-wing candidate. The election comes amid a polarized political climate, with significant implications for Brazil’s future direction and its relationships with global powers.

Where the accounts differ

The Washington Examiner and Al Jazeera both state that Bolsonaro received 47% of valid votes and Lula 45.1%, while the Guardian reports Bolsonaro with 47% and Lula with 45.1%. The Guardian also mentions Bolsonaro received 56.1 million votes to Lula’s 53.8 million, a figure not included in the Washington Examiner or Al Jazeera. The BBC and Newsweek do not provide specific vote counts but note that neither candidate reached the 50% threshold. The Guardian and Al Jazeera mention Bolsonaro’s lead over Lula in the first round, while the BBC and Newsweek describe the race as closely contested with polls showing them roughly tied. The Guardian and Al Jazeera also note that Bolsonaro’s father, Jair Bolsonaro, is serving a 27-year sentence for plotting a coup, a detail included in the BBC and Newsweek but not in the Guardian or Al Jazeera.

Sources Washington Examiner · BBC · The Guardian · NPR · Al Jazeera · Newsweek

Trump praises Israeli hero who thwarted cockpit attack, calls him 'my plumber'

President Donald Trump praised Israeli citizen Yaniv Hayoun, a Flydubai passenger who helped subdue a radicalized Omani co-pilot during a mid-flight attack, calling him “my plumber” and a “Trump fan.” Trump shared a video on his Truth Social account highlighting Hayoun’s actions, which he described as heroic. The incident occurred on a FlyDubai flight from the UAE to Tel Aviv, where the co-pilot allegedly stabbed the pilot and attempted to take control of the aircraft. Passengers, including Hayoun, intervened to stop the attack, with Hayoun entering the cockpit to subdue the attacker. The plane made an emergency landing in Saudi Arabia.

The incident has also drawn attention to security concerns at the RAF Fairford air base in the UK, where five British nationals were arrested on suspicion of planning a terror attack. The U.S. removed its bombers from the base following the arrests, citing security concerns. British Prime Minister Andy Burnham stated there were “strong indications” that Iran was involved, though Iran denied any role. The U.S. and UK are collaborating on the investigation, with the UK emphasizing the complexity of the case.

Where the accounts differ

The New York Post and The Guardian both report that the co-pilot was an Omani national named Hammam al-Hammami, who had studied engineering at RMIT University in Australia and left before completing his degree. The Guardian adds that al-Hammami was barred from flying by Oman over extremist concerns and was later hired by Flydubai. The New York Post does not mention al-Hammami’s name or his educational background. CBS News and The Washington Examiner do not mention the co-pilot’s name or his ties to Australia, focusing instead on the UK arrests and U.S. security concerns. FOX News and The Daily Wire refer to the co-pilot as an Omani national but do not name him or mention his Australian ties.

Sources New York Post · CBS News · Washington Examiner · FOX News · The Daily Wire · The Guardian

US Coast Guard searches for missing air ambulance off coast of Massachusetts

The US Coast Guard confirmed it found debris from a medical transport aircraft off the shore of Massachusetts’ Nantucket Island and is searching for the six people who were on board. The jet lost contact with the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) during a flight from Bermuda to Boston on Saturday morning. The Canadian-registered air ambulance jet had four crew members and two passengers onboard when it departed Bermuda’s LF Wade International Airport overnight for the flight to Massachusetts. The plane was flying at 24,000 feet (7,300 metres) for much of the journey before descending 13,000 feet in about two minutes shortly before flight controllers lost contact. The Massachusetts Port Authority confirmed the aircraft did not arrive at Boston Logan International Airport as scheduled.

The FAA issued a search-and-rescue alert notice for the Gulfstream G-100 at about 1.20am local time Saturday. The plane is operated by Latitude Air Ambulance, based in Ontario, Canada, and provides medical evacuation services. The company’s website states it has run thousands of missions since 2009 with a "flawless safety record." The Coast Guard deployed two search and rescue planes, a helicopter, and a motor lifeboat to continue the search. The National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) is gathering information but has not provided an update on the situation.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian and CBS News both state the plane departed Bermuda at 12:41am on Saturday, while FOX News and Straight Arrow News report the departure time as 11:41pm on Friday. The Guardian and CBS News mention the plane descended 13,000 feet in about two minutes, while FOX News and Al Jazeera report the plane plummeted from 24,000 feet to 11,000 feet in three minutes. The Guardian and CBS News attribute the statement about the plane descending to flight data, while FOX News and Al Jazeera attribute it to FlightAware data. The Guardian and CBS News note the FAA issued a search-and-rescue alert at 1:20am, while FOX News and Straight Arrow News report the FAA issued the alert at 1:20am as well, but Al Jazeera states the FAA alerted the Coast Guard at about 2am on Saturday. The Guardian and CBS News mention the plane had four crew members and two passengers, while FOX News and Al Jazeera specify the composition as two Canadian pilots, two medical staff, a patient, and a relative.

Sources The Guardian · NPR · CBS News · FOX News · Al Jazeera · Straight Arrow News

Trump names national intelligence chief Jay Clayton as new AI czar

President Donald Trump announced on Sunday that Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton will serve as the White House AI czar. Clayton will lead a new initiative called the "Super Intelligence Force," which Trump claims will ensure the United States remains a global leader in the development of artificial intelligence. The force will coordinate federal engagement with consumers, public interest groups, religious organizations, critical infrastructure providers, and technology companies.

Clayton, who previously served as the U.S. Attorney for the Southern District of New York and chairman of the Securities and Exchange Commission, has called super intelligence a "national security issue." He has also pushed back against calls for a "pause" in AI development, arguing that other countries are continuing to advance the technology. The White House announced that Clayton will lead a three-month effort to assess the "risks and opportunities" of AI, with a report due within 120 days.

Where the accounts differ

NPR states that Clayton called super intelligence a "national security issue" and that Trump's "Super Intelligence Force" will "coordinate the Federal Government's engagement with Consumers, Public Interest Groups, Religious Organizations, Critical Infrastructure Providers, and Super Intelligence Companies." FOX News reports that Trump said the force will "ensure that America continues to lead the World in Super Intelligence," and that it will report directly to him and White House chief of staff Susie Wiles. CBS News adds that the force will include Andrew Ferguson, Emil Michael, and Scott Kupor as members, and that Clayton will report to Trump and Wiles. The Guardian notes that Trump created an "AI Force" and a czar to oversee the technology’s growth and monitor for "bad" development, and that Trump met with AI industry leaders to discuss concerns. Straight Arrow News mentions that the force will include Vice President JD Vance, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent. Washington Examiner does not mention the composition of the force or the role of other officials.

Sources Washington Examiner · NPR · FOX News · CBS News · Straight Arrow News · The Guardian

Attorney for accused Cornell student calls on Letitia James to step aside over prior comments

An attorney for one of seven former Cornell University fraternity members accused in a civil lawsuit has raised concerns about New York Attorney General Letitia James’ impartiality in the criminal investigation. Andrew Miltenberg, representing one of the accused, argued that James’ earlier public statements supporting the accuser created an appearance of bias. The case involves allegations that seven fraternity brothers drugged and sexually assaulted a female student at the Chi Phi fraternity house in October 2024. The men have denied wrongdoing, with some claiming the sexual activity was consensual and others denying participation. No criminal charges have been filed against the seven men.

Cornell University’s president, Michael Kotlikoff, expressed deep sorrow over the case, calling it one of the most challenging periods for the university. He pledged greater transparency amid mounting outrage over how the school handled the case. The woman, identified as Jane Doe in court documents, alleges she was sexually assaulted by multiple members of the Chi Phi fraternity in 2024 and has sued the university and seven former students. The case has also renewed scrutiny of the “voluntary intoxication loophole” in New York law, which makes it difficult to prosecute sexual assaults involving victims who voluntarily consumed alcohol or drugs.

Where the accounts differ

The Washington Examiner and the New York Post differ on the number of men involved in the alleged rape. The Washington Examiner states that Giuffra believes more men were involved than the seven listed in the lawsuit, while the New York Post does not mention any discrepancy in the number of accused individuals. The New York Post also attributes the claim about James’ bias to Rensselaer County Executive Steven McLaughlin, while the Washington Examiner does not name a source for the claim about James’ impartiality. The FOX News account provides more detail on Miltenberg’s objection, including his statement that James’ earlier comments created an “avoidable question about the appearance of impartiality,” which is not explicitly stated in the Washington Examiner or the New York Post.

Sources Washington Examiner · The Daily Wire · NPR · New York Post · FOX News · Al Jazeera

Japan Issues 'Strong Protest' After US Marine Accused of Killing Woman

A U.S. Marine has been arrested in connection with the death of a woman in Okinawa, prompting the Japanese government to lodge a protest with the United States. The incident occurred in Naha, Okinawa’s capital, where the suspect, identified as Devin J. Ballard, a 20-year-old lance corporal with the Third Marine Expeditionary Force, was found by police. Japanese authorities allege that Ballard robbed and killed Anna Yagi, 39, who was found dead at a hotel in Naha. Ballard has denied the allegations. The Japanese government has called the incident "extremely brutal and heinous" and demanded strict discipline and preventive measures from the U.S. military. The U.S. embassy in Tokyo has stated it is cooperating with Japanese authorities in the investigation.

The U.S. military maintains a significant presence in Japan, with around 55,000 personnel stationed across the country, including a substantial number in Okinawa. The incident has raised concerns about the U.S. military presence in the region, particularly given the history of crimes involving U.S. service members in Okinawa. Japanese officials have emphasized the need for maximum cooperation with the investigation and stricter measures to prevent future incidents.

Where the accounts differ

Newsweek and The Guardian both report that the suspect is identified as Devin J. Ballard, while ABC News and Straight Arrow News refer to him as Devin Jacob Ballard. The Guardian and NPR mention that the suspect is a 20-year-old Marine, while Straight Arrow News states he was arrested around 1 a.m. local time. ABC News and BBC report that the cause of death was suffocation due to pressure on the neck, while The Guardian and NPR do not specify the cause of death. The Guardian and NPR also note that the suspect was arrested after being spotted in an entertainment district, while Newsweek and Straight Arrow News do not mention this detail. The Guardian and NPR provide additional context about the history of crimes involving U.S. military personnel in Okinawa, which is omitted in Newsweek and Straight Arrow News.

Sources Newsweek · ABC News · The Guardian · NPR · BBC · Straight Arrow News

California Realtor Accused of Spying for China on Taiwan President’s Son

A 34-year-old California realtor, Wanying “Heather” Zhang, was arrested at Los Angeles International Airport on Sunday as she attempted to depart for China. Federal authorities allege she acted as an unregistered agent of the People’s Republic of China, surveilling a close relative of a Taiwanese government official. The FBI and U.S. Attorney Bill Essayli stated that Zhang coordinated the trip with an associate and used tradecraft to avoid detection, failing to notify the Attorney General of her foreign government activities, which is a federal offense. The FBI’s Rapid Response account confirmed the arrest, and the case includes alleged text exchanges and surveillance activities in Seattle, Washington.

Zhang is accused of traveling to Seattle in 2025 to surveil the son of Taiwan’s president, Lai Ching-te, and sending photos, videos, and license plate information to Chinese officials. The FBI alleges she used a rented car to photograph the family’s residence and shared the data with handlers. The Justice Department claims her activities date back to September 2022, with communications involving two individuals believed to be Chinese government officials. The case is part of a broader context of heightened tensions between China and Taiwan, with the U.S. maintaining a strong stance in support of Taiwan’s sovereignty.

Where the accounts differ

Newsweek and The Guardian both state that the FBI did not directly name Zhang’s alleged target but shared a report from the New York Post identifying him as Lai Ting-yu. However, the New York Post and Al Jazeera explicitly name Lai Ting-yu as the target, while Newsweek and The Guardian do not. The New York Post and FOX News describe specific text exchanges between Zhang and a Chinese government official, including a message where Zhang allegedly identified the target as “that young one.” This detail is absent in Newsweek and The Guardian. Additionally, the New York Post and FOX News mention that Zhang and her associate rented a black Nissan Rogue and used a neighbor’s security camera to capture their actions, which is not included in the accounts from Newsweek, The Guardian, or Al Jazeera.

Sources Newsweek · New York Post · FOX News · The Guardian · Just The News · Al Jazeera

White House Monitors Suspected Plague Case in Russia

The White House is monitoring reports of a suspected plague case in Russia, according to a Trump administration official. A 28-year-old woman, Daria Shipilova, worked at an anti-plague research institute in Irkutsk, Russia, and died after breaking a test tube containing the pneumonic plague pathogen. She was hospitalized on Sept. 29 with severe pneumonia and died between Oct. 1 and 2. Russian authorities imposed quarantines at several medical facilities and placed nearly 200 people under medical observation. Russian health authorities have not confirmed that Shipilova contracted plague, stating she had "pneumonia of unknown etiology" and that tests found no microorganisms linked to her work. The White House referred questions to the State Department, which did not respond to a request for comment.

Russian officials have taken precautionary measures in response to Shipilova's death, including quarantining hospitals and monitoring contacts. The Russian public health agency described the epidemiological situation in the region as stable. Some outlets reported that five hospitals were placed under quarantine, while others cited three. The number of quarantined hospitals and the exact number of people under observation vary across reports.

Where the accounts differ

Mother Jones reports that five hospitals were quarantined, while The Daily Caller and Straight Arrow News mention three. The Daily Wire and The Daily Caller state that 200 people were placed under quarantine, while Axios and Straight Arrow News report nearly 200. The Daily Wire and The Daily Caller claim that Shipilova broke a test tube containing live Yersinia pestis, a claim not explicitly made by Axios or Straight Arrow News. The Daily Caller and The Daily Wire mention that some Russian officials cast doubt on the test tube incident, while Axios and Straight Arrow News do not. The Daily Wire and The Daily Caller attribute the claim about the test tube to The Moscow Times, while Axios and Straight Arrow News do not specify the source.

Sources Axios · Mother Jones · The Daily Wire · FOX News · The Daily Caller · Straight Arrow News
