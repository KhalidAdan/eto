US threat of ‘economic D-Day’ for Iran tests Trump’s China detente

The Trump administration has announced plans for an “economic D-Day” against Iran, aiming to sever all economic lifelines sustaining the country. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent described the measures as the “greatest coordinated economic isolation in the history of the world.” The strategy includes targeting Iran’s trade partners, with China, Iran’s largest trade partner, being a key focus. The administration has not yet disclosed the full scope of the sanctions, but officials have emphasized the intent to pressure Iran through economic means. The move comes amid ongoing tensions between the U.S. and Iran, which began with U.S.-Israeli strikes in February that killed Iranian Supreme Leader Ayatollah Khamenei. Iranian officials have denounced the sanctions, calling them an admission of defeat and warning of retaliation.

Iranian officials have responded to the sanctions by stating they will not be intimidated and have prepared scenarios to counter any economic warfare. The U.S. has also faced criticism for not targeting Chinese financial institutions, which are seen as a key node in Iran’s oil trade. Analysts suggest that excluding China from the sanctions could limit the effectiveness of the economic pressure campaign. Meanwhile, the U.S. has also been engaging with other countries, including the UAE, which has temporarily suspended trade with Iran under U.S. pressure.

Where the accounts differ

Al Jazeera reports that the Trump administration has not yet provided details about the “economic D-Day” measures, while Mother Jones states that Bessent offered almost nothing in the way of details. Straight Arrow News mentions that the sanctions are aimed at “strengthening bilateral cooperation between Iran and Pakistan” and includes quotes from Iranian officials, which Al Jazeera does not include. Newsweek highlights the collapse of Iran’s rial and its impact on inflation, a detail absent in Al Jazeera and Mother Jones. Associated Press focuses on the global market reaction to the sanctions, which is not covered in the other accounts. Washington Examiner omits any mention of the sanctions against Iran, instead focusing on the trade war with Canada.

Sources Al Jazeera · Straight Arrow News · Washington Examiner · Mother Jones · Associated Press · Newsweek

Supreme Court: Trump can start carrying out order restricting mail-in voting

The U.S. Supreme Court ruled Monday that President Donald Trump can begin implementing parts of an executive order aimed at restricting mail-in voting. The order, signed in March 2026, directs the Department of Homeland Security to create state-specific lists of eligible voters and requires the U.S. Postal Service to implement new procedures for mail-in ballots. The ruling allows the Department of Homeland Security to proceed with compiling these lists, though it does not resolve the legality of the order itself. The Postal Service had previously issued a final rule requiring states to submit voter information for tracking, but a lower court blocked that rule nationwide. The Supreme Court's decision does not affect the ongoing injunction against the Postal Service's rule, which remains in place.

The ruling was issued in an unsigned order and was met with dissent from three liberal justices, including Ketanji Brown Jackson, who argued that the decision injects chaos into the upcoming midterm elections. The decision allows the administration to move forward with implementing parts of the order, though legal challenges could still affect its full implementation before the November elections. Voting rights groups have expressed concern that the order could confuse voters and disenfranchise those who rely on mail ballots.

Where the accounts differ

UPI reports that the ruling does not address the overall legality of the executive order and that the postal service entered a final rule into the Federal Register despite court injunctions. The Washington Examiner states that the DOJ and GOP states argued the injunction should be lifted because the Democrat-led states lacked standing to file the lawsuit. The Daily Wire notes that the Supreme Court found the states challenging the order had not shown concrete and imminent injuries required to bring their claims. Mother Jones highlights that the ruling overrules a series of federal court rulings blocking the order and instructs DHS to create voter lists. Axios mentions that the administration has asked the Supreme Court to step in and that the USPS violated a federal court order by finalizing the rules.

Sources UPI · Washington Examiner · The Daily Wire · Slate · Mother Jones · Axios

US set for largest mass visa revocation in history targeting up to 200,000 foreigners, officials say

The Trump administration is preparing to revoke business and tourism visas of up to 200,000 foreigners who have applied for or are seeking asylum in the United States. The action, which would be the largest single mass revocation of visas in U.S. history, is expected to be announced in the coming weeks. The revocation would target B1 and B2 visas issued between 2016 and 2026 whose holders have sought asylum or are now seeking asylum. Officials say the revocations would not necessarily result in immediate deportation, but most of those with pending asylum cases would be recategorized, losing their status as business or tourism travelers. The move is part of a broader effort to crack down on what the administration calls abuse of the immigration system.

The administration has been ramping up restrictions on visa applicants, including requiring more information about social media histories and posting expensive bonds for visa processing. In the past 18 months, the State Department has revoked about 175,000 visas for people convicted or accused of crimes, as well as those who have spoken out publicly against U.S. policies. The revocation of B1 and B2 visas is part of this effort, with officials citing concerns about individuals using temporary visas to later apply for asylum.

Iranian hackers disabled UK power plant for four days: Report

According to a report by the Washington Examiner, hackers affiliated with Iran managed to disable a small-scale power plant in the United Kingdom for four days. The cyberattack is believed to be the first successful attempt at shutting down an energy facility in Britain. British officials stated that the outage had little to no impact on the rest of the nation’s power supply or energy generation. The exact identity of the affected power plant was not disclosed due to security concerns.

FOX News reports that Iranian-linked hackers are suspected of forcing a small British power generator offline for four days last month. UK Energy Minister Michael Shanks described the generator as "tiny" and stated that there was no threat to the wider grid and nobody lost power. He emphasized that the UK has one of the most resilient energy systems in the world.

Where the accounts differ

The Washington Examiner states that the cyberattack is "the most successful of its kind" and that the U.K. agency responsible for monitoring cyberattacks told businesses to prepare for cyber operations after Britain backed the United States in its war against Iran. The Examiner also mentions that the recent U.K. cyberattack occurred around the same time as the hacking campaign against U.S. drinking and wastewater treatment facilities.

FOX News attributes the power outage to Iranian-linked hackers and mentions that British security officials spoke with multiple news outlets. It also reports that the British shutdown came weeks after hackers struck more than 30 community water systems in Minnesota, accessing technology used to remotely monitor and control pumps and other equipment.
