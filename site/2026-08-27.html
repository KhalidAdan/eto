3 Americans among hundreds missing after Nepal flash flooding

A flash flood in Nepal on Wednesday killed at least 72 people and left hundreds missing, including three Americans. The disaster struck the northern Rasuwa district along the border with Tibet around 8:30 a.m. local time. The Nepal Tourism Board reported approximately 384 travelers missing, though many nationalities remain unverified. The U.S. Embassy in Nepal is assisting in locating American citizens, but communication has been disrupted. The flood also affected areas along the Bhote Koshi, Trishuli, and Narayani rivers, with severe flooding reported in several districts.

The flood was triggered by a glacial collapse and debris flow, not an earthquake, as confirmed by the U.S. Geological Survey. The International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development warned of potential secondary flooding due to debris blocking river systems. Search-and-rescue operations are ongoing, with Nepali authorities and international teams working to locate survivors and recover bodies. The U.S. Embassy has advised American tourists in the region to seek higher ground and follow local instructions.

Where the accounts differ

Straight Arrow News reports three Americans are missing, while The Daily Wire states more than 50 Americans are among the missing. Associated Press and The Daily Caller both mention 54 Americans missing, but The Daily Wire attributes this number to the Nepal Tourism Board. The Guardian identifies 34 Australians missing, including children as young as 11, which is not mentioned in other accounts. The Daily Caller and Associated Press differ in the number of missing people, with The Daily Caller citing 384 missing and Associated Press reporting over 1,300 missing. The Guardian also notes that nearly 1,500 people are missing, a figure not mentioned elsewhere.

Sources Straight Arrow News · Associated Press · BBC · The Daily Wire · The Daily Caller · The Guardian

Canada Is Spending Billions to Help Its Workers Survive Our Tariffs

Canadian officials announced a $7.5 billion support package for businesses and workers affected by U.S. tariffs, including $3 million non-repayable loans for small businesses and $2 million interest-free loans. The package also includes $2 billion for medium and large firms, $500 million for retraining, and a one-year extension of temporary employment insurance. The measures come after U.S.-Canada trade negotiations collapsed, with the U.S. imposing 50% tariffs on $20 billion in Canadian imports. Canada retaliated with dollar-for-dollar tariffs, which will take effect on September 8.

Canada’s retaliatory tariffs, which include products like steel, aluminum, and dairy, are designed to match U.S. levies. The Canadian government also emphasized the need for a "right attitude" from the U.S. in future negotiations, with Prime Minister Mark Carney stating Canada will return to talks if the U.S. shows true partnership.

Where the accounts differ

Mother Jones and BBC both report Canada’s $7.5 billion support package, but Mother Jones attributes the characterization of the U.S. tariffs as "unreasonable" to the White House, while BBC attributes it to Trump. Axios and UPI both mention Trump’s threat to rename Lake Ontario to "Lake America," but UPI adds that Trump claimed Canada has created a $60 billion deficit with the U.S. Straight Arrow News notes that economists warn of broader economic impacts, including inflation and supply chain disruptions, which are not mentioned in other accounts. Slate mentions Ford’s brother Rob, a detail not included in other accounts.

Sources Mother Jones · Slate · Axios · BBC · UPI · Straight Arrow News

Trump threatens Iran’s partners: How do secondary sanctions work?

The United States has announced new economic sanctions against Iran and threatened countries it trades with, aiming to choke Tehran’s economy. At least 60 entities across the Middle East, Asia and Europe have been targeted in the latest sanctions as part of an economic pressure campaign. The US-Israel war launched on February 28 has already resulted in a spike in oil prices and disruption in global supply chains due to the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz, through which a fifth of global oil and gas previously passed. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the US would target all of Iran’s sources of revenue, including oil, to prevent other countries and companies from doing business with Tehran. He warned that countries must choose between the US and Iran, stressing that the new campaign exposes Tehran’s trade partners to secondary penalties.

The Trump administration has been waging an economic pressure campaign against Iran under the banner of “Operation Economic Fury” since the war began in February. The recent escalation, called “Operation Economic Outcast,” is a follow-up to Trump’s August 19 post where he announced what he called the “most crushing economic operation” against Iran. Bessent said countries that facilitate transactions with Iran will be targeted, and that the US is giving them an opportunity to remedy bad behavior. The threat of secondary sanctions is also why most global banks and financial institutions no longer participate in trade with Russia or Iran.

Where the accounts differ

Al Jazeera and Axios both report that the US has announced new sanctions against Iran and threatened countries that trade with it, but only Axios mentions that the sanctions are part of a broader “economic D-Day” campaign. Christian Science Monitor adds that the US is targeting Iran’s largest economic lifeline in Iraq, which is not mentioned in Al Jazeera or Axios. UPI reports that China has lashed out against the sanctions, which is not mentioned in the other accounts. Mother Jones focuses on the contradiction in Trump’s statements regarding negotiations with Iran, which is not covered in the other accounts. FOX News reports that Iran’s currency has plunged to an all-time low, which is not mentioned in the other accounts.

Sources Al Jazeera · Christian Science Monitor · Mother Jones · UPI · Axios · FOX News

Trump says 'something may come out of' CIA head's unannounced visit to Moscow

CIA Director John Ratcliffe made an unannounced visit to Moscow, arriving on a military plane and departing hours later. The trip was confirmed by the Kremlin, which stated that Ratcliffe met with Russian intelligence officials but did not meet with President Vladimir Putin. Trump described the meeting as "semi-routine" and said Washington "would like to see the war in Ukraine end." The visit occurred amid heightened tensions between the U.S. and Russia, with both sides intensifying military actions against each other.

The U.S. government did not publicly comment on the visit, though some outlets reported that Ukraine was informed in advance and asked to suspend strikes during the trip. The timing of the visit coincided with increased Russian aggression toward NATO countries, including drone and military aircraft flights over Poland, Romania, and Latvia. Analysts suggested the trip could have been a warning to Russia against further attacks on NATO members.

Where the accounts differ

BBC reports that the last comparable meeting between a CIA director and Russian officials occurred in November 2021, when William Burns warned Russia against sending troops into Ukraine. New York Post and Straight Arrow News both mention that the CIA declined to comment, while The Guardian and FOX News note that the White House, CIA, and Pentagon also declined to comment. ABC News states that sources familiar with the trip say Ratcliffe warned Russia not to attack NATO countries, a claim not made by the BBC or Straight Arrow News. The New York Post and FOX News both mention that the trip included a stop in Latvia, while the BBC and Straight Arrow News provide more detail on the flight path and duration.

Sources BBC · New York Post · Straight Arrow News · The Guardian · FOX News · ABC News

Trump claims all mines removed from Strait of Hormuz; Iran and Oman near agreement

President Trump announced that the U.S. Navy has cleared all mines from the main shipping lane of the Strait of Hormuz, calling it a significant move to reduce Iran's leverage over global energy markets. He warned Iran that any new mine placement would result in immediate destruction. The clearing of mines is part of a low-key operation that has been ongoing for several months, allowing more oil tankers to transit the strait. Iran and Oman have been in talks to establish a temporary shipping route through the strait, with both nations expressing hope for a soon-to-be-announced agreement. The U.S. military has been using the southern lane of the strait to move oil tankers, with over 500 ships transiting through that route in the last 30 days.

Iran and Oman have been discussing a framework to create a temporary joint corridor through the strait and clear mines, while also holding technical talks on a permanent navigation route. The U.S. has been blocking Iranian ships from moving through the strait, and Trump's announcement on mine removal has made the U.S. achieve much of its goal in the strait. However, an Omani-Iranian agreement may not be relevant if the U.S. blockade continues to prevent Iranian ships from passing through.

Where the accounts differ

Axios reports that the U.S. Navy has cleared the Traffic Separation Scheme (TSS) — the median lane of the strait — and that Iran had committed to remove the mines as part of a memorandum of understanding (MoU) that unraveled. CBS News states that Iran has not been able to import enough oil due to the U.S. naval blockade, and that an Iranian official criticized the distribution of subsidized fuel. Al Jazeera notes that Iran and Oman have agreed on a temporary route through Iranian territorial waters, which Iran insists will not reopen until the U.S. fulfills its commitments under the MoU. The Guardian highlights the diminishing strategic value of the strait for Iran and the internal debate over its future. BBC reports that Trump claims all mines have been cleared, while Al Jazeera and Axios note that Iran disputes this claim, with Al Jazeera stating that Iran dismissed Trump's claim as an attempt to calm markets.

Sources Axios · Straight Arrow News · CBS News · Al Jazeera · The Guardian · BBC

Final witness in Lindsay Clancy trial says he doesn’t believe she heard a voice during killings

In the Lindsay Clancy trial, an FBI psychiatrist testified that he does not believe Clancy heard a voice ordering her to kill her children. Dr. Gregory Saathoff, who testified in Plymouth, Massachusetts, stated that Clancy’s ability to plan and execute the killings without external direction suggests she knew right from wrong. Prosecutors argue that Clancy, who admitted to killing her children, was not in a psychotic state and acted with intent. The defense claims she suffered from postpartum psychosis, which impaired her judgment. Clancy, who is paralyzed from the waist down after a suicide attempt, faces a mandatory life sentence if found guilty. The trial has drawn national attention, with supporters of Clancy arguing she was a victim of systemic failures in maternal healthcare.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian and CBS News both report that Dr. Gregory Saathoff testified that Clancy’s actions indicate she knew right from wrong. The Guardian adds that Saathoff found it unusual that Clancy said the voice she heard stopped once the killings were complete. CBS News notes that Saathoff last treated a woman with postpartum psychosis in the 1990s. The Federalist and FOX News describe the public support for Clancy, with the Federalist noting a rally with women in pink shirts and FOX News highlighting the jury composition. The Daily Wire and Mother Jones focus on the public reaction and debate over the trial, with the Daily Wire criticizing the support for Clancy as repulsive and Mother Jones questioning whether the trial was necessary.

Sources The Guardian · The Daily Wire · The Federalist · Mother Jones · FOX News · CBS News

ICE agent charged with shooting in Minnesota could be released from Texas jail

An ICE agent, Christian Castro, is facing charges in Minnesota for allegedly shooting through a house door and wounding a man in his leg. Minnesota authorities have sought his extradition from Texas, where he has been held since May. Texas law mandates his release after 90 days of detention, which is set for Thursday. A federal judge has ruled against Minnesota’s request to block his release, allowing the possibility of his release on Thursday. Minnesota officials argue that Castro could flee to Mexico, citing jail calls where he allegedly discussed plans to marry a woman there and buy property.

Where the accounts differ

The Associated Press and The Daily Wire both mention that Castro allegedly lied about being attacked by the victim and another man, but The Guardian and The Daily Wire add that the Department of Homeland Security initially claimed the victim tried to resist and attack an officer. The New York Post and The Guardian note that Minnesota Attorney General Keith Ellison accused Texas Governor Greg Abbott of turning Texas into a sanctuary state for violent criminals, a claim not mentioned in the Associated Press or The Daily Wire. The Guardian and The Daily Wire state that Abbott cited ongoing investigations of fraud in government social service programs as a reason for not responding to the extradition request, while the Associated Press and The Daily Wire do not mention this specific justification.

Sources Associated Press · Just The News · FOX News · New York Post · The Guardian · The Daily Wire

Hospital nursery fire kills 14 newborns in Pakistan's capital, officials order immediate inquiry

At least 14 newborn babies die in fire at Pakistan hospital’s maternity ward

A fire broke out in the maternity ward of the Pakistan Institute of Medical Sciences in Islamabad, resulting in the deaths of at least 14 newborn babies. The blaze started in the early hours of Wednesday and engulfed the ward, which housed at least 15 newborns. Hospital officials confirmed that one baby was rescued, while the rest perished. The fire was attributed to an exploding air conditioning unit, though some accounts suggest a nebulizer may have been the initial cause. Rescue teams quickly arrived at the scene, and authorities have launched an investigation into the incident. The prime minister, Shehbaz Sharif, ordered an immediate inquiry and expressed sorrow over the tragedy.

Witnesses described scenes of panic as thick smoke filled the ward. Families were forced to escape through windows and ladders, with some unable to reach their children. A father who had just given birth to a daughter recounted searching for his wife and child amid the chaos, while another father claimed the nursery door was locked and no staff were present to unlock it. The incident has sparked widespread grief and calls for accountability across Pakistan.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian and Associated Press (AP) report that the fire was caused by an exploding air conditioning unit, while AP also mentions a nebulizer as a possible cause. The Guardian and FOX News both cite Dr. Aneeza Jalil as the hospital spokesperson who stated that one baby was rescued, but FOX News adds that six of the deceased infants' bodies had been given over to families, while the remaining bodies would be given over soon. The Guardian and AP mention that the health minister, Mustafa Kamal, stated that many of the babies were in incubators or on oxygen, and that the oxygen canisters made the fire worse, a detail not included in the other accounts. The Guardian also includes a quote from Sardar Atti ur Rehman, a witness, describing the chaos and his attempt to help a mother, which is not present in the other accounts.

Sources The Guardian · NPR · Associated Press · FOX News
