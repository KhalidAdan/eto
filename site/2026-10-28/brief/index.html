Lula Wins Fourth Term in Brazil, Defeating Bolsonaro Son

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, Brazil’s leftist president, won a fourth term in a runoff election, defeating Flávio Bolsonaro, the son of former president Jair Bolsonaro. Lula secured a victory by over 4 million votes, marking a significant setback for the far right in Latin America. Flávio, who ran as a proxy for his father, refused to concede and called for the election to be annulled. Lula’s win ended a wave of right-wing victories in the region, with seven conservative presidents elected in the past 18 months. The election was marred by claims of fraud, with Trump and his allies suggesting the results were rigged, though no evidence was presented.

The election also saw a two-hour extension of voting, which some, including Flávio’s team, claimed was election interference. However, both Lula’s and Flávio’s teams had requested the extension due to logistical concerns. Despite the claims, Lula’s victory was widely accepted, with large celebrations across Brazil. The result has been seen as a major defeat for the global right, with Lula’s win signaling a shift back toward leftist governance in the region.

Where the accounts differ

Slate and The Nation both report that Lula won by over 4 million votes, but The Nation specifies the final tally as 51.79 to 48.21 percent. Axios states Lula won by more than 4 million votes, while The Guardian and NPR do not specify the exact margin. The Nation and Axios mention Flávio’s refusal to concede, while The Guardian and NPR do not. The Nation and Axios note that Flávio’s team requested the voting extension, while Slate and The Guardian do not. The Nation and Axios also mention coordinated attempts to sabotage Lula’s campaign, which are not detailed in other accounts. The New York Post and Axios report Trump’s comments about the election being rigged, while The Nation and Slate do not mention Trump’s direct involvement in the claims.

Sources Slate · The Nation · Axios · New York Post · NPR · The Guardian

Federal grand jury declines to indict Cassidy Hutchinson

A federal grand jury in Washington, D.C., rejected the Justice Department’s attempt to indict Cassidy Hutchinson over her testimony to Congress about President Trump’s actions during the Jan. 6, 2021, Capitol attack. The decision followed a request by the DOJ’s Civil Rights Division, led by Assistant Attorney General Harmeet Dhillon. Justice Department attorney Neville Hedley informed a magistrate judge that the grand jury had not reached a consensus on the charges, though sources confirmed the reference was to Hutchinson. Hutchinson, a former aide to Trump’s chief of staff Mark Meadows, testified in 2022 about Trump’s alleged knowledge of armed supporters and his behavior during the riot. She described Trump attempting to grab the steering wheel of his presidential vehicle after being denied access to the Capitol. Her account was disputed by Secret Service personnel, and Trump and his allies have accused her of lying to Congress. The failure to secure an indictment marks another setback for the DOJ’s efforts to prosecute Trump’s political adversaries.

Where the accounts differ

The Hill and The Guardian both state that the grand jury rejected the indictment, while The Washington Examiner and New York Post describe the same outcome. The Washington Examiner mentions that the grand jury typically consists of 16 to 23 members, a detail not included in other accounts. The New York Post and The Guardian note that the DOJ’s Civil Rights Division, not the usual U.S. Attorney’s Office, handled the case, a point also made by Axios. The New York Post and The Guardian attribute the claim about the grand jury’s size and the handling of the case to unnamed sources, while The Hill and Axios attribute the same information to specific outlets. The Washington Examiner and The Guardian both mention that Hutchinson’s testimony was seen as particularly compelling due to her proximity to power, a detail not included in The Hill or Axios. The New York Post and The Guardian also note that Hutchinson’s account was disputed by Secret Service personnel, a point not explicitly made by The Hill or Axios.

Sources The Hill · Washington Examiner · New York Post · Axios · The Guardian · Just The News

Trump administration asks Washington to set aside hundreds of ballots from suspected noncitizen voters

The Trump administration has requested Washington state to set aside ballots from more than 400 individuals it alleges are registered to vote without U.S. citizenship. The justice department claims it has identified 1,500 votes cast by these individuals in prior elections and is asking the state to set aside any ballots they might cast in the upcoming midterm elections until their eligibility is confirmed. Washington Secretary of State Steve Hobbs is reviewing the request, noting that the timing of the demand, one week before the general election, is likely intended to cast unfounded suspicion on the results. The administration also seeks information including addresses, dates of birth, and social security numbers to verify the voters’ status.

In California, a federal appeals court rejected the Trump administration’s emergency appeal to set aside mail ballots from 20,000 people it suspects are noncitizens. The 2-1 ruling stated the appeals court lacked jurisdiction and the case should return to the lower court. Meanwhile, the DOJ has also filed a lawsuit against Alaska, seeking to delay certification of the vote if the Republican incumbent does not win. California officials have disputed the list, noting that a preliminary analysis of 200 names included many individuals who appear to be U.S. citizens.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian reports that the Trump administration asked Washington to set aside ballots from more than 400 suspected noncitizens, with 1,500 votes cast by them in prior elections. The Guardian also notes that the state has over 5 million registered voters and that the administration’s request is part of a broader effort to undermine election confidence. The Hill and Washington Examiner both report that the appeals court rejected the DOJ’s emergency appeal in California, with the Hill providing more detail on the 2-1 ruling and the dissenting opinion. The Straight Arrow News and Just The News mention the 20,000 California ballots and the DOJ’s request, but the Straight Arrow News adds that the DOJ is also asking Washington to set aside ballots from more than 400 voters. The Guardian and Just The News both mention that Washington officials claim the timing of the request is intended to cast unfounded suspicion on the results, while the Straight Arrow News adds that Washington GOP Chairman Jim Walsh claims the state officials are refusing to cooperate with federal law.

Trump says he's ending restrictions on climate-warming methane from oil drilling

President Donald Trump announced during a campaign rally in Lubbock, Texas, that his administration is ending a Biden-era rule aimed at reducing methane emissions from oil and gas drilling. The rule, finalized in 2024, required oil and gas operators to eliminate and minimize leaking, flaring, and venting of methane. Trump claimed the move would save consumers and energy producers $45 billion and unlock 210 million barrels of oil. Environmental Protection Agency Administrator Lee Zeldin previewed the changes last month, stating the agency would eliminate the "Super Emitter Program" and set less restrictive standards for small oil and gas wells. The Biden rule was projected to prevent 58 million tons of methane emissions by 2038 and yield $98 billion in benefits, though Trump's EPA rejected the method used to calculate those benefits.

The rollback comes as part of Trump's broader effort to boost domestic energy production amid high fuel prices and the U.S. war with Iran. Environmental groups criticized the move, arguing it would allow companies to continue polluting with methane that could be captured and used for energy. The Associated Press reported that the Biden methane rule was announced during a 2023 U.N. climate conference and applied to existing wells, requiring operators to plug leaking methane from smaller wells that account for up to half of all methane emissions from oil and gas fields.

Where the accounts differ

NPR and The Guardian both report that the Biden methane rule was finalized in 2024 and that it required operators to plug leaking methane from smaller wells, which account for up to half of all methane emissions. However, The Guardian adds that the rule was the first time the federal government set nationwide requirements for existing facilities, while NPR does not mention this. The Hill and Just The News both state that the rule was projected to prevent 58 million tons of methane emissions by 2038, but The Hill also notes that the Biden administration calculated these benefits using the "social costs" of greenhouse gas emissions, a method rejected by Trump's EPA. Fox Business and The Guardian both cite the $45 billion savings figure, but Fox Business also mentions that the EPA's Zeldin said the rule would have cost $18 billion, a claim not included in other accounts.

Sources NPR · Washington Examiner · Fox Business · The Hill · The Guardian · Just The News

Hospital beds for the nation's sickest kids are disappearing

CBS News reports that hospitals across the U.S. have closed pediatric intensive care units in Montana, Oregon, Missouri, Illinois, and California. These units, which serve children from a few weeks old to early adulthood, are being converted into services for newborns or adults. The closures have led to a 30% decrease in pediatric inpatient units from 2008 to 2022, with three-quarters of hospitals now lacking any pediatric inpatient services. The American Academy of Pediatrics warns that the gap in care is especially acute in rural areas, where hospitals are often owned by small nonprofits or private equity companies. A pediatric critical care doctor, Elizabeth Mack, attributes the closures to financial pressures, including lower reimbursement rates for children and a shortage of pediatric subspecialists.

Newsweek highlights the broader challenges facing rural and regional hospitals, noting that over 40% operate at a financial loss and 150 have closed or converted since 2010. The article cites the American Hospital Association's October 2026 report, which includes data on the loss of obstetric services and chemotherapy. Newsweek emphasizes the role of regional hospitals as economic anchors and emergency resources, with examples like Sandhills Medical in South Carolina, which serves a largely low-income and uninsured population. The article also mentions the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which affects Medicaid and insurance coverage, adding to the strain on rural hospitals.

Where the accounts differ

CBS News focuses specifically on the closure of pediatric intensive care units and the impact on children's care, citing the American Academy of Pediatrics and a pediatric critical care doctor, Elizabeth Mack. It does not mention the broader financial struggles of rural hospitals or the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Newsweek, on the other hand, provides a broader context about the financial challenges of rural and regional hospitals, including the loss of obstetric services and chemotherapy, and references the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. CBS News does not mention the role of regional hospitals as economic anchors or the impact of federal policy changes on Medicaid and insurance coverage.

Sources CBS News · Newsweek
