Inside the Fight to Bring Jensen Huang Back to Nvidia

Jensen Huang, former CEO of Nvidia, resigned last week over multiple extramarital affairs, including one involving Alice Huang, a woman identified by US intelligence as a possible spy for the Chinese government. A campaign called “Bring Jensen Back” has gathered nearly 180,000 signatures, urging Nvidia’s board to reinstate Huang while an independent investigation establishes the facts. The campaign includes signatories such as Elon Musk and Steve Chen, along with current and former Nvidia employees. The FBI has been investigating Huang’s affair with Alice Huang for at least a year. Nvidia’s interim CEO, Colette Kress, has been managing the company’s shift to AI-focused chipmaking. President Trump has expressed support for Huang’s potential return, stating he “would not mind” if Huang were reinstated as CEO.

The campaign argues that Huang deserves due process and fairness, emphasizing his long-standing contributions to the company. It claims that the company’s future is not the main concern of those pushing for Huang’s return, but rather a sense of appreciation for his work. The campaign states that Huang “poured his heart and soul into building NVIDIA” and that the company “is the work of his life.” The campaign also highlights that the tech industry is “primarily led by men, designed by men, and is far more likely to be used by men.”

Where the accounts differ

Mother Jones reports that the FBI has been investigating Huang’s affair with Alice Huang for at least a year, while The Daily Wire states that the FBI investigation began after the affair was revealed. The Daily Wire also claims that Alice Huang was a former television journalist who later worked at Megaspeed, a detail not mentioned in Mother Jones. The Daily Wire attributes the claim that Megaspeed purchased over $4 billion in Nvidia hardware to its own reporting, while Mother Jones does not specify the amount. The Free Press does not mention the affair or the campaign to reinstate Huang, focusing instead on Trump’s meeting with Xi and Putin.

Sources Mother Jones · The Hill · FOX News · The Daily Wire · BBC · The Free Press

Trump says Alaska voters chose the wrong Dan Sullivan. What does the data show?

Democratic candidate Mary Peltola has won the US Senate race in Alaska, beating Republican incumbent Senator Dan S Sullivan to complete the Democrats' sweeping victory in the midterm elections. Peltola sealed the win after reaching 50.1% with 99% of votes counted - making Alaska the last Senate seat to be called after the 3 November election. Alaska uses a ranked-choice voting system, which means voters can vote for five candidates in order of preference. If no candidate reaches more than 50% of the first preference votes, then voters' other choices are used to decide who wins.

The race was complicated by the presence of two candidates named Dan Sullivan. Republican Dan S Sullivan has held the US Senate seat in Alaska since 2015 and was running for his third term. Daniel J Sullivan Jr - a retired school teacher from a remote fishing village in Alaska - entered the race in May and has run a low-key campaign. The New York Times reported that Democratic operatives with links to the Peltola campaign had tried to recruit "spoiler" candidates in the Senate race, including Daniel J Sullivan Jr. Peltola has denied all knowledge of the alleged "scheme" and Sullivan Jr has denied any links with the Peltola campaign. President Trump has posted multiple times on Truth Social since the election, claiming that the race has been "rigged".

Where the accounts differ

BBC reports that over a third of voters who put Daniel J Sullivan Jr first left their second choice blank, while NPR states that Peltola won a majority of Alaskans' first-place votes, meaning no ranked-choice calculation was needed. Al Jazeera mentions that the Trump administration alleged that Peltola’s campaign had deliberately recruited the second Dan Sullivan to divide Republican votes, while ABC News states that the Department of Justice preemptively sued to block the certification of the election if Dan S Sullivan lost. Axios highlights that Peltola's victory was driven by support in four Alaska Native strongholds, while Newsweek notes that James Comer claimed Peltola could still face legal challenges over her victory.

Sources BBC · NPR · Al Jazeera · ABC News · Axios · Newsweek

Chuck Schumer Faces Leadership Challenge from Chris Van Hollen

Chuck Schumer, the Senate Democratic leader, faced criticism for his election night speech following Democrats' midterm victories, which some argued failed to address key issues like the Iran War and tech influence. His remarks were seen as out of touch with current concerns, prompting calls for leadership change. Senate Minority Leader Chris Van Hollen, a progressive Democrat from Maryland, has emerged as a challenger to Schumer's leadership, arguing that the party needs a new direction. Van Hollen emphasized the need for more inclusive decision-making and highlighted the importance of addressing issues like the war in Iran and military aid to Israel. He also pointed to the success of progressive candidates like Abdul El-Sayed and Peggy Flanagan as evidence of a shift in Democratic priorities.

Van Hollen's campaign for leadership has gained traction, with him appearing on major networks to outline his vision for the party. He claims the midterm elections were a populist rejection of Trump, not a Washington-driven victory. Van Hollen also called for an end to U.S. funding for the Iran War and criticized Schumer's continued support for military aid to Israel despite his criticism of Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu.

Where the accounts differ

The Nation reports that Schumer's speech omitted key issues like the Iran War and tech influence, while NPR and ABC News do not mention this omission. The Nation attributes Schumer's criticism of Republicans to a focus on "extraneous things" and "costs, chaos, and corruption," whereas NPR and ABC News do not include this specific phrasing. The Nation also notes that Schumer's metaphor about the "red brick wall" was confusing, a detail not present in NPR or ABC News. NPR and ABC News do not mention the Nation's characterization of Schumer as a "political dinosaur."

US intel detects Iranian dirty bomb plot targeting Washington: reports

U.S. intelligence officials have reportedly detected a plot by Iran to detonate a dirty bomb in Washington, D.C., according to multiple outlets. The intelligence includes specific locations and potential targets, prompting contingency planning by American officials. White House spokesperson Lauren Bis stated that there are "no credible threats to the homeland" at this time. President Trump has said the administration is taking the report "very seriously" and warned Iran would face "unprecedented retaliation" if it attacked the U.S. The intelligence reportedly includes intercepted Iranian communications suggesting bomb-making materials had been stashed inside the U.S. However, officials remain divided over the credibility of these claims and whether Iran has the personnel to execute such an attack.

Iran has denied the allegations, with its Foreign Ministry calling the claims "baseless." The report also mentions that Iran has previously been linked to plots targeting Americans on U.S. soil, including a 2011 plot to assassinate Saudi Arabia’s ambassador and a 2024 plot to assassinate Trump. The intelligence suggests that radioactive material or chemical agents needed for the dirty bomb may have been stashed in the U.S. for years, and that Iran could have used the "porous" border to bring in operatives. The White House has reportedly held several meetings to discuss potential countermeasures, though no public confirmation exists that Iran has positioned materials or operatives for an attack.

Where the accounts differ

The New York Post and The Daily Wire both mention that U.S. officials have been meeting to discuss the intelligence, but The Daily Wire adds that the White House pushed back on the assessment, calling the reported planning "aspirational." The Washington Examiner notes that Secretary of State Marco Rubio dismissed the reports as not indicating an "imminent threat," while the New York Post states that officials are "divided over how credible the intelligence is." The Daily Wire and FOX News both include quotes from Iran’s Foreign Ministry denying the allegations, but FOX News adds that Iran’s Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi invoked Iran’s history as a victim of chemical warfare, which is not mentioned in the other accounts. The Daily Wire also includes details about the Secret Service moving Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent into military housing, which is not mentioned in the other accounts.

Sources New York Post · The Daily Wire · BBC · FOX News · Axios · Washington Examiner

White House withdraws Heidi Overton’s nomination to become next FDA commissioner

The White House has withdrawn Heidi Overton’s nomination to become the next Food and Drug Administration commissioner before the Senate Health, Education, Labor and Pensions Committee was due to consider her on Wednesday. Overton, a physician and deputy director of the White House Domestic Policy Council, was nominated by President Trump in August. The decision comes the day before the Senate's Health, Education, Labor and Pensions Committee was scheduled to vote on her nomination. The FDA has been led by acting commissioner Kyle Diamantas since Marty Makary resigned in May.

Overton faced skepticism from some Republicans over her role in the administration's efforts to change childhood vaccine recommendations. A senior administration official told Axios that Overton did not have enough support to be confirmed as the next FDA commissioner. The official stated, "We need to go in a new direction at FDA, and everybody fully supports [Health] Secretary [Robert F.] Kennedy Jr.'s vision to Make America Healthy Again and get back to a more populist and common-sense approach to health in America."

Where the accounts differ

The Washington Examiner and Just The News both state that the White House pulled the nomination due to lack of congressional support, while the New York Post and Axios attribute the withdrawal to bipartisan pushback and criticism over Overton's role in changing childhood vaccine recommendations. The New York Post and FOX News mention Overton's alleged "big tobacco ties" and anti-abortion stance, which are not included in the Washington Examiner or Just The News accounts. The FOX News account also includes details about Overton's confirmation hearing and her clashes with Senator Bernie Sanders and Senator Bill Cassidy, which are not present in the other accounts. The CBS News account adds information about Overton's testimony regarding the FDA-approved chemical abortion drug mifepristone and her execution of Trump's pro-vaping policies, which are not mentioned in the other accounts.

New York City Mayor Disbands NYPD Counterterrorism Unit Amid Protests and Civilian Oversight Plans

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani announced Monday that he would dissolve the NYPD’s Strategic Response Group (SRG), a unit responsible for counterterrorism and managing protest crowds. The SRG was formed in 2015. Mamdani stated that the move fulfills a campaign promise to separate counterterrorism from protest response. The city plans to replace the SRG with a civilian-led “Community Dialogue and De-escalation Division” and a “First Amendment Support Team,” both of which will lack weapons and arrest powers. The NYPD will also create a new Patrol Support Division with officers trained in crowd management. The reforms include banning irritant gas, kettling, and long guns during protests. The NYPD’s intelligence and counterterrorism functions will be consolidated within the department’s intelligence bureau.

The New York Post reported that the SRG was involved in incidents such as the June 2020 kettling of protesters, the Gracie Mansion bombing attempt, and the NFL Headquarters mass shooting. The Guardian noted that the SRG has faced criticism for using excessive force and aggressive tactics against protesters, and that the reforms include significant restrictions on police tactics during demonstrations.

Where the accounts differ

The New York Post and The Guardian differ in the specific incidents cited involving the SRG. The New York Post mentions the June 2020 kettling incident, the Gracie Mansion bombing attempt, and the NFL Headquarters mass shooting, while The Guardian does not include these specific events. The Guardian also notes that the SRG has faced criticism for using excessive force, while The New York Post attributes this criticism to civil rights groups. The Free Press and FOX News do not provide specific incidents, focusing instead on the broader implications of the SRG’s dissolution. The Daily Wire and The Daily Caller attribute the SRG’s role in preventing violence to its counterterrorism capabilities, while The Guardian and The New York Post emphasize the unit’s involvement in protest management.

Pakistani man sentenced to 27 years for Iran-backed plot to kill Trump

A Pakistani man, Asif Merchant, was sentenced to 27 years in prison for his role in an Iran-backed plot to assassinate former U.S. President Donald Trump. Merchant was found guilty in March of attempting to commit an act of terrorism transcending national boundaries and murder for hire. The plot involved hiring hitmen to kill Trump, former President Joe Biden, and former U.S. ambassador to the United Nations Nikki Haley. The hitmen were undercover FBI agents. Merchant claimed he acted under the direction of Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) to avenge the 2020 U.S. airstrike that killed Iranian general Qasem Soleimani. He was arrested in July 2024 in Texas as he prepared to leave the U.S. The sentence was handed down by U.S. District Judge Eric R. Komitee.

Prosecutors stated that Merchant traveled to Iran 15 times, where he received training from Iranian intelligence officers, before coming to the U.S. in April 2024. He paid $5,000 to the undercover hitmen as an initial payment. The Department of Justice described the IRGC as a global terrorist force. Merchant admitted to being recruited by the IRGC in 2022 and said he was forced into the plot to protect his family in Iran.

Where the accounts differ

BBC and The Guardian both report that Merchant was arrested in July 2024 in Texas, while The Daily Wire and Just The News state he was arrested on July 12, 2024. CBS News mentions that Merchant was arrested on July 12, 2024, the day before Thomas Matthew Crooks shot Trump during a campaign rally in Butler, Pennsylvania. The Daily Wire and The Guardian note that Merchant had searched for Trump rally locations and used Google Maps to find a rally in New Jersey, while BBC and CBS News do not mention this detail. The Daily Wire and The Guardian also state that Merchant had social media posts praising Soleimani, which BBC and CBS News do not mention. The Washington Examiner does not mention the arrest date or the Google Maps activity.

Clarence Thomas calls foul on California ruling he says could turn citizens into government mouthpieces

The U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear a challenge to California’s requirement that licensed physicians complete continuing medical education (CME) courses including discussion of implicit bias. The case involves Dr. Azadeh Khatibi, a physician who argues the state compels her to convey a government-favored message in violation of the First Amendment. California maintains that while instructors must include discussion of implicit bias, they are not required to personally endorse the theory and may openly disagree with it. The Ninth Circuit previously ruled that CME courses constitute government speech due to California’s extensive regulation of the medical profession, meaning the state’s requirements are not subject to First Amendment scrutiny.

Justice Clarence Thomas, joined by Justice Samuel Alito, dissented from the Supreme Court’s decision not to take up the case. They argued that California’s implicit-bias-training requirement compels instructors to affirm the state’s view that implicit bias causes disparities in health outcomes, thereby violating First Amendment protections. Thomas warned that allowing the law to stand could have implications beyond the medical field, potentially enabling states to compel speech from private citizens.

Where the accounts differ

The New York Post and FOX News both report that Thomas and Alito dissented from the Supreme Court’s decision not to take up the case, and that they argued California’s law compels private speakers to communicate the government’s viewpoint. The New York Post adds that Thomas and Alito said the Ninth Circuit erred in finding that the CME requirement is government speech. FOX News omits this specific critique and instead reports that Thomas and Alito disputed California’s characterization of whether it was compelling instructors to take certain positions. The Axios and ABC News accounts do not mention the dissenting justices’ specific arguments about the government-speech doctrine, focusing instead on the broader context of the Supreme Court’s decision on DEI-related issues.

Sources New York Post · Axios · ABC News · FOX News

Fears grow over US sanctions on ICJ judges ahead of Myanmar genocide ruling

The International Court of Justice (ICJ) is set to rule on whether Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya. The US has warned that it may impose sanctions on ICJ judges, including Nobel peace prize winner Navi Pillay, if the court finds Myanmar guilty. The US and Israel fear the ruling could set a precedent that makes it easier to accuse other states of genocide. Lawyers for Rohingya survivors argue that Myanmar’s military actions in 2016 and 2017 show clear intent to destroy the Rohingya group. The US has already sanctioned ICC judges and called the ICC a “rogue court.” The Gambia, as a litigant, appointed Pillay as an ad hoc judge in the case against Myanmar.

Rohingya refugees, including Dil Hossain and his family, have fled Myanmar and now live in refugee camps in Bangladesh. They hope the ICJ ruling will recognize the atrocities against the Rohingya and support their return home. Over 1.2 million Rohingya remain in Bangladesh, many in camps in Cox’s Bazar. Despite the legal case, the prospects for a safe return are uncertain due to ongoing conflict in Myanmar. Rohingya refugees describe life in exile as marked by hardship, dependence on aid, and mental distress.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian reports that the US and Israel fear the ICJ ruling could set a precedent for accusing other states of genocide, including Israel in Gaza. It also mentions that the US has sanctioned ICC judges and called the ICC a “rogue court.” The Guardian attributes the claim about the US warning judges to Mike Waltz, the US ambassador to the UN, and quotes him directly. Al Jazeera does not mention the US-Israel concern or the sanctions against the ICC. Al Jazeera focuses on the personal stories of Rohingya refugees and their hopes for justice, while The Guardian emphasizes the political and legal implications of the ruling.

Sources The Guardian · Al Jazeera
