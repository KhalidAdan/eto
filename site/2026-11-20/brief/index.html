Elon Musk claims ‘I’m not Maga’ and says he regrets vast sums to back Republicans

Elon Musk denied being associated with the "Make America Great Again" (MAGA) movement during a virtual appearance at the New York Times Dealbook Summit. He admitted to spending nearly $500 million on Republican candidates in the 2026 midterms, which he called a "failed" effort. Musk also expressed regret over his financial support for Trump's re-election campaign in 2024, which cost him at least $250 million. He stated that he felt "pretty foolish" about the amount spent, arguing that campaign cash alone cannot determine election outcomes. Musk also distanced himself from the Republican Party's base, saying he does not share "blind adherence to a set of policies by the Republican party."

Musk, who previously described himself as "dark, gothic MAGA" at a 2024 Trump rally, now claims he is "not MAGA." He also criticized Ukraine’s president, Volodymyr Zelenskyy, for allegedly lying about restrictions on his Starshield satellite system. Musk further stated that his interactions with Anthropic were positive, calling the AI firm "good people" and "principled." He also mentioned that he had served for 180 days as head of the Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE) under Trump’s administration before leaving due to a public falling-out.

Army delays detonation in Iowa that could send toxic dust into Illinois

The U.S. Army has postponed plans to detonate unstable explosives at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa, after public and environmental concerns. Originally scheduled for Friday, November 20, the detonation was delayed to Monday, November 23, to avoid a forecasted temperature drop that could destabilize the material. The operation involves 68 barrels of lead azide, a highly sensitive explosive used in military ammunition. The Army claims the material has deteriorated during storage, becoming increasingly sensitive to friction and shock. Environmental groups and local officials have raised concerns about the potential for toxic lead dust to spread across parts of Iowa and Illinois, affecting farmland, playgrounds, and residential areas. The Army has advised residents in the Middletown City Park neighborhood to temporarily leave the area during the detonation and recommends staying indoors with doors and windows closed to minimize exposure.

Beloved hippo Fiona dies days after swallowing hat

Fiona, a Nile hippopotamus, died at the Cincinnati Zoo after swallowing a knit hat that was accidentally flung into her enclosure. The zoo confirmed the death on Thursday, stating that despite efforts by veterinary and animal care teams, Fiona could not recover. The hat was tossed into the air by a visitor and caught on a ceiling fan, which launched it into Fiona’s habitat. The zoo attributed the incident to an accident. Fiona, who was born prematurely in 2017, had become a global sensation due to her survival and popularity. Her death has prompted widespread mourning from zoo staff, visitors, and public figures.

The Cincinnati Zoo's care team attempted various methods to help Fiona pass the hat, including using a vibration plate borrowed from a horse farm. Veterinarians also performed an ultrasound and a colonoscopy to assess Fiona's condition. Despite these efforts, Fiona's body could not fight the blockage, and she ultimately passed away. The zoo emphasized that surgery was not an option for a full-grown hippo due to the animal's size and thick skin.

Myanmar becomes first state found responsible for genocide by UN court

The International Court of Justice (ICJ) ruled Thursday that Myanmar committed genocide against its Rohingya Muslim minority during a 2017 military crackdown. The court found that the violence was intended to destroy the Rohingya population, resulting in thousands of deaths and the displacement of over 740,000 people to Bangladesh. The ruling, which was 9–6, marks the first time a state has been held responsible for genocide by the ICJ. The court ordered Myanmar to investigate and punish those responsible, provide reparations to the Rohingya, and allow refugees to return home. Myanmar has consistently denied allegations of mass rape, murder, and arson.

The court also acknowledged the ongoing conflict in Myanmar, which began with a 2021 military coup and has led to over 100,000 deaths and the displacement of 5.2 million people. The ruling could have significant implications for other genocide cases, including South Africa's case against Israel over alleged actions in Gaza.

Where the accounts differ

NPR and Christian Science Monitor also describe the ruling as a finding of genocide. Al Jazeera and BBC note that the case was brought by The Gambia on behalf of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, while Axios and The Guardian attribute the case to The Gambia directly. Al Jazeera and BBC mention the 2021 military coup and its impact, which Axios and The Guardian do not include. Nvidia halts China AI chip shipments amid CEO scandal and national security concerns

Nvidia has stopped shipping certain advanced AI chips to China, according to reports. The company's interim CEO, Colette Kress, stated that it is no longer shipping licensed Hopper data center products to China. This decision comes after the resignation of former CEO Jensen Huang, who stepped down amid allegations of extramarital affairs, including one with an alleged Chinese spy. The U.S. government had previously approved exports of certain Nvidia chips to China under specific conditions, but the company has now excluded Chinese data center sales from its revenue forecasts. The move has raised questions about the company's ability to serve the Chinese market despite changes to U.S. export rules.

The scandal has drawn attention to the broader implications of AI chip exports for national security. The Biden administration had imposed strict restrictions on the sale of advanced AI chips to China, but the Trump administration eased these restrictions, allowing for the export of certain Nvidia processors. This policy shift followed discussions between Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping, and efforts by Huang to expand Nvidia's access to China. However, Beijing has also imposed its own restrictions on H200 imports, seeking to reduce its reliance on American semiconductor technology.

California has launched an investigation into whether Nvidia's exports of advanced chips ended up in China, violating U.S. restrictions. The state's attorney general, Rob Bonta, issued a subpoena to Nvidia, citing concerns about potential misleading investors and the transfer of confidential information to China. The investigation also looks into whether former CEO Huang's actions may have contributed to the unauthorized transfer of advanced chips or sensitive data.
