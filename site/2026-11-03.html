Haley Stevens backs Mike Rogers. And, Gulf states brace for potential attack on Iran

Democratic Rep. Haley Stevens stunned the political landscape by endorsing GOP Michigan Senate candidate Mike Rogers for Michigan's open Senate seat, breaking with her party less than 48 hours before Election Day. The surprise defection followed weeks of deteriorating relations with her former primary opponent, Democratic nominee Abdul El-Sayed. In her endorsement of Rogers, Stevens cited El-Sayed's ties to political streamer Hasan Piker, whose remarks concerning Jewish people and Israel sparked backlash in August. El-Sayed sought to distance himself from the controversy, but Stevens has criticized him for not doing enough to reassure Black and Jewish voters who backed her in the primary.

The U.S. and its Gulf partners are on edge as tensions with Iran escalate. Qatar has been actively leading negotiations with Iran over the past 72 hours to prevent further fighting. Iran says there's a two-day plan to stop attacks on ships in the Strait of Hormuz and resume talks with the U.S., depending on the U.S. response. Trump said that the U.S. has neutralized key components of Iran's military but has a couple of targets left, according to NPR's Aya Batrawy. Gulf states worry that further attacks will destabilize the region and disrupt global energy supplies.

Where the accounts differ

NPR reports that Stevens cited El-Sayed's ties to Hasan Piker, while ABC News states that Stevens' endorsement came after months of tension between her and El-Sayed since he defeated her in the Democratic primary. The Guardian and Axios do not mention the Piker connection. ABC News also notes that El-Sayed's campaign reported a record fundraising haul following the endorsement, which is not mentioned in other accounts. Mother Jones describes the endorsement as a "Democratic Betrayal" and highlights the personal vendetta angle, which is not present in other accounts. The Guardian and Axios do not mention the Piker connection or the fundraising surge.

Sources NPR · Axios · The Guardian · Mother Jones · Al Jazeera · ABC News

Jet may have broken apart before Florida crash, officials say, as family named among victims

A private jet crashed in Florida on Sunday, killing all nine people on board. The Learjet 60, en route from Austin, Texas, to the Bahamas, nosedived into a multi-lane highway and exploded on impact. The National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) said parts of the aircraft may have broken off in the air before crashing. The crash occurred near Military Trail in Palm Beach County, leaving a large crater on the road. Heavy rainfall and thunderstorms were reported in the area at the time. Emergency crews are working to recover wreckage, which is partially submerged in water due to recent storms. The NTSB is investigating whether a cockpit voice recorder was on board and if it can be recovered. The crash site is near a mobile home park, with debris scattered across nearby businesses and homes.

The NTSB confirmed that the plane was descending at an alarming rate, with air traffic control recordings showing the pilot reporting "moderate turbulence" and "managing the ice." The pilot later declared an emergency, stating the plane was descending at 10,000 feet per minute and later at 20,000 feet per minute. The plane was reported to be flying at 35,000 feet when it began its rapid descent. The crash site was near a residential area, with witnesses describing the impact as catastrophic. One man in a car on the ground sustained a leg injury, but no one on the ground was killed.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian and NPR report that the plane was descending at up to 30,000 feet per minute, while CBS News and Straight Arrow News mention a descent rate exceeding 32,000 feet per minute. The Guardian and NPR also state that the plane was heading east at 35,000 feet and requested to climb another 2,000 feet, while the BBC and CBS News mention the plane was at 35,000 feet when it began its descent. The Guardian and NPR attribute the request to climb to the FAA, while the BBC and CBS News attribute it to the pilot. The Guardian and NPR also mention that the plane banked sharply to the right before crashing, a detail not included in the BBC or CBS News accounts.

Sources BBC · The Guardian · NPR · CBS News · Straight Arrow News · Washington Examiner

Trump’s List of Alleged Noncitizen Voters in California May Be Filled With Errors

The Trump administration’s attempt to block 20,000 “likely noncitizen” voters from casting ballots in California appears to be based on a list with significant inaccuracies. Two data analysts at the University of California, Los Angeles’ Voting Rights Project found that over 40 percent of the people on the Department of Justice’s list were not on the state’s voter rolls. The Justice Department provided the list to California on October 13, requesting validation of each individual’s citizenship status. California declined to assist, prompting the administration to file a lawsuit to prevent what it called “unlawful dilution of American citizens’ votes.”

A federal judge ruled that the Trump administration’s demands to segregate ballots from suspected noncitizens likely violated the Constitution. The ruling, issued on Election Day, barred federal officials from making such demands even as the administration sought Supreme Court intervention. The judge found that the Constitution gives states and Congress, but not the executive branch, authority over election rules. The administration has also threatened prosecution of state and local election officials who process ballots from its list.

Where the accounts differ

Mother Jones and Axios both report that the Justice Department’s list included naturalized citizens who were “highly likely” to have been inaccurately labeled as ineligible. Mother Jones states that the UCLA researchers found nearly 7,500 naturalized citizens were “highly likely” to have been inaccurately labeled, while Axios reports that an expert found at least 22 members of CHIRLA were wrongly included and another 66 were “almost certainly” on the list. The Guardian and The Daily Wire mention that the Justice Department charged 34 people on the list with election-related crimes, while Mother Jones and Axios report that 28 people were charged and 11 arrested. The Guardian also notes that five of the six new defendants were arrested, while The Daily Wire states that 34 people were charged and 28 were arrested.

Sources Mother Jones · Axios · Washington Examiner · The Daily Wire · ABC News · The Guardian

US Conducts Defensive Strikes on Iranian Island Ahead of Midterm Elections

The U.S. military launched defensive strikes on Iran’s Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz on Monday, according to multiple outlets. The attacks were described as a response to Iranian attacks on U.S. assets, with officials stating they were limited in scope. The strikes came on the eve of the U.S. midterm elections, which are expected to have significant implications for the political landscape. President Donald Trump has been under pressure to end the conflict with Iran, which began in February, and has repeatedly emphasized that the U.S. holds the upper hand in negotiations.

Trump has stated he is not in a hurry to resume full-scale military action against Iran, though he has warned that the U.S. will take decisive action if diplomatic talks fail. He has also mentioned the possibility of targeting Iran’s nuclear facilities, including a site referred to as "Pickaxe Mountain," which he claims could be neutralized quickly. The strikes are part of a broader context of tensions between the U.S. and Iran, with both sides engaged in ongoing diplomatic and military posturing.

Where the accounts differ

Newsweek reports that the strikes are part of a broader context of deferred decisions and executive action expected in the post-midterm period, while The Guardian notes that the strikes were kept secret by the Trump administration to avoid signaling an intent to resume military action. The Washington Examiner and BBC both describe the strikes as defensive, but the Guardian adds that the administration intentionally limited public disclosure. New York Post and The Guardian both mention Trump’s comments about the conflict being a "soft war," but only The Guardian attributes the quote to a specific interview with Fox News. Newsweek and The Guardian differ in their emphasis on the political implications of the strikes, with Newsweek focusing on the broader post-midterm chaos and The Guardian highlighting the impact on voter sentiment.

Sources Newsweek · New York Post · BBC · Washington Examiner · The Guardian

Judge dismisses criminal case against ex-FBI officials accused of lying to Congress about Hunter Biden investigation

A federal magistrate judge dismissed criminal charges against two former FBI officials, Nikki Cole Floris and Bradley Benavides, who were accused of lying to Congress about their knowledge of the Hunter Biden laptop investigation. The judge, Zia Faruqui, criticized the Justice Department for what she called a rushed and politically motivated prosecution. The case stemmed from allegations that the officials made false statements during congressional interviews in 2023 about their involvement in the FBI's handling of the Hunter Biden investigation. The judge ruled that the DOJ failed to establish probable cause for the charges.

The ruling followed a preliminary hearing where Faruqui questioned the government's decision to pursue the case through a criminal complaint rather than a grand jury indictment. She also criticized prosecutors for attempting to add obstruction charges without judicial approval. The judge noted that the alleged false statements were made in 2023, yet the DOJ waited three years to bring the case, and the charges were filed just weeks before the midterm elections, despite a policy prohibiting timing actions to influence elections.

Where the accounts differ

FOX News states that the judge described the case as a "rush job" directed by President Donald Trump and FBI Director Kash Patel, while NPR reports that the judge noted the investigation was opened on Patel's orders the day after Trump's speech in mid-July. Straight Arrow News does not mention Trump's involvement in the timing of the investigation. FOX News also mentions that the judge apologized to the suspect accused of plotting an assassination attempt against Trump, a detail not included in the other accounts.

Sources FOX News · NPR · Straight Arrow News

Ukraine and Russia Report Hacked Emergency Alert Systems With False Nuclear and Chemical Threats

Ukraine’s Air Force confirmed that its air alert app, used to warn citizens of incoming air attacks, was hacked and sent false warnings about radiation and chemical threats in Kyiv. The alerts also claimed a Russian hypersonic Oreshnik missile had been deployed and warned of street fighting in the capital. Ukraine denied any radiation threat and urged citizens to follow official updates. Russia’s Emergencies Ministry later reported that its own warning app had been hacked, sending false messages about drone attacks and nuclear warheads mounted on Ukrainian drones. Both countries claimed their systems had been compromised, though no group has publicly taken responsibility for the attacks.

The fake alerts began after a six-day ceasefire in the Russian-occupied Kherson region ended. Ukraine’s Center for Strategic Communications blamed Russia for the hack, citing the use of Russian in one of the messages. Russia’s ministry did not name any suspects but stated the alerts spread "false information." The incidents occurred just after 10pm (20:00 GMT) in Ukraine and shortly after in Russia, causing public alarm before officials clarified the situation.

Where the accounts differ

Al Jazeera and FOX News both report that Ukraine’s Air Force blamed Russia for the hack, citing the use of Russian in one of the messages. Newsweek adds that Russia’s Oreshnik missile was mentioned in the false alerts, noting it was first used in November 2024. BBC does not specify who was blamed for the hack, only that both countries claimed their systems were hacked. FOX News attributes the false warnings to "cyber actors associated with Russian Intelligence," a claim not made by the other outlets.

Sources Al Jazeera · Newsweek · BBC · FOX News

New York child dies from measles in state’s first fatal case in 30 years

A child in rural northern New York has died after contracting measles, marking the state’s first death from the disease in 30 years, state officials announced. The child died in Lewis County, about 93 miles north of Syracuse. New York governor Kathy Hochul expressed heartbreak over the death and linked it to the federal government’s stance on immunisation. Measles is a highly contagious virus that can cause serious health complications, with deaths occurring in approximately one to three patients per 1,000 cases. The CDC reported 4,588 cases of measles across the country in 2026 as of late October, more than double the tally for 2025. Pennsylvania health officials have reported six deaths this year.

The New York State Department of Health and Lewis County Public Health announced the death of an unvaccinated child in Lewis County, which has the fourth-highest measles case count in the state with 15 cases reported this year. Gov. Hochul declared a disaster emergency in early October due to measles cases in neighboring states and rural areas of New York, allowing nurses to order testing and expanding the pool of people who can administer vaccines.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian reports that the CDC has confirmed three measles-associated deaths in the US this year, while the New York Post states that Pennsylvania health officials have reported six deaths. The Washington Examiner does not mention the number of deaths reported by Pennsylvania or the CDC. The New York Post provides details about the measles outbreak in Lewis County, including the number of cases, while the Guardian and Washington Examiner do not specify the number of cases in Lewis County. The Guardian attributes the death to the federal government’s stance on immunisation, while the New York Post attributes the death to an unvaccinated child.

Sources The Guardian · Washington Examiner · New York Post

Billionaire tax puts California’s economic future before midterm voters

California voters are set to cast their ballots on Proposition 40, a proposed one-time 5% wealth tax on individuals and trusts with assets over $1 billion. The tax would fund healthcare, education, and food assistance. Proponents argue it could generate about $100 billion in revenue, though some economists warn it may shrink the state’s tax base over time. Opponents, including several billionaires, claim the tax could drive wealthy residents and businesses out of the state. Google co-founders Larry Page and Sergey Brin, Oracle founder Larry Ellison, and PayPal co-founder Peter Thiel have all expressed concerns about the tax. Max Levchin, co-founder of Affirm Holdings, stated he would "probably" leave California if the tax passes.

The tax would apply retroactively to those who were California residents as of Jan. 1, 2026, and would cover assets like businesses, securities, and intellectual property, but exclude real property, pensions, and retirement accounts. Advocates say billionaires typically pay little in income taxes due to their wealth being derived from investments, so the wealth tax would raise additional revenue even after accounting for some departures. UC Berkeley economists Gabriel Zucman and Emmanuel Saez estimated the tax could raise about $100 billion over five years with minimal impact on income tax revenue.

Where the accounts differ

Fox Business reports that Max Levchin, co-founder of Affirm Holdings, said he would "probably" leave California if the tax passes, while the New York Post states that Don Hankey, a billionaire who moved to Las Vegas, warned that California has sacrificed years of income tax revenue by driving wealthy residents away. Fox Business also mentions that a working paper by economists at the Hoover Institution estimated collections would fall between $28 billion and $36 billion after accounting for departures and avoidance. The New York Post notes that a Stanford study found at least seven billionaires, with a combined fortune of nearly $540 billion, have already left California.

Sources Fox Business · New York Post

Russian bombing kills 29 people at Ukraine aid centre, Kyiv says

Russian forces bombed a humanitarian aid centre in Komyshuvakha, Zaporizhzhia region, killing at least 29 people, according to both outlets. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy initially reported 18 deaths, later revised to 26, while regional head Mykhailo Semikin confirmed 24 deaths, including four police officers and two rescuers. The attack occurred during a period of heightened Russian strikes, with Zelenskyy criticizing Moscow for continuing attacks despite a recent ceasefire in Oleshky. Rescue operations were hampered by Russian drone attacks, according to Semikin. A day of mourning was declared for the Zaporizhzhia region. The attack is part of a broader wave of strikes that damaged power plants, hospitals, and caused casualties across Ukraine. The US and Russia are set to resume peace talks in Abu Dhabi next week.

Where the accounts differ

BBC reports that Zelenskyy initially put the death toll at 18, later revised to 26, while the state emergencies service DSNS confirmed 24 deaths. The Guardian states that at least 48 people are still missing. BBC mentions that Zelenskyy said Russia could not have been unaware that residents received aid weekly at the site, while The Guardian attributes the same statement to Zelenskyy and adds that the attack showed Russia's intent to kill rather than de-escalate. The Guardian also provides more detail on the broader strike pattern, including attacks on power plants, hospitals, and casualties in Kharkiv and Dnipro regions, which BBC does not mention.

Sources BBC · The Guardian
