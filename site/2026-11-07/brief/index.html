Trump Names Stephen Miller as New White House Chief of Staff

President Donald Trump announced on Friday that Stephen Miller, his deputy chief of staff, will replace Susie Wiles as White House chief of staff starting November 9, 2026. The move comes after Republicans lost control of both chambers of Congress in the midterm elections. Miller, known for his hardline immigration policies, is expected to continue Trump's agenda, including efforts to strengthen border security and end birthright citizenship. Wiles, who had been a key figure in the administration, resigned following the election results.

Trump described Miller as a "great fighter" and "very loyal," emphasizing his role in the administration's successes. The president also mentioned that Miller's appointment signals a continuation of his policies, particularly on immigration, which has been a central focus of his administration. The decision follows a period of significant political shifts, with Democrats gaining control of Congress and Trump facing criticism for his approach to immigration and other issues.

Where the accounts differ

Mother Jones and The Daily Wire both state that Trump called Miller "one of my most trusted advisors," while Newsweek and Washington Examiner refer to him as "one of the most influential and uncompromising figures in his administration." The Daily Wire and Mother Jones mention that Wiles resigned after the midterm losses, while Newsweek and Washington Examiner attribute her resignation to the election results. The Daily Wire and The Washington Examiner note that Miller is described as a "wartime consigliere" by a source close to the White House, a characterization not included in other accounts. The Daily Wire and The Washington Examiner also mention that Trump's move to promote Miller gained support from Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, a detail absent in other reports.

US judge halts construction of Trump’s triumphal arch in Washington, DC

A federal judge ordered a 14-day pause on the construction of President Donald Trump’s 250-foot “triumphal arch” in Washington, DC, following a court ruling. The National Capital Planning Commission (NCPC) approved the project with an 8-4 vote, despite opposition from dozens of witnesses. The judge’s decision follows multiple lawsuits challenging the construction, including one from Vietnam war veterans who argued the arch would block the view between Arlington National Cemetery and the Lincoln Memorial. Trump has proposed building the arch near Arlington National Cemetery, where war veterans are buried, raising concerns about its impact on the cemetery’s visibility.

The project has drawn criticism from preservationists, lawmakers, and veterans, who argue it could disrupt views of the Lincoln Memorial and Arlington National Cemetery. Trump has also claimed the arch would be converted into a military complex capable of deploying drones and storing ammunition. The White House has stated the arch would commemorate 250 years of US independence, comparing it to the Arc de Triomphe in Paris. The judge’s order temporarily blocks construction, excavation, and other preparatory activities, requiring the administration to certify compliance by November 9.

Where the accounts differ

Al Jazeera and The Hill both report that the judge cited concerns about traffic, disaster preparedness, and environmental harm as reasons for the pause. Mother Jones and Axios note that the judge also referenced the need for congressional authorization and compliance with federal requirements. FOX News and Axios mention that Trump has previously claimed the arch would include military capabilities, such as snipers and drone deployment, which are not explicitly mentioned in Al Jazeera or The Hill. The Hill and Axios state that the judge ordered the government to file a status report by November 9, while Al Jazeera and The Hill do not mention this detail. Mother Jones and Axios also highlight that the NCPC vote was packed with over 100 public comments, mostly against the project, which is not explicitly stated in Al Jazeera or The Hill.

Sources Al Jazeera · FOX News · Washington Examiner · Mother Jones · Axios · The Hill

Gavin Newsom Has Commuted the Largest Death Row in the Western Hemisphere

California Gov. Gavin Newsom has commuted the death sentences of all 548 people on death row in the state to life without the possibility of parole. Newsom, who is term-limited and will leave office in January, described the action as incompatible with California’s values and a failure of the legal system. The decision comes after California has not executed anyone since 2006, and Newsom had previously instituted a formal moratorium on executions in 2019. The move is seen as symbolic, as the state has not carried out an execution in over two decades. Newsom’s successor, Xavier Becerra, has committed to extending the execution moratorium once in office.

Newsom’s commutations must be individually approved by California’s courts, and the state Supreme Court will review cases where the individual was convicted of multiple felonies. The United States has executed 33 people in 2026 so far, though most executions are carried out at the state level. Newsom’s decision has been praised by civil rights advocates and some victims’ families, who argue that the death penalty is costly and unreliable. However, prosecutors and some victims’ relatives have criticized the move, arguing it undermines justice for victims and fails to address the severity of the crimes committed.

Where the accounts differ

Mother Jones and The Guardian both state that Newsom’s commutations will convert all death sentences to life without parole, while FOX News and The New York Post highlight specific cases, such as Isauro Aguirre, who murdered an 8-year-old boy, and Richard Allen Davis, who kidnapped and murdered Polly Klaas. The Guardian and Mother Jones mention that 220 inmates waited 15 years or more for legal representation, a detail not included in other accounts. The Hill notes that 70% of the commutations will require review by the California Supreme Court, a point not mentioned in other outlets. The New York Post and FOX News emphasize the political nature of the decision, while Mother Jones and The Guardian focus on the moral and legal arguments.

Sources Mother Jones · BBC · The Guardian · FOX News · New York Post · The Hill

Russell Brand found guilty of rape and sexual assault

Russell Brand, 51, was found guilty of one count of rape and three counts of sexual assault by a jury at Southwark Crown Court in London. The verdict followed a four-week trial involving six women who alleged incidents spanning from 1999 to 2009. Brand was acquitted of two rape charges and one count of attempted indecent assault. The judge, Mr Justice Bennathan, remanded Brand in custody pending sentencing, which is scheduled for Monday. Brand, who had pleaded not guilty to the charges, showed little emotion during the verdict and repeatedly said, "I'm good, I'm good, I'm good," before being taken into custody. The Metropolitan Police stated that Brand remains under investigation for two further rape allegations.

DOJ's Harmeet Dhillon resigns

Harmeet Dhillon, assistant attorney general for civil rights at the Justice Department, has resigned from her role, Attorney General Todd Blanche said Thursday. Dhillon spearheaded the DOJ's efforts to monitor midterm elections in all 50 states for the first time in U.S. history, sending approximately 1,000 monitors to ensure "election integrity." She was appointed by President Trump in December 2024 and confirmed by the Senate in April 2025. The DOJ referred Axios' request for comment to Blanche's post.

Two top Justice Department officials resigned Friday, including the prosecutor whose South Florida office oversaw President Trump’s so-called grand conspiracy probe. Jason Reding Quinones, the South Florida-based U.S. attorney tasked with leading a sweeping and controversial conspiracy probe targeting President Donald Trump's foes, has resigned from the Justice Department, Attorney General Todd Blanche announced Friday. The dual resignations came in quick succession, with Blanche sharing just an hour and a half later that John Eisenberg, assistant attorney general for the National Security Division, was also departing.

Colom and Hyde-Smith's razor-thin Senate race spotlights Mississippi's voter rolls

The Mississippi Senate race between Democrat Scott Colom and Republican Cindy Hyde-Smith remains too close to call, with Colom holding a narrow lead of over 2,000 votes as of Friday. The race has drawn attention due to the state's use of provisional ballots and a controversial voter-roll cleanup. Mississippi Secretary of State Michael Watson announced that 3,600 absentee-by-mail ballots remain to be counted, along with an unknown number of provisional ballots. These ballots are required for voters who lacked proper identification or had their addresses changed. The state's system allows for these ballots to be counted if they were postmarked by Election Day and received by November 10. The race has also raised concerns about the reliability of third-party data used to verify voter addresses, particularly after a $50,000 deal with Experian in March. Election officials in Oktibbeha County confirmed that some records were inaccurate, with one voter being incorrectly told he lived in a different county. The race has led to legal disputes over how provisional ballots should be handled, with both campaigns preparing for potential legal challenges.

Kenyan Cult Leader Sentenced to 200 Years for Killing 190 Children and One Adult

Kenyan cult leader Paul Mackenzie was sentenced to 200 years in prison for the murders of 190 children and one adult, according to multiple outlets. The court found him guilty of ordering his followers to starve themselves and their children to death in the Shakahola Forest, with some victims also suffocated, strangled, or beaten. Mackenzie, founder of Good News International Church, was convicted alongside 28 others, including guards and gravediggers, for their roles in the killings. The sentences for Mackenzie include 100 years for murder by starvation and 100 years for direct homicide, which will run consecutively. The court rejected the prosecution’s request for the death penalty, citing Kenya’s moratorium on executions since 1987. The case stems from the 2023 discovery of over 400 bodies in shallow graves at the Chakama Ranch in Shakahola, where Mackenzie’s followers lived in isolation.

Mackenzie was arrested in 2023 after authorities received a tip about his activities. He had previously been arrested in 2017 on "radicalization" charges. The court found that Mackenzie’s teachings, which included fasting to meet Jesus, led to the deaths of his followers. Some victims were isolated and denied food while their parents watched, according to the judge. The court described the killings as "cold and calculated," with no remorse shown by the perpetrators. Mackenzie and his followers believed he was "Jesus’s mouthpiece," and those who resisted his orders were beaten to death.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian and BBC mention that 191 victims were identified, including 190 children and one adult, whereas The Daily Caller and CBS News refer to 190 children and one adult.
