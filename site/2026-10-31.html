Alaska Senate race faces confusion over two Dan Sullivans on ballot

The Alaska Senate race is facing confusion after two candidates with nearly identical names appear on the ballot: Republican incumbent Dan S. Sullivan and unaffiliated challenger Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. The ballot lists them as "Sullivan, Dan S." (Registered Republican, Incumbent) and "Sullivan, Daniel J. Jr." respectively. The controversy has drawn attention from President Trump, who accused Democratic candidate Mary Peltola and her campaign of election fraud. Alaska Governor Mike Dunleavy ordered an investigation into the allegations, citing concerns that the duplicate names could confuse voters. The Justice Department also filed a federal lawsuit seeking to block certification of the election if the incumbent loses by a margin potentially attributable to voter confusion.

The New York Times reported that Democratic operatives linked to Peltola's campaign helped recruit Daniel J. Sullivan to run, as part of a broader effort to dilute support for the incumbent. Peltola's campaign and the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee denied involvement, though the report cited texts, interviews, and documents supporting the claim. The Alaska Supreme Court previously ruled that state officials lacked authority to disqualify Sullivan based on his candidacy, but the court did not determine whether his candidacy was intended to mislead voters.

Where the accounts differ

The Daily Wire and Axios both report that the New York Times cited texts, interviews, and documents to support the claim that Peltola's campaign recruited Daniel J. Sullivan. The Daily Wire adds that the report included a statement from Peltola's fired campaign manager, Elisa Rios, who initially claimed her former boss was not directly involved but later retracted her statement. The Washington Examiner does not mention the New York Times report or the involvement of Peltola's campaign in recruiting Sullivan. The BBC and The Hill both note that Peltola's campaign and the DSCC denied recruiting Sullivan, though the BBC states that the NYT report has not been independently verified.

Sources The Dispatch · Axios · BBC · The Hill · Washington Examiner · The Daily Wire

Final Israeli election results show Netanyahu falling short as his party alleges fraud

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and his Likud party failed to secure a 61-seat majority in the Knesset, with the party winning 53 seats. The opposition bloc led by Gadi Eisenkot secured 48 seats, while Arab parties captured 19 seats. This outcome leaves Netanyahu’s party in a precarious position, as the opposition and Arab parties could potentially form a majority government. The final results were announced on Friday, confirming the election’s outcome.

Netanyahu’s Likud party accused Arab-led parties of stealing the election through alleged foreign funding and voter fraud, prompting calls for an investigation by Shin Bet. The opposition and Arab parties rejected these claims, with Gadi Eisenkot accusing Netanyahu of an organized campaign to delegitimize the election results. The Israeli police and election committee have stated there is no evidence to support the allegations of widespread fraud.

Where the accounts differ

The Washington Examiner and The Guardian both report that Netanyahu’s bloc secured 53 seats and the opposition bloc 48, with Arab parties taking 19. However, The Guardian and Axios provide more detail on Netanyahu’s allegations of foreign interference, specifically mentioning Qatar as the alleged foreign power. The Guardian and Axios also note that the Shin Bet investigation was based on classified intelligence, while the Washington Examiner does not mention this. The Straight Arrow News states that Israeli police found no evidence to support the fraud claims, a detail not included in the other accounts. The BBC and The Hill mention the potential for a national unity government and the timeline for forming a new government, which is not detailed in the Washington Examiner or Straight Arrow News.

Sources Washington Examiner · The Guardian · Axios · BBC · The Hill · Straight Arrow News

Qataris hold marathon talks in Tehran in bid for U.S.-Iran deal before midterms

Qatari mediators held talks in Tehran for more than 12 hours on Friday in an effort to reach a deal to restart direct negotiations between the U.S. and Iran and avoid an outbreak of war after the midterms, according to diplomatic sources. The discussions will continue in Doha, Qatar, on Saturday. A U.S. official said two competing demands have stymied the talks so far: the Trump administration's insistence that Iran make nuclear concessions as part of resuming talks, and Iran's stipulation that the U.S. first lift its naval blockade, release frozen funds and allow it to sell oil. The Qataris are trying to convince Iran to allow U.N. inspectors to visit its bombed nuclear sites as a confidence-building measure that the U.S. would respond to with some sanctions relief. The U.S. is now just days away from a possible military operation that would inflict enormous damage on Iran and likely lead to retaliatory strikes against Israel and a host of Gulf countries.

Where the accounts differ

Axios reports that Qatari mediators were in regular contact with the White House during Friday's daylong session, while Straight Arrow News does not mention such contact. Axios also states that Trump is skeptical Iran will ever agree to a deal he finds acceptable, whereas The Free Press notes that Trump is leaning toward some form of renewed military action. CBS News does not mention Qatari mediation efforts or U.S.-Iran negotiations, focusing instead on military actions in the region. FOX News highlights public frustration with the war and mentions Rep. Pat Harrigan's call for Trump to involve Congress in future decisions, which is not covered in Axios or The Free Press.

Sources Axios · Straight Arrow News · Slate · CBS News · The Free Press · FOX News

Trump’s Attempts to Interfere in the Midterms Have Been “Complete Failures”

Federal authorities, including the Department of Justice and Immigration and Customs Enforcement, have been attempting to identify and block mail-in ballots from noncitizens in California. These efforts include door-knocking by Homeland Security Investigations and the use of subpoenas to obtain records from election officials. A federal court in California recently blocked the Justice Department from demanding that election officials set aside ballots for 20,000 voters flagged as possibly noncitizens, citing concerns over disenfranchisement and election chaos. The administration has also faced legal challenges over its use of federal resources to scrutinize voter registration, with courts finding its actions may violate the Voting Rights Act.

The administration’s efforts have been met with legal pushback, including a federal judge ordering the DOJ to stop threatening California election officials with criminal prosecution. The judge emphasized that the right to vote must be protected from intimidation and that federal overreach could undermine public trust in elections. Advocacy groups and legal experts have criticized the administration’s approach as an attempt to intimidate voters and officials, arguing that the data used to flag noncitizens is unreliable and that the actions could suppress legitimate votes.

Where the accounts differ

Mother Jones and CBS News both report that a federal judge ordered the DOJ to stop threatening California election officials with criminal prosecution. However, CBS News specifies that the judge was Trina Thompson, while Mother Jones does not name the judge. Additionally, CBS News mentions that the judge issued a temporary restraining order, which Mother Jones does not include. FOX News and Straight Arrow News both report that the DOJ issued criminal subpoenas to election workers in California, but FOX News provides more specific details about the nature of the subpoenas, including the request for records on how the department stops non-citizens from voting. Straight Arrow News does not mention the subpoenas but focuses on the appeals court’s rejection of the DOJ’s request to set aside ballots.

Sources Mother Jones · NPR · CBS News · Straight Arrow News · Washington Examiner · FOX News

US inmate Christa Pike says she woke up 'confused and in pain' after failed execution

Christa Pike, a death row inmate in Tennessee, survived a failed execution attempt on September 30, 2026. She described waking up in a hospital "confused and in pain" after two doses of lethal injection. Pike, 50, was convicted of the 1995 torture and murder of her classmate Colleen Slemmer. Her lawyers claim the drugs did not enter her bloodstream, leading to physical injuries. Tennessee Governor Bill Lee paused all executions for the remainder of the year and ordered an independent review. Pike remains scheduled to die, though her legal team has requested a change to life in prison.

Pike's case has drawn attention to the broader issue of botched executions in the United States. The Tennessee Department of Correction has not explained why the execution did not go as planned, though it has defended the method used. Pike's mother, May Martinez, attended the botched execution and called it a "mess," expressing a desire for an apology. The failed execution is not the first in Tennessee in 2026; a similar attempt on Tony Carruthers was postponed in May due to difficulties finding a vein for the lethal injection.

Where the accounts differ

BBC reports that Pike's lawyers have spent decades appealing her case and that both the US Supreme Court and Governor Lee declined to block the execution. Al Jazeera states that Pike's legal team has asked a federal judge to allow her to interact with other prisoners, citing psychological harm from solitary confinement. Straight Arrow News mentions that Pike's legal team shared a new statement in which she detailed her recovery and mental state. Just The News notes that Pike's attorneys said she suffered from pneumonia and a blood clot, couldn't swallow, and was unable to move her arms or hands when she woke up. The Daily Wire states that Pike is the first known person to survive after execution drugs were actually administered. FOX News reports that Pike's attorneys say she is in critical condition after the botched lethal injection and that she has been largely isolated in the prison’s medical ward.

Sources BBC · Al Jazeera · Straight Arrow News · Just The News · The Daily Wire · FOX News

Trump reverses decision to cancel Kansas and Iowa rallies before midterms

President Donald Trump has reversed his decision to cancel in-person campaign rallies in Kansas and Iowa, set for Sunday, after initially planning to hold tele-rallies instead. The change comes as the midterms approach, with key Senate races in both states seen as crucial to Republican control of Congress. Trump will campaign for Republican Senate candidates Roger Marshall in Kansas and Ashley Hinson in Iowa. The decision follows pressure from local Republican campaigns, according to sources. The rallies will take place in Salina, Kansas, and Dubuque, Iowa, with tele-rallies also scheduled for Saturday. Trump has been traveling extensively ahead of the midterms, holding 25 rallies across 19 states in October, according to the Associated Press. The White House confirmed the change, stating that Trump will hold both in-person rallies while continuing with tele-rallies.

Where the accounts differ

Al Jazeera reports that Trump accused Democrats of recruiting an independent candidate with the same name as incumbent Republican Senator Dan Sullivan in Alaska, which is part of a legal dispute that could delay the Senate result. The Daily Wire and The Guardian do not mention this dispute, while The New York Post and Straight Arrow News do not reference the Alaska legal issue. The Guardian notes that polls show Democrats are leading in several key races, including in Kansas and Iowa, while Al Jazeera and The Daily Wire provide more specific poll numbers. The New York Post and Straight Arrow News mention that Trump’s decision was influenced by pressure from local Republican campaigns, while The Guardian and Al Jazeera attribute the change to a combination of scheduling and campaign strategy.

Sources Al Jazeera · The Daily Wire · New York Post · Just The News · The Guardian · Straight Arrow News

North Korean group blamed for $6.3bn Binance hack in biggest ever crypto heist

Binance, the world’s largest cryptocurrency exchange, reported a $6.3bn hack, described as the largest in history, with experts pointing to North Korea as the likely culprit. The company said it would cover all customer losses and froze $3bn of the affected assets. Analysts noted the attack involved "never-seen-before" laundering patterns and was attributed to North Korea’s state-backed hackers. Binance confirmed the breach started with a compromised laptop belonging to one of its engineers, though subsequent investigations found additional points of breach. The company suspended deposits and withdrawals while conducting security checks. Markets reacted with a 5% drop in bitcoin’s price. Binance co-CEO Richard Teng stated, “Your money is safe,” and assured users that all losses would be covered.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian and BBC both report the hack as the largest in history, with the Guardian citing $6.3bn and the BBC also citing $6.3bn. Axios reports a range of $1.7bn to $5.7bn, while NPR and Fox Business cite $6.3bn. The Guardian and BBC attribute the attack to North Korea’s TraderTraitor unit, while Fox Business and the Christian Science Monitor do not explicitly name the group but suggest North Korean involvement. The Guardian and BBC mention the FBI’s involvement in attributing the 2025 Bybit hack to North Korea, while Axios and NPR do not. The Guardian and BBC note that Binance’s co-founder Changpeng Zhao was jailed in 2024 and pardoned in 2025, while Fox Business and the Christian Science Monitor do not mention this. The Guardian and BBC report that Binance froze $3bn in assets, while Axios and NPR report that $3bn was frozen with the help of industry partners.

Sources The Guardian · Axios · BBC · NPR · Christian Science Monitor · Fox Business

US greenlights $2.1bn Patriot missile sale to Ukraine

The United States has approved the sale of 500 Patriot PAC-3 missiles to Ukraine for an estimated $2.1 billion. The State Department announced the deal on Friday, stating that Ukraine will pay for the purchase using donations from Canada, Germany, and Norway through a program called US JUMPSTART, along with national funds financed by the European Union’s Ukraine Support Loan. The sale is described as one of the most significant steps to supply defensive weapons to Kyiv since the start of the second administration of President Donald Trump in January 2025. The Patriot missile system is noted as the most effective weapon to counter fast-moving ballistic missiles.

Ukraine’s President Volodymyr Zelenskyy praised the decision but warned that deliveries may take years, as the missiles are yet to be manufactured and are not expected to be delivered until a couple of years into the 2030s. Zelenskyy has long emphasized the urgent need for more missile interceptors to counter Russia’s increasing attacks on Ukrainian energy and civilian infrastructure. A Russian drone strike on Thursday killed at least 14 people on a crowded rail platform in Dnipro, with additional casualties reported in other cities.

Where the accounts differ

Al Jazeera and New York Post both mention that the missiles are not expected to be delivered until a couple of years into the 2030s, while The Guardian and CBS News state that the delivery could take years but do not specify the exact timeframe. The Guardian and CBS News also note that Ukraine’s Ministry of Defense is encouraging the Pentagon to bring forward the delivery date, possibly by providing existing missiles in the US arsenal. Al Jazeera and New York Post do not mention this encouragement. The Guardian and CBS News attribute the claim about the delivery timeframe to Zelenskyy, while Al Jazeera and New York Post attribute it to unnamed sources.

Sources Al Jazeera · CBS News · BBC · Just The News · New York Post · The Guardian
