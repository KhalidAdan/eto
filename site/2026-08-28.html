People from more than 30 countries are missing after the flash floods in Nepal and China

A glacier collapse in Tibet triggered catastrophic flash flooding on the Nepal-Tibet border, resulting in significant casualties and missing persons. Authorities in both countries reported over 160 deaths and more than 1,300 people missing as of Thursday. Many of the missing were foreigners in the area for trekking or a pilgrimage to a sacred Hindu site. The number of missing may vary due to differences in data collection, reporting, and verification. Nepal Police reported at least 165 dead and 826 missing, while China State media reported at least three deaths and 558 missing in Gyirong County, Tibet. The U.S. State Department confirmed about 90 Americans are missing, though no confirmed deaths of U.S. citizens have been reported. The International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies said it will send $1.2 million in emergency funding to the Nepal Red Cross.

Where the accounts differ

Associated Press and Al Jazeera both reported that Nepal Police said 826 people were missing, including 113 Nepali tourists, 85 security personnel, and 161 residents from Rasuwa district. UPI and NPR reported that Nepal Tourism Board later said 517 foreigners were missing, while Al Jazeera and NPR stated that Nepal said 600 foreign tourists were missing. UPI reported that China said 260 of the 558 missing were foreigners, while Al Jazeera and NPR reported that China said 260 foreign nationals were missing in Tibet. UPI and NPR also reported that India said 133 Indians were missing on a pilgrimage to Mount Kailash, while Al Jazeera and Associated Press reported that India said 179 of those missing were from India. UPI and NPR reported that the U.S. said 65 of the missing were from the United States, while Al Jazeera and Associated Press reported that the U.S. said 65 of the missing were from the United States. UPI and NPR reported that Australia said 34 citizens were missing, while Al Jazeera and Associated Press reported that Australia said 34 citizens were missing.

Sources Associated Press · UPI · BBC · The Guardian · NPR · Al Jazeera

Trump's mail-voting rules face legal battles as courts weigh their legality

A federal judge in Boston has issued conflicting rulings on the legality of President Trump's executive order aimed at tightening mail-in voting rules ahead of the midterm elections. On Wednesday, Judge Indira Talwani lifted a nationwide block on the Postal Service's new requirements for mail ballots, allowing the agency to proceed with implementing the rule. However, on Thursday, she issued a temporary restraining order blocking enforcement of the rule for 14 days, citing concerns over voter disenfranchisement. The judge argued that the rule, which requires states to submit voter lists and use standardized ballot envelopes, risks preventing voters from casting ballots by mail. The Supreme Court had previously allowed the rule to move forward, but the court did not rule on its legality.

The White House has defended the rule as a necessary measure to ensure election security, with White House spokesperson Lauren Bis calling it "commonsense election integrity measures." The rule, which was finalized by the Postal Service in late August, requires states to submit voter information through a federal portal and use barcoded envelopes for ballots. Critics, including Democratic-led states and voting rights groups, argue the rule oversteps federal authority and could suppress voter turnout.

Where the accounts differ

Axios reports that a federal judge lifted a nationwide block on the Postal Service's rule, while The New York Post and Washington Examiner state that the same judge issued a temporary restraining order blocking enforcement of the rule. NPR and FOX News also note the judge issued a temporary restraining order, but NPR adds that the judge reestablished a nationwide block she had previously put on USPS' efforts. The Daily Wire states that the Supreme Court allowed the rule to move forward, while the New York Post and Washington Examiner clarify that the court only addressed a procedural issue and did not rule on the rule's legality.

Sources Axios · The Daily Wire · New York Post · NPR · Washington Examiner · FOX News

Meta agrees to $18 billion settlement with states over child safety claims

Meta has reached a settlement with 52 U.S. states, U.S. territories, and Washington, D.C., agreeing to pay up to $18 billion to resolve claims that Facebook and Instagram harmed children. The agreement includes measures such as two-hour daily time limits for under-18 users, nighttime access restrictions, and parental controls. The company also urged rival platforms like YouTube and TikTok to adopt similar policies. The settlement ends a federal trial that could have resulted in billions in damages for Meta.

The settlement requires Meta to implement changes to its platforms, including limiting notifications for children during school hours, blocking access from midnight to 6 a.m., and offering a non-personalized feed as an option. Meta did not admit wrongdoing and stated that its existing protections for young users are sufficient. The agreement also includes provisions for an independent auditor to assess compliance with the terms.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian reports that whistleblower Arturo Béjar criticized the settlement as insufficient, arguing it does not address algorithmic design choices that make Meta’s platforms addictive. The Daily Wire notes that the settlement includes a $17.1 billion figure, while other accounts cite $18 billion. Axios states the settlement is $16.7 billion, with $5 billion contingent on TikTok and YouTube joining. Fox Business mentions a $18 billion settlement, while the Daily Wire and BBC report $18 billion. The Guardian and The Daily Wire mention that Meta did not admit wrongdoing, while Axios and Fox Business note that the company did not accept any wrongdoing.

Sources BBC · The Guardian · Mother Jones · Axios · Fox Business · The Daily Wire

New York Gov Hochul rejects Trump's 'Lake America' renaming with blunt message

President Donald Trump signed an executive order renaming Lake Ontario to "Lake America" amid a trade dispute with Canada. The order directs the Interior Department and Board on Geographic Names to update federal records and provide guidance so federal references reflect the new name. New York Gov. Kathy Hochul rejected the move, stating her state would continue using the old name. U.S. states are not bound by the order and can continue using "Lake Ontario" on state maps, highway signs and tourism materials. Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney criticized the renaming, noting the name comes from an Indigenous word meaning "the lake is beautiful, the lake is big," which is more than 400 years old.

The renaming is part of Trump's broader trade dispute with Canada, which includes 50% tariffs on about $20 billion worth of Canadian goods. Canada has announced retaliatory tariffs set to take effect on Sept. 8. Trump has also faced criticism from state leaders, including Wisconsin Gov. Tony Evers and Vermont Gov. Phil Scott, who called the move "petty and disappointing." The name change is largely symbolic, as Canada and international bodies are not required to adopt the new name.

Where the accounts differ

FOX News reports that Trump's order directs the Interior Department and Board on Geographic Names to update federal records and provide guidance, while Axios states the order directs Interior Secretary Doug Burgum and his department to update the Geographic Names Information System (GNIS) to reflect the renaming within 30 days. Mother Jones mentions that Trump cannot force Canada or any other country to follow his preferred naming conventions, a point not explicitly made in the other accounts. UPI and Washington Examiner both note that the name change is mostly symbolic and that Canada will continue to call the lake "Lake Ontario," a detail not included in FOX News or Axios.

Sources FOX News · Axios · Mother Jones · NPR · UPI · Washington Examiner

Trump administration illegally retaliated against Anthropic, judge rules

A US federal judge ruled that the Trump administration's designation of AI company Anthropic as a supply chain risk was unlawful. The decision was made by US District Judge Rita Lin, who found that the government had punished Anthropic for its public criticism of the Pentagon's stance on AI deployment. Anthropic had refused to allow the military to use its AI models for surveillance or autonomous weapons, leading to the designation. The ruling permanently suspended the sanctions and overturned the designation, which was the first time a US company had been publicly labeled a supply chain risk under an obscure procurement statute. Anthropic welcomed the ruling, stating it remained focused on working with the government to harness AI for national security.

The judge ruled that the government's actions violated Anthropic's First Amendment rights by retaliating against its protected speech and its Fifth Amendment rights by denying it due process. The Pentagon had argued that Anthropic's refusal to accept new contract terms raised concerns over its technology's use. Anthropic claimed its AI models were not reliable enough for autonomous weapons and opposed domestic surveillance as a violation of rights. The government had previously described Anthropic as a "radical left, woke company" attempting to control military activity.

Where the accounts differ

BBC reports that the Pentagon designated Anthropic as a supply chain risk in February, a law typically reserved for foreign firms. The Guardian and Al Jazeera also mention this, but the Guardian adds that the designation was the first time a US company had been publicly labeled a supply chain risk under an obscure statute. CBS News and Axios note that the designation was the first time a US company had been labeled a supply chain risk under an obscure procurement statute aimed at protecting military systems from foreign sabotage. Washington Examiner refers to the designation as a "national security risk" and mentions the War Department, while the other outlets refer to the Pentagon. The Guardian and CBS News mention that the government argued Anthropic's refusal to accept contractual terms led to the designation, while the BBC and Al Jazeera attribute the designation to the Pentagon's refusal to accept Anthropic's terms.

Sources BBC · The Guardian · Al Jazeera · CBS News · Axios · Washington Examiner
