AI regulation faces political deadlock as calls grow for Congress to act

Leading U.S. AI companies endorsed a potential pause in AI development this weekend following troubling hacking incidents and displays of new model capabilities. The suggested pause caused a dip in global markets as investors considered the economic impact of a slowdown. Critics, however, made clear their distrust of frontier AI players, arguing that new regulatory hurdles could give these companies too much power. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei called for a slowdown in development to reduce risks, a stance supported by Elon Musk and Sam Altman of OpenAI. Meanwhile, President Donald Trump dismissed calls for a slowdown, asserting that AI development is a national priority and that the U.S. is leading China in the AI race. Trump argued that the only control AI needs is a "strong and smart" president, and he criticized critics as bringing up "things that won't happen." Some Republicans, including House Speaker Mike Johnson, called for Congress to take action, but the political landscape remains divided, with no clear path to swift legislation.

Where the accounts differ

Axios reports that Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei proposed new AI regulatory proposals in an essay, while Fox News attributes the same claim to Trump's White House AI advisor David Sacks. UPI and New York Post describe Trump as dismissing AI safety concerns as "things that won’t happen," whereas Fox Business and FOX News frame his comments as a rejection of "fearmongering" and a call for continued AI development. BBC and UPI note that Trump's comments were made during a visit to Ireland, while Axios and New York Post mention the same event but attribute different quotes to Trump. Axios and New York Post both mention that Anthropic employees resigned in protest, but only Axios names the employees as Joe Benton and Jacob Coxon.

Sources Axios · BBC · UPI · New York Post · Fox Business · FOX News

Supreme Court rejects mail-in voting restrictions ahead of midterms in blow to Trump admin

The U.S. Supreme Court on Monday blocked the implementation of new mail-in voting restrictions by the U.S. Postal Service, which were part of a Trump administration effort to limit ballot access ahead of the November midterm elections. The decision, described as a 7-2 ruling, allowed states to continue sending out mail ballots under existing procedures. The ruling was based on the argument that the Postal Service’s rule would be too late to implement and could disenfranchise voters. Justice Brett Kavanaugh, a Trump appointee, joined the majority in finding the rule arbitrary and capricious. Justices Samuel Alito and Clarence Thomas dissented, arguing the administration had the legal authority to regulate mail-in voting.

The Trump administration had issued an executive order in March 2026 directing the Postal Service to implement new rules requiring states to provide lists of eligible voters and to use standardized ballot envelopes with barcodes. The rule, which was finalized in late August, would have required states to submit voter lists to the Postal Service before the midterms. The Postal Service would then have been empowered to reject ballots that did not meet these new standards. The administration argued that the rule was necessary to prevent fraud, though critics warned it would disenfranchise millions of voters, particularly those in vulnerable groups.

Where the accounts differ

The New York Post and The Daily Wire both mention that Justice Kavanaugh agreed with the majority, while Mother Jones and Al Jazeera note that Kavanaugh’s reasoning was that the rule would be too late to implement. The Washington Examiner incorrectly states that the court decided in the Trump administration’s favor, which is not accurate. UPI and The Daily Wire both mention that Judge Carl J. Nichols ruled the plan likely illegal, while the New York Post and Al Jazeera do not mention this specific ruling. The Daily Wire and Mother Jones describe the rule in more detail, including the requirement for standardized envelopes and barcodes, which are not explicitly mentioned in the New York Post or Al Jazeera.

Sources New York Post · Mother Jones · Al Jazeera · UPI · Washington Examiner · The Daily Wire

Houthis seize strategic Red Sea islands as analysts warn of impending oil crunch

Yemen’s Houthi militants have seized two strategic islands in the Red Sea, reinforcing the Iran-backed group’s ability to control a key shipping route. The islands of Greater and Lesser Hanish lie 160km north of the Bab al-Mandab strait, a critical chokepoint connecting the Red Sea to the open ocean and Saudi Arabia’s key Asian markets. Analysts warn that the closure of Saudi Arabia’s east-west pipeline, which has been vital for moving oil from the Strait of Hormuz to the Red Sea export hub of Yanbu, could further strain global oil supplies. Experts say the stock buffers that once shielded the world from oil price shocks have largely disappeared, increasing the risk of higher prices.

The Houthi advance has prompted Saudi Arabia to increase its military readiness, with air alerts flashing daily. The group has also launched attacks on Saudi Arabia using ballistic missiles and drones, wounding 13 civilians. The Houthis claim they are not seeking to block all commercial shipping in the Red Sea, only Saudi-linked ships. Over 94,000 people in Yemen have fled their homes since fighting escalated, with many seeking refuge in Djibouti, just 32km from a US military base.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian and Al Jazeera both report the Houthi seizure of the Hanish islands, but Al Jazeera mentions the capture of Perim island in the Bab al-Mandab strait, which The Guardian does not. Al Jazeera also states that the Houthis claimed attacks on Saudi Arabia using ballistic missiles and drones, while The Guardian attributes the attacks to the Houthis but does not specify the type of weapons used. The Guardian reports that the Houthis have not sought to block all commercial shipping, a claim not made by Al Jazeera. CBS News and UPI both mention the attack on the Saudi East-West pipeline, but CBS News states the attack was carried out by Iran-backed Houthis, while UPI does not attribute the attack to a specific group.

Sources The Guardian · Al Jazeera · CBS News · UPI · The Dispatch · FOX News

Trump responds to request for Lindsay Clancy pardon, calls it a state matter

President Donald Trump addressed a request from Lindsay Clancy’s attorney, Kevin Reddington, for a pardon following the mistrial in the Clancy case. Trump stated that the charges were a “state situation, not a federal one,” emphasizing that the president can only pardon federal crimes. The case involved Clancy, who admitted to killing her three children, and the trial ended in a mistrial after 11 jurors voted for not guilty by reason of insanity, with one juror dissenting.

The trial, which drew national attention, saw a mix of public reactions. Some outlets highlighted the juror’s role in the mistrial, while others focused on the broader implications of the case. The Daily Wire criticized the media and legal system for what it called a “debacle of national significance,” while The Federalist framed the jury’s decision as an example of activist jurors influenced by progressive ideologies.

Where the accounts differ

The Washington Examiner and The Free Press both mention Trump’s response to Reddington’s request for a pardon, but only the Washington Examiner provides the specific detail that Trump called the charges a “state situation, not a federal one.” The Free Press focuses on the public accusations against Patrick Clancy, the ex-husband of Lindsay Clancy, and does not mention Trump’s comments. The Daily Wire, The Federalist, and The Nation all discuss the juror’s role and the broader implications of the case, but none mention Trump’s response to the pardon request. The Nation and The Federalist both reference calls to repeal the 19th Amendment, while The Daily Wire and The Free Press do not.

States and Cities Sue To Stop Trump’s Immigrant “Wealth Test” Rule Before It Takes Effect

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Attorney General Letitia James announced new state actions and $7.25 million in emergency legal defense funding to counter federal ICE overreach on August 12. A group of municipalities, led by New York City, is separately suing over the same rules. The new regulations, scheduled to go into effect September 18, reverse a set of 2022 rules that prohibited immigration officers from considering immigrants’ potential future use of non-cash public benefits—like food stamps, Children’s Health Insurance Program benefits, and Medicaid—when determining whether to grant visas. Under the rules scheduled to go into effect this week, officials can use an applicant’s need for food or medical aid to help determine grounds for inadmissibility.

Cities and states suing over the rule change argue that it “throws out more than a century of settled law and decades of federal guidance and gives officers wide latitude to make decisions that are inconsistent and discriminatory,” representatives for New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani wrote in a news release. The cities and counties argue the new rule will deter immigrant families from using Medicaid, SNAP, and housing assistance programs. New York City, Chicago, San Francisco and Seattle, along with Santa Clara County, California, and King County, Washington, filed the suit.

Where the accounts differ

Mother Jones and UPI both state the rule is set to go into effect September 18, while FOX News and ABC News say it takes effect Friday. Mother Jones and Axios mention the rule reverses 2022 rules that prohibited immigration officers from considering non-cash benefits, while UPI and ABC News say the rule expands the list of benefits considered. The Daily Wire states the rule could affect up to four million people, while Axios and UPI mention estimates of 950,000 to 1.4 million to 4.1 million. FOX News and The Daily Wire attribute the claim about people dying to Mamdani, while Mother Jones and UPI attribute it to Mamdani and others. The Daily Wire and FOX News mention the rule could affect U.S. citizen children, while Mother Jones and UPI do not.

Sources Mother Jones · Axios · UPI · The Daily Wire · FOX News · ABC News

NATO jets shoot down drone violating Lithuania’s airspace

NATO fighter jets shot down a drone that entered Lithuanian airspace, according to Lithuanian President Gitanas Nausėda. The drone, which originated from neighboring Belarus, was intercepted near the Kaunas Reservoir. The incident occurred as part of a NATO mission, with Italian pilots credited for the interception. Lithuania’s National Crisis Management Center confirmed the drone’s origin and trajectory, noting it flew south of Vilnius before being shot down. The drone’s purpose and whether it was armed remain under investigation. Poland also scrambled fighter jets due to Russian drones conducting strikes on Ukrainian territory, though no violation of Polish airspace was reported.

Lithuania’s President Nausėda emphasized the importance of NATO readiness amid Russia’s aggression against Ukraine. The incident follows a pattern of drone incursions reported by Baltic states since Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022. NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte previously warned that strikes near NATO territory would prompt increased support for Ukraine. The drone downing was described as the first such incident in Lithuania’s airspace, though similar events have occurred in Estonia and Latvia.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian and UPI both state the drone was shot down by Italian fighter jets, while Newsweek and NPR do not specify which nation’s jets were involved. The Guardian and UPI mention the drone was shot down near the Kaunas Reservoir, while NPR and BBC describe the location as near the village of Pratkūnai. The Guardian and UPI attribute the drone’s origin to Belarus, while the BBC and NPR suggest the drone may have originated from Belarus but traveled through Russian airspace. The Washington Examiner omits details about the drone’s origin and trajectory, only mentioning that the president blamed Russia. The Guardian and UPI provide specific times for the incident, while other accounts do not.

Sources The Guardian · Washington Examiner · NPR · Newsweek · BBC · UPI

Mariska Hargitay Hosted Emmys, Mocked Celebrities in Opening Monologue

Mariska Hargitay made her hosting debut at the 78th Primetime Emmy Awards, using her opening monologue to playfully roast several high-profile stars. The appearance marked a milestone for Hargitay, who became the first woman to host the Emmys in 15 years. Celebrities Nicole Kidman, Dick Wolf, Amy Poehler, Tina Fey, and Martin Short found themselves the target of Hargitay's lighthearted jokes as she kicked off TV's biggest night. Known to millions as Olivia Benson on Law & Order: Special Victims Unit (SVU), Hargitay stepped into one of television's most visible hosting roles with a mix of music, self-deprecation, and celebrity ribbing. Her monologue set the tone for the ceremony while highlighting both industry icons and some of television's most recognizable faces.

Hargitay's first outing as Emmys host began before she even reached the stage. A prerecorded opening segment showed the actress preparing for the ceremony alongside New York Knicks star Jalen Brunson. Hoping for reassurance, she asked whether he had ever experienced a moment when millions of people were watching and everything came down to one night. Brunson's deadpan response, "Seriously?" immediately undercut the comparison and delivered the first laugh of the evening. The opening package also featured a surprise appearance from Taylor Swift in a spoof Law & Order: SVU sketch. Playing a rookie detective, Swift joined Hargitay's Benson in a tongue-in-cheek investigation into whether the singer would attend the Emmys, before joking that she might instead show up in a pre-taped segment supporting the host. Afterward, Hargitay launched into a musical number built around Joan Jett's classic "I Love Rock 'n' Roll," rewriting the lyrics to spotlight Emmy nominees before transitioning into her live monologue.

Where the accounts differ

Newsweek reports that Hargitay's opening monologue included jokes targeting Amy Poehler and Tina Fey, referencing that she was the first woman to host the Emmys since Jane Lynch in 2011, and that Poehler and Fey had said no for 15 years. Al Jazeera and CBS News do not mention this specific joke. The Guardian and NPR also do not reference this line, though they do note Hargitay's jokes about Martin Short and Dick Wolf. BBC does not mention the monologue at all, focusing instead on the winners and Matthew Rhys' double win.

Sources Newsweek · Al Jazeera · CBS News · The Guardian · NPR · BBC

EPA ends limits on power-plant climate pollution

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) announced it will repeal rules limiting greenhouse gas emissions from coal and natural gas power plants. The agency also plans to argue that greenhouse gases do not endanger human health or the environment, so it does not have the authority to regulate their emission through the Clean Air Act. The EPA said repealing the Biden-era emissions standards would save $310 billion over roughly 20 years. Environmental groups warned that the move would come at a huge cost to Americans' health and the planet. The EPA's move is expected to face immediate legal challenges.

The EPA said in a statement that it found the Biden-era rules on power plant emissions had "exceeded the agency's authority." The agency said the regulations required "control technologies that are not adequately demonstrated, effectively forcing plants to retire rather than setting standards they could actually meet." The EPA is also seeking to get rid of all other greenhouse gas rules for the power sector. The changes, which will undergo public scrutiny, were proposed last year by Zeldin. Safeguards against "hazardous air pollutants" would remain in place "to safeguard human health."

Where the accounts differ

BBC reports that the EPA said the Biden-era rules would stop 4,500 premature deaths annually, while UPI does not mention this figure. UPI states that the EPA plans to argue that greenhouse gases do not endanger human health or the environment, a claim not made by BBC. BBC mentions that the EPA said any potential public health harms are too uncertain to tie specifically to the US power sector, while UPI attributes this claim to The New York Times. UPI also includes quotes from Letitia James, New York's attorney general, and Manish Bapna, president of the Natural Resources Defense Council, which are not present in BBC.

Sources BBC · UPI
