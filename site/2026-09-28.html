UK police arrest several under explosives act in 'major incident' near US air base

British police arrested five men on suspicion of offenses under the Explosives Act near RAF Fairford, a U.S. military base in southwest England. The incident, declared a "major incident," led to the evacuation of around 85 households and the establishment of a 400-meter cordon around the suspect vehicles. Armed police and explosive ordnance disposal teams were deployed to the scene. President Donald Trump praised the collaboration with British authorities, stating the suspects had been under surveillance for a "long time" and were "looking to do big damage" to the U.S. base. The U.S. military has used RAF Fairford for operations against Iran, though no direct link to Iran has been confirmed.

Where the accounts differ

ABC News and The Daily Wire both report that Trump stated the suspects were "looking to do big damage" to the U.S. base. The Hill and Washington Examiner attribute this characterization to Trump as well, but The Hill adds that Rep. Kat Cammack claimed Iran was "clearly" involved in the plot. The Daily Wire and Newsweek mention that authorities are investigating a potential link to Iran, while ABC News and The Hill note that no connection to Iran has been confirmed. The Hill and Washington Examiner report that the nationalities of the arrested men are not public knowledge, while The Daily Wire and Newsweek do not mention this detail.

Sources ABC News · The Hill · Straight Arrow News · Washington Examiner · The Daily Wire · Newsweek

Iran 'fully prepared' for war to resume if Trump authorizes strikes after midterms

Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi stated that Iran is “fully prepared” for the conflict with the U.S. to resume if President Trump authorizes new strikes on the regime after the November midterm elections. Araghchi told NBC News that the U.S. has “tested us in their aggression” and in diplomacy. He said that although Trump rejected Iran’s seven-day proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz and enter a ceasefire, the offer still stands. Araghchi emphasized that Iran is ready for both war and diplomacy, with the decision ultimately resting with Trump. Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian told Fox News that he last spoke with Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei in person about a month and a half ago and said Khamenei is “completely” able to govern.

Trump told Axios in a phone interview that he expects U.S. negotiators to engage in more talks with Iran this week, though he said the deal Iran proposed is not the one he wants. He also mentioned that the U.S. military is facilitating a lot of oil out of the Strait of Hormuz, with over 20 million barrels transiting through the strait over the weekend. Trump said he is “always thinking about” resuming strikes against Iran.

Where the accounts differ

The Hill and The Guardian both report that Araghchi said Iran is “fully prepared” for war if Trump authorizes strikes, but The Guardian adds that Araghchi said Iran has “never closed the door to diplomacy or negotiations, but at the same time, we have never backed down from our rights.” The New York Post and The Hill both mention that Araghchi said Iran’s nuclear enrichment program is not illegal and intended for “certain” purposes, while The Guardian states that Araghchi said Iran’s conditions for reopening the Strait of Hormuz are “clear” and any move toward reopening the strait is contingent upon these conditions being fulfilled. The UPI and The Guardian both mention that Trump referred to the Strait of Hormuz as the “Trump Strait,” but The Guardian adds that Trump also said he would “annihilate” Iran if he couldn’t make a peace deal. The BBC and The Guardian both report that Araghchi said Iran is waiting for a “definitive” official U.S. response through the mediators, but The BBC adds that Araghchi said Trump had made “many contradictory” statements in the past.

Sources The Hill · Axios · BBC · UPI · New York Post · The Guardian

Powerful Northeast Storm Kills At Least One, Knocks Out Power For 100,000

A powerful storm swept across the Northeast on Saturday, killing at least one person and knocking out power to about 100,000 customers. Leighton Brown, a 56-year-old New York City Housing Authority (NYCHA) employee, was killed in Brooklyn when a tree fell on him during the storm, NYCHA said. New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani urged residents to avoid travel, warning that the city would still feel the storm’s effects even as the heaviest rain passed offshore. In New Jersey, Governor Mikie Sherrill warned that a temporary break in the weather did not mean the danger had passed. The storm is expected to linger through Monday, prolonging the threat of flooding along the Northeast coast.

A slow-moving, powerful storm known as a nor’easter crept along the coast of the US north-east on Saturday, producing high winds that knocked out electricity to thousands of people while whipping up big waves and heavy rains that created dangerous conditions in some places. About 100,000 customers in the region were without electricity Saturday. The storm is expected to affect the mid-Atlantic area and New England throughout the weekend. In New Jersey, Brian Kelly, mayor of the small shore borough of Sea Bright, warned that water could reach six feet on some streets and compared the expected flooding to Hurricane Irene, which devastated the region in 2011.

Where the accounts differ

The Daily Wire and BBC both report that a man was killed by a falling tree in Brooklyn, but only The Daily Wire names him as Leighton Brown and attributes the statement to NYCHA. The Guardian and NPR do not mention the death of a specific individual, though both report the storm killed at least one person. The Guardian and BBC report that in Surf City, New Jersey, a marina owner named Kenny Formica described the damage to his business, including a boat being blown off its lift, while The Daily Wire and UPI do not mention this specific incident. The Guardian and NPR report that the storm caused widespread power outages, with more than 100,000 households affected, while The Daily Wire and BBC report similar numbers but do not attribute the figure to a specific source. The Guardian and NPR also mention that the storm caused cancellations of events such as concerts and festivals, while The Daily Wire and BBC do not specify which events were canceled.

Sources The Daily Wire · The Guardian · BBC · UPI · Straight Arrow News · NPR

At least 27 dead after two mass shootings in South Africa, police say

Two separate mass shootings near South Africa’s two biggest cities killed at least 27 people, as the country grapples with one of the world’s highest murder rates. The first incident occurred in Wedela township, southwest of Johannesburg, where eight suspects armed with AK-47 assault rifles and pistols opened fire at a bar, killing 17 people and wounding 15. In a separate attack in Lwandle, near Cape Town, gunmen killed 10 people and wounded 11 at an entertainment venue. Police said they were searching for multiple suspects in both cases, with no arrests made yet. The attacks happened days after a mass shooting near Durban killed 11 people, including a pregnant woman.

South Africa has struggled with high rates of violent crime for years, but the series of mass shootings this week were shocking even to a country used to some of the world’s highest violent crime rates. President Cyril Ramaphosa called the level of criminality unacceptable and said security forces were taking “all stops” to address the issue. Police in Gauteng province launched a manhunt for the eight suspects in the Johannesburg tavern shooting, with no arrests yet made. Western Cape police set up a team to investigate the Cape Town shooting, with no arrests announced.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian and CBS News both report that 17 people were killed in the Johannesburg attack and 10 in the Cape Town attack, but the Guardian adds that 15 were injured in the first attack and 11 in the second. Al Jazeera and ABC News also report 17 and 10 deaths respectively, but ABC News specifies that the victims included 13 men and four women in the Johannesburg attack. NPR reports 10 deaths in the Cape Town attack and 11 in the Johannesburg attack, but also mentions that 11 others were injured in the Cape Town attack. The Guardian and BBC both mention that the motive for the Cape Town attack was possibly robbery or turf wars between illegal mining groups, while CBS News and Al Jazeera do not specify a motive. The Guardian and BBC also note that the Johannesburg attack may have been retaliation for a previous shooting at the same venue, which Al Jazeera and CBS News do not mention.

Sources The Guardian · Al Jazeera · ABC News · CBS News · BBC · NPR

Trump stumps for Protect College Sports Act at Texas-Tennessee game

President Donald Trump addressed the Protect College Sports Act while attending the University of Texas versus University of Tennessee college football game on Saturday. During an interview with commentator Clay Travis, Trump claimed that House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) could ensure the bill’s success in the House, noting that the bill had 78 votes. The game took place in Knoxville, Tennessee, where Tennessee offered 50 percent off concessions, including alcohol, in the hour before kickoff to encourage early fan arrivals. The event also featured the presence of ESPN’s “College GameDay,” which added to the expected security challenges. Trump’s arrival was marked by an Air Force One flyover, and he was seen on the stadium’s big screen during the game.

The White House confirmed that Trump’s presence at the game did not cause any issues with team communications, as was the case during the College Football Playoff championship game in January. However, Texas coach Steve Sarkisian warned that headsets for on-field communication could be disrupted by Trump’s movements within the stadium, noting that teams had to prepare for such disruptions without warning.

Where the accounts differ

The New York Post details Tennessee’s concession discount strategy and the logistical challenges of Trump’s arrival, which are not covered in the Washington Examiner or The Daily Wire. The Daily Wire notes that Trump was interviewed by Clay Travis during halftime, a detail absent from the Washington Examiner and New York Post accounts. The Guardian and The Hill do not mention the game or the Protect College Sports Act, focusing instead on taxpayer-funded Trump ads.

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei to have private White House dinner with Trump

President Trump plans to host Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei at a private White House dinner on Sunday evening, according to sources familiar with the event. The meeting, which will be the first one-on-one between the two, is seen as a sign of thawing relations between Anthropic and the administration, which has had longstanding tensions with the company. Trump confirmed the dinner to Fox News’ Aishah Hasnie, and the event comes two days before a planned meeting with House Speaker Mike Johnson and top AI executives. Amodei was not among the tech leaders invited to last week’s state dinner for Chinese President Xi Jinping, which included OpenAI’s Sam Altman, Amazon’s Jeff Bezos, and Meta’s Mark Zuckerberg.

Amodei has called for slowing the pace of AI development, warning that rapid advancements pose significant risks. His concerns were echoed by former Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon, who resigned earlier this month, arguing that leading AI companies are not acting responsibly. The White House has also been involved in a legal battle with the Pentagon over Anthropic’s designation as a supply-chain risk. A federal appeals court recently upheld the Pentagon’s decision, allowing the department to continue designating Anthropic as a national security risk.

Where the accounts differ

Blanche defends Trump’s White House media ban: ‘It is a privilege, not a right’

On Sunday, Attorney General Todd Blanche argued that the White House was permitted to ban three news outlets from the White House because “it is a privilege and not a right to be there.” Blanche appeared on ABC News’ This Week, where he stated that the administration had decided to ban CNN, MS NOW, and Politico beginning on 19 September due to the president’s frustration with what he called the outlets’ inaccurate reporting. Blanche did not provide evidence for his claim, and when pressed by Martha Raddatz to name a specific report the administration objected to, he refused. A federal judge had previously issued a temporary restraining order requiring the White House to restore the credentials of affected journalists, allowing them back into the executive complex. However, on Saturday, CNN reported that its journalists were barred from joining the press pool traveling with the president on Air Force One.

The dispute between the administration and the press corps had appeared to be resolving before CNN was again blocked from providing pool TV coverage of Trump on Saturday. This followed a period where outlets stood in solidarity with CNN, MS NOW, and Politico by briefly refusing to provide any pool TV feeds of the president. Blanche claimed it was not unusual for a news outlet to be excluded from the president’s plane, stating that the White House routinely decides who can go on Air Force One.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian and ABC News both report that Blanche claimed the White House had not denied access to the outlets, but The Guardian adds that Blanche suggested any negative reporting about Trump must be inaccurate. The Hill and Washington Examiner both mention that Trump cited national security concerns, but The Hill provides more detail on the legal arguments and expert opinions regarding the use of national security as a justification. NPR notes that CNN was blocked from Air Force One travel and that other outlets refused to replace them, while The Guardian and ABC News do not mention this detail. The Dispatch does not provide specific details about the ban or Blanche’s statements, only a general critique of the administration’s approach to press access.

Pope Leo criticizes legalization of assisted dying as "false compassion"

Pope Leo XIV condemned the legalization of medically assisted dying during a visit to Lourdes, France, calling it "false compassion" and emphasizing that what is legal is not necessarily moral. The pope addressed a large crowd at the shrine, urging them to reject the idea of causing death under the guise of compassion. He also visited a care center for sick and disabled pilgrims, encouraging those in pain to recognize their inherent dignity. During his visit, Leo met with seven survivors of clergy sexual abuse, acknowledging their painful testimonies and reaffirming his commitment to preventing future harm. The pope's remarks align with Catholic teaching that all human life must be protected from conception until natural death, opposing abortion, euthanasia, and assisted suicide.

In addition to his comments on assisted dying, Pope Leo addressed the issue of clergy sexual abuse during a private meeting with French bishops. He urged them to maintain "the greatest vigilance" in rooting out the "scourge" of abuse within the church. The pope also met with abuse survivors, including Jean-Marie Delbos, who described the encounter as "extraordinary." Leo's visit to Lourdes, a major pilgrimage site, included a large public Mass attended by hundreds of thousands of people, with many in wheelchairs, reflecting the diverse needs of the faithful.

Where the accounts differ

CBS News reports that Pope Leo met with seven survivors of clergy sexual abuse, including one who was abused as an adult, and quotes survivors Jérôme Guillement and Olivier Mardi. BBC and The Guardian also mention the meeting with survivors, with BBC noting that the gathering was behind closed doors and that the remarks were later released to reporters. Al Jazeera and The Guardian describe the meeting as emotional and touching, with survivors expressing hope for real change. FOX News includes a detailed account of a Canadian case involving assisted suicide, including a specific incident involving Brigitte Stegemann, which is not mentioned in other accounts. Straight Arrow News provides additional context on the legal expansion of assisted dying in France and the United States, including quotes from religious scholars.
