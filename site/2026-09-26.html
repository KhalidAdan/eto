SCOTUS Smacks Down MAGA Map of Missouri

The U.S. Supreme Court ruled on Friday that Missouri must use the 2022 congressional map for the 2026 midterm elections, blocking a Republican-backed 2025 map that would have given the GOP an additional seat. The decision came after months of legal battles over the redistricting plan, which was drawn under President Trump’s direction. The 2025 map, which split the heavily Democratic Kansas City area into multiple districts, was used in August primaries but was later invalidated by the Missouri Supreme Court due to a pending voter referendum. The Supreme Court’s final ruling reaffirmed the use of the older 6-2 map, which gives Republicans six seats and Democrats two.

The dispute began when Missouri Republicans, under Trump’s urging, redrew the congressional map in 2025 to secure an extra seat. The new map, which would have allowed Republicans to control seven of the state’s eight seats, was used in the August primary elections. However, the Missouri Supreme Court ruled that the map could not be used in the November general election without voter approval. The U.S. Supreme Court later blocked a federal appeals court’s attempt to reinstate the 2025 map, citing the risk of electoral chaos and the need to maintain the status quo.

Supreme Court allows Trump to use controversial database to check voter citizenship

The US Supreme Court has lifted restrictions on the Trump administration to use a controversial federal database that uses citizenship information and other sensitive data to identify voters suspected of being non-citizens. Critics have questioned the reliability of the data, warning it could result in US citizens being wrongly removed from electoral rolls. The Department of Homeland Security programme, known as Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements (Save), allows officials to verify the eligibility of voters on their rolls. The database was first created in 1986 to help officials verify the citizenship or immigration status of those applying for public benefits. The Trump administration expanded its search capabilities by allowing bulk searches using sensitive information from Social Security rolls. The use of the Save programme by individual states is voluntary.

The Supreme Court ruled that a 1996 law allows DHS to seek citizenship and immigration data from federal agencies, and has "an obligation to respond" to states seeking that information. The previous judge's order, the Supreme Court ruled, "prevents the federal government from using the programme it believes is best suited to the task". Voting rights groups called the ruling "profoundly disappointing". The League of Women Voters, the lead plaintiff in the case, said in a statement that the ruling "puts millions of Americans at risk of being unlawfully targeted by the administration's unreliable voter purge database weeks before the midterm elections".

Where the accounts differ

BBC reports that the Supreme Court ruled that the National Voter Registration Act prohibits broad purges of voter rolls in the weeks before an election, which limits any "potential impact" of the decision ahead of the midterms. Mother Jones states that the Supreme Court's conservative majority wrote that the government was likely to prevail on the merits of the case, overturning a district court order blocking the Trump administration’s use of the program. FOX News mentions that the immediate impact of Friday's order on the upcoming midterms is likely limited, citing the Associated Press. The Daily Wire notes that the Supreme Court’s decision came from the court’s six conservative justices. Just The News states that the stay is not a final ruling on the merits and that federal law also bars systematic voter-roll purges within 90 days of an election.

Sources BBC · Mother Jones · Axios · FOX News · The Daily Wire · Just The News

New Jersey lieutenant governor resigns after sexual harassment accusation

New Jersey Lieutenant Governor Dale Caldwell resigned on Friday after an investigation found credible evidence of sexual harassment and ethics violations. The report concluded that Caldwell’s behavior constituted sexual harassment and was derogatory or demeaning. Investigators found that Caldwell made a romantic advance toward a female staffer’s friend and then made a derogatory comment about women after being rebuffed. The report also found that Caldwell attempted to secure a promotion for his girlfriend without disclosing their relationship and brought personal guests to multiple ticketed events without reimbursement.

TikTok to pay Alabama $100m and limit teenage use in first state settlement

TikTok has agreed to pay Alabama at least $100m and implement restrictions for teenage users, avoiding a trial. The settlement includes a two-hour daily time limit for teens, restricted access from midnight to 6am, and suspending notifications during school hours. Alabama Attorney General Steve Marshall praised the deal as a victory for parents. The agreement mirrors safety provisions agreed by Meta, including age verification and banning beauty filters for teenagers. TikTok will also offer young users a non-personalised content feed. The settlement could increase to $300m if 40 other attorneys general sign similar agreements. Alabama’s suit is part of a broader wave of litigation against social media companies over alleged harm to young users.

TikTok’s settlement with Alabama includes a conditional restriction that Meta also agreed to: expanding the night-time shutdown period to 10pm to 7am if other platforms also commit to do the same. The company has previously settled cases against it, including a Los Angeles lawsuit and a Kentucky school district case. Going to trial would have exposed TikTok to public scrutiny of its safety practices. Alabama originally sued TikTok and its Chinese parent company ByteDance in April 2025, alleging the app was engineered to hook young users “just like a sophisticated gambling machine.” The state later narrowed its case to claims under Alabama’s Deceptive Trade Practices Act, focusing on whether TikTok misrepresented the effectiveness of features such as “Restricted Mode” and “Kids Mode.”

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian adds that the settlement could increase to $300m if conditions are not met, while CBS News states the $100m payment is due within 45 days and the additional $183.8m is contingent on other states reaching qualifying settlements. Al Jazeera mentions the potential for up to $300m but does not specify the conditions. The Washington Examiner does not mention the potential for additional payments. ICE wasted millions in rapid push to expand detention capacity, watchdog finds

A government watchdog report found that Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) wasted millions of taxpayer dollars in its rapid expansion of detention facilities to fulfill the Trump administration’s mass deportation agenda. The Government Accountability Office (GAO) criticized ICE for failing to conduct necessary analyses and planning, leading to significant financial waste. The report highlighted that ICE invested billions in six new initiatives without proper strategic planning, resulting in unnecessary costs and inefficiencies. The agency received over $75 billion in funding since Trump returned to office, including $45 billion through the One Big Beautiful Bill Act and $31 billion from the Secure America Act. The GAO report noted that ICE’s rush to expand detention capacity led to mismanaged projects, including the purchase of 11 warehouses for conversion into detention centers, which cost about $1.07 billion. Some of these warehouses were later sold, resulting in unrecoverable costs.

The GAO also pointed out that ICE failed to account for long-term operating costs of the facilities, leading to additional financial burdens. For example, the agency spent $20 million on nonrecoverable costs for seven warehouses it no longer plans to use. The report warned that selling these warehouses for less than the purchase price could result in further losses. At Guantanamo Bay, the Department of Defense spent $2.85 million on tents that were never used after they failed to meet detention standards. The facility, which was expected to hold up to 30,000 detainees, only had an average of 16 detainees daily in fiscal year 2026.

Where the accounts differ

Straight Arrow News reports that ICE spent $1 billion on 11 warehouses and that four are still in use, while Axios states that the total cost of the warehouses was $1.07 billion and that ICE lost $7.7 million in nonrecoverable costs. CBS News adds that $1.55 billion still sits with Chicago Title Insurance for acquiring detention centers. Axios also mentions that ICE spent $7.1 million on unneeded meals at Fort Bliss and that the bed rate at Alligator Alcatraz was $249 per day, 171% higher than ICE’s median rate. Straight Arrow News and CBS News both mention the $707 million purchase price for the warehouses, while Axios does not specify the exact cost.
