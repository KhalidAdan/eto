US Army to detonate 68 barrels of lead azide in Iowa amid legal and political delays

The US Army has announced it will proceed with a controlled detonation of 68 barrels of lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa, scheduled for Wednesday morning. The operation, delayed multiple times due to public outcry and legal challenges, involves the destruction of a stockpile of degraded lead azide, a highly volatile chemical used in munitions. The Army claims the material, stored for over two decades, has recrystallized and poses a significant risk of spontaneous detonation if moved. Officials say the detonation is the only viable option to prevent a more dangerous uncontrolled explosion.

The detonation is expected to release a plume of toxic lead dust over a 100-mile radius, with the exact path of the plume changing with each rescheduling. Residents within five miles are advised to evacuate, and those within 20 miles are told to stay indoors with windows closed. The Iowa Department of Health recommends staying indoors for 24-48 hours after the blast, conflicting with the timing of Thanksgiving, a major travel day. The Army has implemented mitigation measures, including installing a dam and using aqueous foam to absorb lead, following public pressure and court rulings.

Kilmar Ábrego García leaves US for Spain, ending Trump deportation battle

Kilmar Ábrego García, a Salvadoran national, left the United States for Spain on Tuesday. He departed on a commercial flight, ending a prolonged legal battle with the Trump administration. García was initially deported to El Salvador in March 2025 despite a 2019 court order barring his removal due to gang-related risks. The administration later admitted the deportation was an error and brought him back to the U.S., where he faced human trafficking charges that were later dismissed. The Trump administration continued efforts to deport him to other countries, including Uganda, Ghana, and Liberia. García had previously expressed willingness to be deported to Costa Rica, which agreed to accept him as a refugee. His departure marks the end of a 20-month legal struggle.

García, who is married to a U.S. citizen and lived in Maryland, came to the U.S. illegally from El Salvador as a teenager. His case was elevated to the Supreme Court, which ordered the government to facilitate his return. Federal prosecutors had alleged he transported hundreds of illegal migrants, but the case was dismissed by a federal judge in May 2026. García’s legal team stated his departure was "temporary" and that he had "many options to return to the United States," though they expect the next administration to prioritize his family reunification.

Trump pardons turkeys Freedom and Feathers during 2026 National Thanksgiving Turkey Pardon

President Donald Trump pardoned two turkeys, Freedom and Feathers, during the 2026 National Thanksgiving Turkey Pardon. The ceremony took place on the South Lawn of the White House, where Trump delivered remarks about the event. The turkeys were raised on a farm in Fort Seybert, West Virginia, and their names were selected through a White House public poll. Both turkeys will be sent to Texas A&M University for retirement. The event is part of an annual tradition that dates back to 1947, with the formal pardon tradition beginning in 1989 under President George H.W. Bush.

The National Turkey Federation, which has been involved in the tradition since 1947, stated that the turkeys were named through a public vote, with Freedom and Feathers beating out other options like "Commander and Chief" and "Honey and Ham." The turkeys spent Monday night at the Willard InterContinental Hotel in Washington, D.C., where they were provided with special accommodations, including a premium room with two queen beds and tailored room service. The turkeys will be cared for by students at Texas A&M University.

All 6 passengers found dead inside wreckage of plane off Mississippi coast

A small plane crashed off the Mississippi Gulf Coast on Tuesday, resulting in the deaths of all six people on board. Divers located the remains of the victims inside the aircraft's fuselage approximately 2 miles northeast of Horn Island, Mississippi, according to the U.S. Coast Guard. The victims included four adults and two boys, aged 8 and 10. The Cessna 210 was traveling from New Orleans Lakefront Airport to Page Field in Fort Myers, Florida, when it crashed. The plane was descending at 10,000 feet per minute when contact was lost, as reported by multiple outlets. Fort Hood shooter Nidal Hasan seeks stay of execution to challenge death sentence

Nidal Hasan, the former U.S. Army officer convicted of killing 13 people at Fort Hood in 2009, has filed a motion in civilian court to halt his execution. The motion, supported by the American Civil Liberties Union, seeks a stay so Hasan can file a habeas corpus petition challenging the constitutionality of his death sentence. Hasan is scheduled to be executed by an Army firing squad on Dec. 3 at Fort Hood, Texas. The execution would be the first military death sentence carried out in over six decades.

Hasan, who was convicted in 2013 of 13 counts of premeditated murder and 32 counts of attempted premeditated murder, remains on military death row at Fort Leavenworth, Kansas. His legal team argues that he must have the opportunity to pursue federal review of his case. The Army has also denied the request of three survivors of the 2009 shooting to witness the execution, citing regulations that define direct victims as those who were physically wounded by gunfire.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian and ABC News mention that the Army denied the request of three survivors to witness the execution, but only The Guardian and ABC News specify that the survivors were not physically wounded by gunfire.
