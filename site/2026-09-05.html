Lindsay Clancy Murder Trial Ends in Mistrial After Juror Deadlock

Lindsay Clancy, a Massachusetts mother accused of murdering her three children in January 2023, faced a jury that could not reach a unanimous verdict. The trial ended in a mistrial after jurors deadlocked for seven days. Clancy’s defense argued she suffered from postpartum psychosis, claiming she was not criminally responsible for her actions. Prosecutors maintained she was in control and therefore criminally liable. Clancy, who is paralyzed and in a wheelchair following her alleged suicide attempt after the killings, remains in psychiatric custody at Tewksbury State Hospital. The case has sparked national debate over mental health, the insanity defense, and the legal standards for criminal responsibility.

Where the accounts differ

The Daily Wire and FOX News both mention the 11-1 jury split, but the Daily Wire refers to the holdout as a "male juror," while FOX News states the defense attorney referred to the holdout as a "male inside and outside court." The Daily Wire also claims that the prosecution must prove sanity beyond a reasonable doubt, while CBS News and FOX News both note that Massachusetts law places the burden of proof on the prosecution to show criminal responsibility. The Washington Examiner reports that Republicans criticized the jury for not convicting Clancy, while the BBC and Newsweek focus on public reactions and the broader societal implications of the case. The Daily Wire and FOX News both describe the defense’s arguments, but the Daily Wire adds that the defense’s claim about medication interactions was weak, while CBS News and FOX News do not mention this point.

Sources The Daily Wire · Newsweek · FOX News · CBS News · BBC · Washington Examiner

Dozens of trapped hydropower tunnel workers in Nepal could still be alive, official says

Nepal authorities have said they are “confident” that dozens of workers in Nepal’s Rasuwagadhi hydropower plant are still alive, more than a week after deadly flash floods left them trapped in an underground chamber. The floods, triggered by the collapse of a melting glacier on the border between Nepal and China-controlled Tibet, caused widespread destruction, wiping away villages, bridges, and hydropower dams. At least 1,252 people have been confirmed dead from the floods, with more than 4,200 remaining missing. Rescue efforts have focused on hydropower tunnels, where authorities believe workers may have survived for over a week due to air pockets. Over 270 people have been rescued so far.

Rescuers in Nepal pulled two workers alive from a hydropower tunnel on Friday, nine days after the floods. Officials identified the two men as a mechanical foreman and a mechanical supervisor. The rescue at the Trishuli 3A tunnel brought the total number of survivors to three, including a Chinese national rescued on Saturday. At least 557 workers are missing from the Trishuli 3A hydropower project, with about 115 believed to be trapped in the main tunnel. Officials remain hopeful that more workers will be rescued, with ongoing efforts to locate survivors in other hydropower projects.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian reports that officials believe 46 people are still alive in an underground room at the Rasuwagadhi hydropower plant, while the Associated Press states that at least 900 workers are missing from 12 hydropower projects in Nepal, with roughly 500 believed to be trapped in tunnels. The Guardian also mentions that at least 639 workers were still missing from hydropower plants along the Bhote Koshi and Triushuli rivers, whereas the Washington Examiner and NPR report different numbers of missing workers. The Associated Press and BBC mention a Chinese national rescued from a hydropower tunnel, while the Guardian and NPR do not specify the nationality of the rescued workers. The Guardian and ABC News report that the death toll is at least 1,252, while the Associated Press and ABC News report a higher death toll of at least 1,300.

Sources The Guardian · NPR · ABC News · Washington Examiner · Associated Press · BBC

Midterm mail ballots start to go to voters as litigation creates uncertainty

Mail voting for this year’s midterm elections has begun in several states, including North Carolina, amid ongoing legal battles over President Donald Trump’s attempts to limit the practice. Election officials have stated that ballots will be mailed out as expected, despite the uncertainty caused by litigation. Trump has pushed to restrict mail voting, which he has falsely blamed for his 2020 loss, though he himself has voted by mail. The U.S. Supreme Court recently issued a procedural ruling in Trump’s favor, but did not address the merits of the case. Election officials have warned that confusion over the executive order and court fights has already affected some voters, but they have assured that mail voting remains a safe and secure process.

The Trump administration has asked the Supreme Court to lift a block on its new mail ballot rule for the U.S. Postal Service, which requires mail-in ballots to bear Election Mail markings and be sent in automation-compatible envelopes with unique barcodes. The rule, developed under Trump’s executive order, has been temporarily blocked by a federal judge, who argued that it would be too late in the election cycle to implement such changes without causing significant harm and confusion. The Postal Service’s rule has also been challenged by states and civil rights groups, who argue it infringes on states’ rights to administer elections.

Where the accounts differ

ABC News reports that North Carolina is scheduled to become the first state to widely distribute mail ballots, while Washington Examiner and The Daily Wire note that North Carolina is set to distribute ballots Friday. CBS News states that mail ballots will go out to voters in several states in mid-September, with North Carolina mailing its first ballots Friday. Straight Arrow News confirms that North Carolina is set to distribute ballots Friday, regardless of how the lawsuit proceeds. The Daily Wire mentions that states may voluntarily use the Postal Service’s new system, but none have announced plans to do so, while CBS News states that the Postal Service’s rule requires states to include specific design elements on mail ballot envelopes and submit voter information to an online portal.

Missouri's top court blocks new House map favoring GOP until voters can weigh in

The Missouri Supreme Court has unanimously blocked the state from moving forward with a new House map that favors Republicans in this year's midterm elections, ordering a statewide vote on whether to uphold or reject the new map. The decision marks a rare setback for Republicans in the national redistricting battle, as they fight to hold onto their slim House majority. The court placed the new districts on hold because citizens submitted petition signatures seeking a referendum. The legislation containing the new districts "did not go into effect and will not go into effect unless and until approved by the voters." As a result, the congressional map adopted after the 2020 census "remains in full force and effect for the November 2026 general election."

The new districts were used in the August primaries but cannot be used in the November general election. The court ruled that a constitutional provision allowing a referendum on "any act" of the legislature encompasses congressional redistricting. The ruling capped a year of political maneuvering and legal wrangling over the new districts. Missouri Attorney General Catherine Hanaway indicated her office will appeal to the Supreme Court, calling the decision a "full-fledged constitutional crisis."

Where the accounts differ

CBS News and The Guardian both state that the new districts were used in the August primaries, while The Washington Examiner and Al Jazeera mention that the new map was used in the primaries. The Daily Wire and The Guardian note that the new map would have shifted the House delegation to seven Republicans and one Democrat, while CBS News and The Washington Examiner do not specify the exact number of seats. The Daily Wire attributes the claim about the 7-1 advantage to the court's decision, while CBS News and The Guardian attribute it to the legislature's plan. The Daily Wire also mentions that the case was brought by Richard von Glahn, the executive director of "People Not Politicians," while CBS News and The Guardian do not name the plaintiff.

Excavation work on Trump's arch to begin over next 2 weeks: Interior secretary

The Trump administration announced that excavation work for the proposed 250-foot "triumphal arch" will begin over the next two weeks, according to Interior Secretary Doug Burgum. The project, located on Columbia Island between the Lincoln Memorial and Arlington National Cemetery, is part of a broader effort to leave a lasting mark on Washington, D.C. The National Park Service has endorsed the site for the project, though it noted potential adverse effects on historic sightlines. A lawsuit from three veterans and an architectural historian argues that the project lacks congressional authorization, and the Justice Department has moved to dismiss the case.

The National Capital Planning Commission has not yet issued final approval for the construction plan, and the National Park Service has not finalized its historic-preservation review. The administration claims the excavation is part of an archaeological survey, not actual construction, and that the work will not violate a court order requiring 14 days' notice before construction begins. The project is also under aviation review, with the Federal Aviation Administration still studying its impact on nearby airspace.

Where the accounts differ

CBS News and The Guardian also mention this, but CBS adds that the National Park Service has acknowledged potential adverse impacts on the landscape. Straight Arrow News and The New York Post do not mention the DOJ's position on the nature of the excavation work. US diesel prices hit a record high of $5.85 on average as the Iran war disrupts the flow of fuel

Diesel prices in the U.S. reached an all-time high of $5.85 per gallon, according to the American Automobile Association (AAA). This surge is attributed to the ongoing war with Iran, which has disrupted global fuel supplies. Higher diesel costs are expected to increase transportation expenses for goods, affecting consumers through higher prices for items like groceries, clothing, and furniture. The situation has also raised concerns about inflation and its impact on everyday costs.

The war has led to increased volatility in oil markets, with the Strait of Hormuz, a critical oil transit route, experiencing heightened tensions. This has contributed to higher crude oil prices, which in turn affect diesel costs. The Trump administration has been involved in efforts to secure oil supplies, including a deal with Venezuela, which aims to increase U.S. oil reserves and potentially lower fuel prices in the long term.

Where the accounts differ

The Associated Press and CBS News both report that diesel prices reached $5.85 per gallon, but CBS News adds that this is the highest since the Russia-Ukraine war, while the Associated Press notes it is the first time ever. The Guardian and Axios provide additional context about the impact on farmers and the broader economy, including the role of global refining capacity and the effect on agricultural costs. The Daily Wire and CBS News mention the Trump-Venezuela deal, while the Guardian and Axios highlight the role of the Strait of Hormuz and the impact on global oil supply. The BBC includes details about the U.S.-Israel war with Iran and mentions the role of Venezuela under Trump's administration, which is not explicitly stated in other accounts.

Sources Associated Press · BBC · The Guardian · Axios · The Daily Wire · CBS News

White House website debuts 5 retro arcade video games that promote Trump's agenda

The White House website unveiled a collection of five low-resolution video games on Thursday, each tied to different aspects of President Donald Trump’s agenda. The games, part of a new section called "Arcade," include "Build the Wall," a Tetris-style game that challenges players to stack sections of a border wall while protecting the country from what the game calls the "coming horde." Another game, "Rio Run," resembles the mobile favorite "Snake," with an icon resembling Trump running around gathering potential border crossers. "Supply Line" is a condensed version of the arcade game "Tapper," where players sort food along an assembly line and reject items that don't meet "Make America Healthy Again" standards. Two games take place over the skies of the nation's capital, including "Flappy Bill," where an eagle carries a bill across the National Mall. "Trump Savings Tycoon" allows players to collect money to "Fill your kids' Trump Accounts," a savings program offering $1,000 to every child born during Trump's term.

The White House announced the launch of the arcade on social media with animations parodying Xbox, PlayStation, and Nintendo loading screens. One such post featured the Sega logo turning into "MAGA," as in Trump's "Make America Great Again" slogan. The initiative is part of the administration's effort to engage a younger audience and promote its policies through interactive media.

Where the accounts differ

ABC News reports that "Trump Savings Tycoon" is part of the administration's savings plan offering $1,000 to every child born during Trump's term. Newsweek and Washington Examiner also mention this program, but Newsweek adds that more than six million Trump Accounts had already been requested as of July 6. Mother Jones does not mention the number of accounts requested, instead focusing on the dehumanizing nature of the games. Straight Arrow News and Al Jazeera do not mention the number of accounts requested or the savings program, while Al Jazeera highlights the backlash from critics who argue the games prioritize entertainment over pressing issues like rising costs and the Iran war.

Sources ABC News · Newsweek · Washington Examiner · Mother Jones · Straight Arrow News · Al Jazeera

Volkswagen announces it will cut 100,000 jobs by 2030

Volkswagen has approved plans to cut 100,000 jobs by 2030 as part of a restructuring effort to address financial challenges and competition. The company announced further cuts of 50,000 jobs and a staggered end to production at four German plants between 2031 and 2034. The restructuring includes a cost-cutting plan to reduce positions by 50,000 by 2030, bringing the total job losses to 100,000. The supervisory board acknowledged overcapacity in Europe for producing 500,000 vehicles with no market. The number of car models produced by the VW group will be cut by half. Volkswagen employs more than 650,000 people across its brands.

Volkswagen’s chief executive, Oliver Blume, described the plan as a “strong signal for the future” and emphasized the need to align workforce levels with economic realities. The plan was approved after a meeting with shareholders, unions, and state representatives. IG Metall, the company’s largest union, stated that concessions were made on both sides to avoid a dangerous escalation of the conflict. The deal includes a stay of execution for four plants threatened with closure in exchange for staff reductions. Blume also mentioned a “three-figure billion sum” investment to enhance the competitiveness of the company’s brands.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian states that Volkswagen will cut 100,000 jobs by 2030, with an additional 50,000 cuts announced this week, and that the total job losses will be the largest restructuring in the global automotive industry. The BBC reports that the 100,000 job cuts are the result of a plan approved in March for 50,000 cuts, with the current approval bringing the total to 100,000. The Guardian mentions that the number of car models produced by the VW group will be slashed by half, while the BBC states that the number of models will be cut by 50% and complexity reduced by 75%. The Guardian also notes that the total job cuts will amount to about 15% of the company’s employees, while the BBC does not specify the percentage. The Guardian mentions that the supervisory board acknowledged overcapacity in Europe for producing 500,000 vehicles with no market, while the BBC does not include this detail.

Sources The Guardian · BBC
