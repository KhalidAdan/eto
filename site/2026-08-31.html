U.S. strikes Iran to prevent Hormuz mine threat

The U.S. military struck Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island in the Strait of Hormuz to prevent the deployment of sea mines, according to a U.S. official. The strike marked the first known U.S. attack on Iran since late July. Iran retaliated by launching ballistic missiles at a U.S. base in Jordan, though no damage or casualties were reported. The Jordanian military intercepted eight missiles. The U.S. Central Command stated the strike was a "limited, precise action" against "minelaying forces posing an imminent threat." Oil prices surged following the strike, with Brent crude up 2.9% and U.S. crude up 2.7%. The Strait of Hormuz, a critical shipping route, remains closed to commercial traffic, though Iran and Oman agreed on a temporary maritime route.

The conflict has led to significant disruptions in oil trade, with Brent crude rising about 22% since the war began in February. The closure of the strait has affected global oil prices and exposed U.S. energy companies to risks, with some reporting reduced oil and gas supplies from the Gulf region. The U.S. strike is part of a broader military and economic pressure campaign against Iran, which includes sanctions and a naval blockade affecting Iran's oil exports.

Where the accounts differ

AP reports the U.S. strike occurred on the Strait of Hormuz, with oil prices surging 3% following the attack. Al Jazeera states the strike was in response to Iran’s closure of the strait and notes the rise in oil prices since February. Axios and Washington Examiner both mention the U.S. strike on Larak Island and the Iranian retaliation with missiles. BBC and Straight Arrow News report the strike killed two people and injured two others, while Axios and Washington Examiner attribute the strike to U.S. forces observing Iranian preparations for mine deployment. Al Jazeera and BBC provide more context on the economic impact and the role of U.S. energy companies in the region.

Sources Associated Press · Al Jazeera · Axios · BBC · Straight Arrow News · Washington Examiner

Number of missing in Nepal and Tibet rises to 3,000 as bad weather hampers search

A catastrophic flash flood along the Nepal-Tibet border has left at least 900 people dead and more than 3,000 missing, according to conflicting reports from Nepali and Chinese authorities. The disaster, triggered by a glacial collapse, devastated mountainous regions and caused widespread destruction. Rescue operations have been hampered by poor weather, with aerial rescues temporarily halted and ground teams struggling through mud and debris. Over 3,700 people have been rescued in Nepal, with efforts focused on hydropower tunnels where dozens of workers are believed to be trapped. International aid has been pledged, including from the U.S., India, and the EU, to support recovery efforts. Survivors describe losing everything, with many left homeless and without access to clean water or food.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian reports a combined death toll of 691 with more than 3,000 missing, while UPI and ABC News cite a higher death toll of at least 919. The Guardian states that Nepal’s disaster agency reported 675 dead and 2,498 missing, whereas UPI and ABC News note Nepal’s National Disaster Management Authority reported 903 bodies recovered and 4,247 missing. Al Jazeera and the Guardian mention 933 hydropower workers among the missing, while UPI and ABC News report 933 workers missing but with different numbers of total missing. The Guardian and Al Jazeera note that 517 foreigners are missing, while UPI and ABC News report 592 foreign nationals missing. The Guardian and Al Jazeera state that 261 foreigners are among those missing in Tibet, while UPI and ABC News do not specify the number of foreigners missing in Tibet. The Guardian and Al Jazeera report that Nepal has requested foreign help for specialized tasks but not search and rescue, while UPI and ABC News do not mention this request.

Sources The Guardian · Al Jazeera · Newsweek · UPI · NPR · ABC News

1 dead, around 15 missing after Grand Canyon hit by flash floods

A flash flood struck parts of Grand Canyon National Park over the weekend, resulting in the death of one person and the disappearance of at least 15 others. The National Park Service (NPS) confirmed the body of a 46-year-old man was recovered near Crystal Rapids on the Colorado River. The flood, which occurred on August 29, 2026, led to the evacuation of at least 62 people and caused extensive damage to infrastructure, including the destruction of footbridges over Bright Angel Creek. The NPS is seeking information about individuals who may have been in the affected areas during the flood, including those at Bright Angel Campground, Phantom Ranch, and the North Kaibab Trail.

The National Park Service reported that the flash flood was triggered by a significant amount of rainfall, with about half an inch to an inch of rain falling within half an hour in the area. The flooding also damaged the Transcanyon Waterline, a critical pipeline that supplies water to the park, leading to water restrictions and the closure of lodges and hotels within the park. The NPS is urging visitors to stay away from closed areas and is working to account for individuals who may have been in the affected regions.

Where the accounts differ

CBS News states that at least 15 people were still missing, though the figure is subject to change. Washington Examiner reports that the NPS is asking for information about more than 20 individuals who may be missing or unaccounted for. New York Post mentions that the NPS revised the number of missing people from 20 to 15. Straight Arrow News notes that more than 20 had been reported missing earlier, but the number was later adjusted. FOX News highlights the case of Dr. John Giusti, a Texas chiropractor, and his hiking group, who are among those unaccounted for, though the NPS has not publicly identified the individuals.

Sources CBS News · Washington Examiner · Just The News · New York Post · Straight Arrow News · FOX News

Google Maps Complies With Trump’s ‘Lake America’ Order

Google began displaying “Lake America” in place of “Lake Ontario” for U.S. users of Google Maps on Saturday, complying with an executive order President Donald Trump signed Thursday amid an escalating trade dispute with Canada. The order directs the Interior Department to update the U.S. Geographic Names Information System (GNIS), the federal database that governs official American map labels. Google stated its maps are updated “to reflect name changes in official government sources,” and that the change follows the company’s “long-standing policy for bodies of water with names that vary from country to country.” Under the new display rules, U.S. users see “Lake America,” Canadian users continue to see “Lake Ontario,” and users elsewhere see both names, with “Lake America” appearing in parentheses.

Ontario Premier Doug Ford responded by erecting a large sign along the Canadian shoreline reading “Lake Ontario — Now and Always,” criticizing Trump’s move. Ford stated the name would endure beyond the political moment, saying, “Long after President Trump is gone, it will still be called Lake Ontario.” Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney also rejected the rebranding, writing that “Canadians also know that naming reality means calling it Lake Ontario — then, now and always.” The lake, the smallest of the Great Lakes, sits on the border between New York and Ontario, with its surface area split roughly 52% Canada and 48% U.S.

Where the accounts differ

The Daily Wire reports that MapQuest said it won’t adopt the new name at all, instead rolling out a tool letting users rename the lake to whatever they choose. UPI and CBS News do not mention this detail. Straight Arrow News and Newsweek include information about the Seneca Nation leader criticizing Trump’s decision, while The Daily Wire and BBC attribute the statement to J. Conrad Seneca. UPI and CBS News mention the 50% tariffs imposed by the U.S. on Canadian goods, while The Daily Wire and BBC note that Canada will implement “dollar for dollar” counter-tariffs. The Daily Wire and BBC mention Trump’s AI-generated videos, which other accounts do not.

Sources The Daily Wire · UPI · Straight Arrow News · CBS News · Newsweek · BBC

US, Venezuela tout ‘biggest oil deal in world history’

President Donald Trump announced a sweeping oil agreement with Venezuela, calling it the "biggest oil deal in world history." According to Trump, the deal would give the U.S. majority control over more than 65 billion barrels of Venezuela’s proven oil reserves. Venezuelan interim President Delcy Rodríguez praised the agreement, calling it a "historic milestone" and stating it would bring "a significant flow of investment" for Venezuela’s hydrocarbons industry. The deal is expected to develop 17 strategic oil fields, with a production target of more than 1.5 million barrels per day over 25 years. Trump claimed the agreement would "substantially lower Gas Prices for all Americans, long into the future."

The agreement, negotiated by Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, is said to involve private business interests. A U.S. official told The Associated Press that the U.S. would have a 55% effective interest in the output of a new private company managing the reserves. The deal comes amid high global oil prices and a depleted U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve, which has fallen below 300 million barrels, its lowest level in over four decades.

Where the accounts differ

Straight Arrow News claims the deal "MORE THAN DOUBLES American Oil Reserves," a statement not found in any other account. Fox Business reports the deal targets 1.5 million barrels per day and could generate $200 billion in revenue for Venezuela, while UPI and Washington Examiner mention the U.S. will use Venezuelan oil to "fill up" the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. NPR notes that the U.S. does not have a national oil company and questions how the deal will operate. Fox News states the U.S. will have a 55% effective interest in the output of a new private company, a detail not mentioned in Straight Arrow News or UPI.

Sources Straight Arrow News · Fox Business · UPI · Washington Examiner · FOX News · NPR

El-Sayed apologizes for comments responding to attack on Michigan synagogue

Abdul El-Sayed, the Democratic nominee for U.S. Senate in Michigan, apologized to the state's Jewish Democratic Caucus on Saturday for comments he made after an attack on a synagogue in March. El-Sayed tied the attack to Israel's actions in the Middle East and said his comments may have been misconstrued to justify the attacker's actions. He expressed regret for any pain his remarks may have caused and emphasized that the attack was condemned outright. The synagogue attack occurred in March when a man rammed his car into the building and opened fire on a security guard before dying by suicide. The attacker, Ayman Mohammad Ghazali, had lost four relatives in an Israeli airstrike on Lebanon the week prior.

El-Sayed faced criticism for his statement that "hurt people hurt people," which some argued appeared to contextualize or excuse the attacker's actions. He acknowledged that he should have stopped after condemning the attack and admitted that offering context was not the right approach at the time. El-Sayed's apology was part of his effort to unify Democrats after a contentious primary and to move past controversies involving allegations of antisemitism, including his association with Hasan Piker. The Senate race is seen as a key contest for control of the Senate following the 2026 midterms.

Where the accounts differ

ABC News and CBS News both report that El-Sayed apologized for comments he made after the synagogue attack, which he tied to Israel's actions. Newsweek adds that the apology comes amid new polling suggesting the Senate race remains competitive and includes details about the FBI's involvement in the attack, noting that Ghazali was inspired by Hezbollah. Washington Examiner states that El-Sayed was criticized for linking Ghazali's actions to military tensions between Israel and Lebanon and includes a quote from El-Sayed about "hurt people hurt people." FOX News focuses on El-Sayed's campaign ties to Hasan Piker and shared donors with Dan Bilzerian, omitting details about the synagogue attack and El-Sayed's apology. Axios provides a detailed account of El-Sayed's apology and his refusal to distance himself from Piker, including quotes from El-Sayed and references to Piker's controversial remarks.

Sources ABC News · CBS News · Newsweek · Washington Examiner · FOX News · Axios

One person killed, five wounded in shooting at rave in Switzerland

A shooting occurred at a rave in Aarau, northern Switzerland, during the early hours of Sunday, leaving one person dead and five others injured. The incident took place at the Schachen horse racing track, where the annual techno party "Aarau Rave VOL. 7" was being held. Police confirmed that a 22-year-old woman died at the scene, while four men and one woman, aged between 24 and 27, were taken to the hospital with injuries ranging from minor to severe. All victims are residents of Switzerland, though Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni identified the deceased as Italian citizen Maria Spinelli and noted that two other injured individuals were also Italian. A large-scale police operation is ongoing to locate the perpetrators, who remain unknown.

Authorities were alerted to the shooting around 2:30 a.m. local time, with witnesses initially mistaking the sounds for fireworks. The shooting occurred during the rave, which had attracted approximately 3,500 attendees. Police have not yet determined the motive or exact circumstances of the incident, though they have ruled out the possibility that the suspects pose a threat again. The Swiss government has deployed a significant police presence, including armed officers and an army helicopter, as part of the investigation.

Where the accounts differ

Al Jazeera and UPI both mention that the shooting occurred before 3 a.m., while BBC and CBS News specify it was around 2:30 a.m. Al Jazeera and UPI state that the victims were all Swiss residents, whereas Associated Press and CBS News note that two of the injured were Italian citizens, as identified by Giorgia Meloni. UPI and ABC News mention that cartridge casings indicated at least two calibers of ammunition were used, suggesting multiple weapons, while Al Jazeera and BBC do not specify the type of firearms used. Associated Press and CBS News report that the police ruled out a terrorist attack, citing that the rave was already winding down, a claim not explicitly made by Al Jazeera or UPI.

Sources Al Jazeera · Associated Press · ABC News · BBC · UPI · CBS News

Russian drone strike near Kyiv kills 37, damages 50 homes

A Russian drone strike hit an ammunition depot near Kyiv on Friday, killing at least 37 people and injuring 42 others, including four children. The attack occurred in the village of Myla, 15 miles outside central Kyiv, and caused widespread destruction, including damage to at least 50 residential buildings and a care home for elderly and disabled people. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy condemned the storage of explosives near civilian areas, stating criminal proceedings had been opened for "official negligence." The strike followed similar incidents in recent months, including a July attack on a depot in Vyshneve that killed 22 people. Local officials reported that the attack sparked a fire and explosions that lasted for several hours, with rescue operations ongoing.

Volodymyr, a security guard in Myla, described the aftermath of the strike, noting that the village's garden and home were severely damaged. He and his family, who had no electricity or water after the attack, found some surviving vegetables to share. Vira, 65, and her son Serhii, who hid in a bunker during the attack, blamed those who stored the ammunition in their village. They reported finding a missile shell, several mines, and cluster munitions on their property after the explosions subsided.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian reports that 38 people died in the attack, with four still missing, while NPR and UPI state the death toll is at least 37. The Guardian also mentions that 30 of the dead were from a care home, while BBC and NPR cite 34 from the same facility. UPI and BBC note that a local official, Taras Didych, was killed while helping at the scene, which is not mentioned in the Guardian or NPR accounts. The Guardian and BBC describe the warehouse as storing "explosive objects," while NPR and UPI specify it as an ammunition depot. The Guardian and BBC mention that the warehouse was owned by the Ukrainian military, a detail not included in NPR or UPI reports.

Sources The Guardian · NPR · UPI · Straight Arrow News · BBC

Rising deaths in Nepal floods force urgent aid requests, endangering thousands' health and safety.

Official asks for assistance preserving bodies as number of deaths in Nepal floods rises

Rescue efforts in Nepal continue after devastating floods, with the death toll rising to 675 and 2,498 people missing, according to Nepal’s Disaster Management Authority. At least seven were killed in Tibet, and 554 are missing. The Independent Power Producers’ Association reported that almost 900 Nepali employees working on hydropower projects are unaccounted for, with 361 rescued. Nepal Electricity Authority stated that at least 12 hydropower projects are out of action due to flooding, and another 15 under construction are affected. Mass burials have started in several districts, but preserving bodies has become difficult. The Nepali foreign minister, Shisir Khanal, requested assistance from foreign governments to obtain freezers for preserving victims’ bodies, citing the risk of fast decomposition due to bodies being in water for a long time. A preliminary assessment by Chinese and Nepalese experts identified the disaster as triggered by the collapse of a high-altitude glacier in Nepal. The Chinese Foreign Ministry noted that rising temperatures are accelerating glacier melt in the Himalayas, increasing the risk of collapses and sudden flooding. Khanal also addressed the growing threat from climate change in the Himalayas, speaking with Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi, who agreed to increase the sharing of real-time hydrological data.

Where the accounts differ

Straight Arrow News reports that the death toll rose by more than 50 people over six hours, though there could be more. This detail is not included in the NPR account. Straight Arrow News also mentions that Dr. Sameer Kumar Adhikari, joint spokesperson of the Ministry of Health and Food Hygiene in Nepal, stated that body management has begun in several districts, including Chitwan and East Nawalparasi. NPR does not include this information. Additionally, Straight Arrow News states that teams from India and China arrived in Nepal on Saturday to assist in rescuing workers, a detail not present in the NPR account.

Sources Straight Arrow News · NPR
