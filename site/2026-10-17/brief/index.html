Katie Zacharia’s short tenure as White House press secretary ends amid legal credential scrutiny

Katie Zacharia was appointed White House press secretary in late October 2026, replacing Karoline Leavitt, and served for less than five days before being replaced. Her tenure was marked by immediate controversy over her legal credentials, as she claimed to have passed the bar exam while living in China. During her first press briefing, Zacharia was asked where she passed the bar, and she responded, “When I moved to China,” which led to confusion and criticism. Zacharia later told the New York Times she had misheard the question and thought it was asking about her location after law school. The Wall Street Journal reported that Zacharia failed the California bar exam after graduating from law school in 2011. The State Bar of California confirmed there was no record of her being licensed under either her married or maiden name. Zacharia’s LinkedIn profile lists her as an associate at a Beijing law firm from 2011 to 2013 and as general counsel for Fix California. The White House defended her qualifications, with communications director Steven Cheung calling her “uniquely qualified” for the role.

Where the accounts differ

The Washington Examiner and The Hill both note Zacharia’s short tenure and her initial press briefing, but The Hill provides more detail on the timeline of her appointment and the immediate fallout. The Guardian and CBS News both report that Zacharia admitted she never passed the bar exam, though CBS News adds that she claimed she didn’t need to pass it to work in China. The Guardian also mentions Zacharia’s LinkedIn profile gap and her work with Beachbody, which CBS News does not include. The Slate article characterizes Zacharia’s performance as “clumsy” and “embarrassing,” which the other outlets do not explicitly state. The New York Post and The Hill focus on the broader context of Trump’s administration and the political implications of Zacharia’s appointment, while the other accounts focus more on her legal credentials and performance.

Sources Washington Examiner · The Guardian · CBS News · The Hill · New York Post · Slate

Elon Musk donates over $158 million to Texas Senate GOP super PAC

Elon Musk donated $158 million to the Senate Leadership Fund (SLF), a major Republican super PAC, in September 2026, according to Federal Election Commission filings. The donation accounted for more than half of SLF’s fundraising in the third quarter. The funds were quickly transferred to a Texas-based PAC supporting Ken Paxton, the Republican Senate candidate. Musk also contributed over $80 million to his own political group, America PAC, during the same period. The Texas Senate race is one of the most expensive and closely watched contests in the 2026 midterms, with over $500 million in ad spending reported so far. Democratic candidate James Talarico raised $64 million in the third quarter, the largest amount by any Senate candidate in history, according to Politico.

Musk’s donations have intensified the already tight race, with polls showing Talarico leading by a narrow margin. Talarico, a progressive candidate, has focused on Paxton’s history of corruption and has gained support from liberal voters. Paxton, backed by Trump and the GOP establishment, faces significant challenges as Democrats have outspent Republicans in the race. Musk’s involvement has raised questions about the influence of private wealth in politics, with some outlets noting the scale of his contributions compared to previous election cycles.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian and CBS News both report Musk donated $158 million to SLF, but CBS News adds that Musk gave $75 million on Sept. 9 and $83 million two weeks later, totaling $158 million. The Guardian does not specify the timing of the donations. The Daily Caller and Axios both mention Talarico raised $64 million in the third quarter, but the Daily Caller adds that Talarico raised over $134 million since launching his campaign in September 2025. The Guardian and CBS News attribute the $64 million figure to Politico, while the Daily Caller cites Politico directly. The Guardian and Axios report Musk’s team and SLF found Paxton’s chances of winning were dwindling, leading Musk to step in, but CBS News does not mention this analysis. The Guardian and Axios also note Musk’s 2025 prediction to reduce political spending, which CBS News and The Daily Caller omit.

Sources NPR · The Guardian · CBS News · Axios · The Daily Caller · Mother Jones

Trump: White House didn't impede satellite system in Ukraine

President Trump claimed Friday that his administration did not disrupt an Elon Musk-owned satellite system used by Ukraine to wage war against Russia. The restrictions imposed earlier this week on the system hampered the Ukrainian military's ability to intercept Russian attack drones for 24 hours. Ukrainian officials remain highly skeptical of Trump's claims and believe someone in the U.S. government participated in throttling the systems. The Ukrainian military uses SpaceX's Starlink satellite communications system to control and operate its reconnaissance and intercept drones. When Starlink doesn't operate at full capacity, Ukraine's ability to gather intelligence, use interceptor drones and conduct long-range strikes against Russia is impeded. Ukraine relies on two satellite systems: One is SpaceX's Starlink, which it uses for communications, ground forces, logistics and defensive drones — ones that shoot down incoming drones. The other is Starshield, which is controlled by the U.S. government and used for offensive military purposes. Both systems were restricted, according to Ukraine.

Where the accounts differ

Axios reports that Trump said the White House did not restrict the satellite system, while the New York Post states that Trump denied ordering an alleged slowdown of the US-provided internet services to Ukraine’s military. Straight Arrow News and the Guardian both mention that Zelenskyy questioned U.S. support and that Starlink access had not been fully restored. The Christian Science Monitor and BBC report that the U.S. ceased intelligence sharing with Ukraine, which led to a lack of early warnings for Russian missile attacks. The Guardian also states that Trump denied being responsible for the slowdown, while the New York Post mentions that the Telegraph reported SpaceX CEO Elon Musk pitched the idea of a slowdown to Trump and that the president approved. The New York Post also notes that an administration official told them the White House gave no such instructions, insisting it was Pentagon freelancing.

Sources Axios · New York Post · Straight Arrow News · Christian Science Monitor · BBC · The Guardian

Trump waives Russia sanctions he signed into law

President Donald Trump signed an executive order on Friday to waive new sanctions and tariffs on Russia, which he had previously signed into law. The move came two days before the sanctions were set to take effect and a week after announcing a diesel deal with Russian President Vladimir Putin. The law, named after late Senator Lindsey Graham, was passed by Congress and aimed to impose economic pressure on Russia by sanctioning officials, financial institutions, and energy exports. Trump claimed the waiver was in the national interest.

The sanctions law included provisions for tariffs of up to 100 percent on major importers of Russian oil and gas, with exceptions for countries importing less than 15 percent of Russia’s natural gas. It also targeted Russian officials and a shadow fleet of tankers used to circumvent sanctions. Trump’s decision to suspend the sanctions has drawn criticism from both Democrats and Republicans, with some lawmakers accusing him of capitulating to Moscow and undermining support for Ukraine.

Russian envoy Kirill Dmitriev called the waiver “an important and truly historic moment,” while Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy expressed disappointment, stating the sanctions could have helped “enormously” in pressuring Russia to end the war. Zelenskyy also highlighted recent Russian attacks on Ukrainian energy infrastructure, which he said were aimed at causing a blackout ahead of winter.

Where the accounts differ

Al Jazeera and Newsweek both mention the diesel deal with Putin and the suspension of sanctions, but only Newsweek specifies that the order created and then blocked a 500 percent tariff on Russian goods and a potential 100 percent tariff against major buyers of Russian oil and gas. BBC and Fox Business both note the diesel deal and the waiver of tariffs under the Graham Act, but only Fox Business mentions the specific provision about countries that knowingly bought Russian oil 30 days after the law went into effect. ABC News and Washington Examiner both state that Trump signed the order waiving the sanctions, but only Washington Examiner refers to the law as the “Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act.”

Sources Al Jazeera · BBC · Washington Examiner · Fox Business · ABC News · Newsweek

Federal agents raided Rep. Ilhan Omar’s DC home amid immigration and financial investigations

Federal agents with Homeland Security Investigations (HSI) raided the Washington, D.C., home of Rep. Ilhan Omar, D-Minn., early Monday morning, according to a source familiar with the operation. The raid included the seizure of Omar’s cellphone, notebooks, personal computers, and passport. The Department of Justice described the action as part of an ongoing federal criminal investigation. U.S. Attorney Jeanine Pirro confirmed her office is investigating Omar’s financial disclosures, while separate reporting indicated federal investigators had subpoenaed Omar’s relatives as part of an immigration-related probe. Omar has denied wrongdoing and called the investigations “complete shams.” The raid occurred just three weeks before the November midterms, raising concerns about the timing of the probe.

Rep. Ilhan Omar, a Somali-born refugee who became a U.S. citizen in 2000, has faced scrutiny over her immigration records, including whether she lied about her age when she arrived in the U.S. in 1995. The Trump administration has alleged she or her late father lied about her age on her citizenship application, with some accounts suggesting her birth year was adjusted from 1981 to 1982. Omar’s husband, Tim Mynett, has dismissed rumors that she fled the country after the raid, stating he has “no concerns” about what federal authorities will find. The investigation into her financial disclosures includes questions about her reported net worth, with Trump suggesting her accountant may have reported fraudulent activities.

Where the accounts differ

The Nation claims the raid was part of an effort to “strip the Minnesota congresswoman of her American citizenship and deport her from the US,” while The Intercept states the raid was part of an “ongoing effort by the Trump administration to strip the Minnesota Democrat of her U.S. citizenship.” The New York Post reports that Omar’s birth year was adjusted from 1981 to 1982 in the Minnesota Legislative Reference Library, a detail not mentioned in the other accounts. The Nation also attributes the characterization of the raid as an attack on immigrants and people of color to the Trump administration, while the Intercept attributes the characterization of DHS as a weapon to Rep. Delia Ramirez. The New York Post mentions Omar’s husband’s comments about her net worth, which are not included in the other accounts.

Sources Mother Jones · The Nation · The Intercept · FOX News · New York Post · BBC

FBI investigates letters with ‘unknown substance’ sent to US election officials

Federal authorities are investigating suspicious mailings sent to state election officials and congressional offices, some of which contained a powdery substance. The FBI and US Postal Inspection Service are working to trace the origin of the letters, which were sent to officials in Louisiana, Oklahoma, Ohio, and other states. Some packages were intercepted before reaching their intended recipients, while others prompted evacuations and hazmat responses. Officials have urged recipients to treat the mail as potentially hazardous. The incidents come just weeks before the US midterm elections, raising concerns about the safety of election workers and lawmakers.

The FBI confirmed it is investigating a series of suspicious mailings sent to various addresses nationwide, including Secretary of State offices and their related election officers. Some of the letters contained an unknown substance, and the agency is working with law enforcement partners to identify those responsible. The US Postal Inspection Service referred the Guardian to the FBI, which declined to comment on the nature of the substance. In some cases, the powder was later identified as non-hazardous, such as baking powder in an Illinois case.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian and New York Post both mention that the FBI referred the Guardian to the FBI, while the New York Post also notes that the FBI declined to comment on the nature of the substance. The New York Post and FOX News report that the suspicious letters were mailed with a return address linked to the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA), which has since moved out of the building. The Guardian and BBC do not mention the DSA return address. The Guardian and FOX News both report that Chris Murphy and Alex Padilla were targeted, while the BBC and New York Post also include this information. The New York Post and FOX News state that the FBI has not confirmed the substance, while the Guardian and BBC note that some packages were identified as non-hazardous.

Sources The Guardian · NPR · BBC · New York Post · FOX News

U.S. President Donald Trump condemned Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky for resuming strikes on Russian oil facilities, calling it a "disgrace." Zelensky also criticized Trump for easing sanctions on Russian diesel and for restricting access to the Starlink satellite network, which is critical for Ukrainian drone operations. The Ukrainian leader emphasized that targeting Russian oil facilities was a strategic move to weaken Moscow's war effort.

Zelensky has been vocal about his frustration with Trump's energy ceasefire, which he said was not agreed to by Russia. He told the Financial Times that the pause on attacking refineries was finished, adding, "We're done." Meanwhile, Trump claimed that Zelensky was not interested in settling the war and that he was more difficult to deal with than Russian President Vladimir Putin. Zelensky, in turn, criticized Trump for his relationship with Putin, suggesting that the U.S. president had an affinity for autocrats.

At least 33 pupils and teacher killed after school roof collapses in Pakistan

A school roof collapsed in the north-western Pakistani city of Peshawar, killing at least 33 pupils and a teacher, according to hospital officials. Emergency services were called to the private Al-Khair school in Dir Colony at 08:48 (03:48 GMT) on Thursday after lessons had begun. The roof of an upper-floor classroom collapsed, causing ceilings below to cave in and trap children beneath the debris. Rescue officials reported that the school building had been constructed on a temporary basis, and that classrooms on three floors had been brought down. The school is on a very narrow street, making it difficult for rescue workers to reach the site.

Rescuers pulled more than 20 children from the rubble, with some in critical condition. An English teacher from a nearby school said he personally pulled out six children, most of whom were alive and talking but later found to have passed away. A local resident, Sohail, said he and others tried to remove children from under the rubble using shovels. Peshaw, police chief Mian Saeed stated that the entire structure, which had "substandard" roof slabs, was "overloaded" and beyond the limit of how much weight it could bear.

Where the accounts differ

BBC reports that at least 33 pupils and a teacher were killed, while Straight Arrow News states that at least 28 people are dead, with 27 students and one teacher confirmed dead. BBC mentions that more than a dozen others are injured, with some in critical condition, while Straight Arrow News reports that rescuers have pulled at least 45 children from the debris, some in critical condition. BBC attributes the collapse to a roof of an upper-floor classroom causing ceilings below to cave in, while Straight Arrow News cites a rescuer saying the roof of an upper floor gave way, bringing down the ceiling below and causing both stories to collapse. BBC states that the school building was constructed on a temporary basis, while Straight Arrow News does not mention the construction type.

Sources BBC · Straight Arrow News
