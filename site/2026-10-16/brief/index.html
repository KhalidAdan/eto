Consumer inflation rose faster than expected in September, CPI report shows

The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) reported that the consumer price index (CPI) increased 0.6% on a monthly basis in September, up from 0.4% in August, and is up 3.7% from a year ago. Energy prices rose 5.2% monthly and 20.4% annually, with gasoline prices increasing 9.1% from the prior month and 34.2% from last year. Core CPI, which excludes volatile energy and gas prices, rose 0.3% monthly and 2.6% annually, both higher than economists' expectations. The BLS noted that gasoline accounted for over half of the monthly rise in the headline CPI. Inflation has created severe financial pressures for U.S. households, particularly lower-income Americans, who spend a larger share of their income on necessities.

Where the accounts differ

Fox Business reports that core CPI rose 0.3% monthly and 2.6% annually, while Mother Jones states that the September CPI increase was the largest gain in five months. The Guardian notes that the September CPI reading was not yet published when its polling was conducted, and that 38% of Republicans believed the economy was worsening. Newsweek and Axios both report that Trump's approval rating on inflation is at a new low, 22%, while Fox Business and CBS News mention that the annual CPI figure came in hotter than the estimate of 3.6%. The Guardian and CBS News attribute the rise in energy prices to the war in Iran and Russia's war with Ukraine, while Fox Business and Mother Jones cite the war in Iran as the cause.

Children Among Dead In Oregon Elementary School Shooting, Bystander Takes Out Gunman

A shooting at Silver Rail Elementary School in Bend, Oregon, resulted in the deaths of at least four people, including three children and an adult teacher. The 16-year-old suspect, who was a Bend resident, was shot and killed by a community member before police arrived. The incident began around 12:59 p.m. when police received a call from a driver who reported being shot at multiple times by someone with a long gun. The suspect forced his way into the school by shooting at locked doors. Police arrived at the scene within three minutes, finding the suspect dead and the victims already accounted for. A reunification center was set up at a local church for families. Oregon Gov. Tina Kotek praised the community member who intervened, calling their actions heroic.

Trump calls off plan to livestream Fort Hood shooter's execution

President Trump announced Thursday that the execution of Fort Hood shooter Nidal Hasan will not be livestreamed, reversing a plan proposed by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. Trump stated that while he believed streaming the execution might serve as a deterrent, many people would prefer not to watch. The decision came after significant backlash from both Republicans and Democrats, who warned that livestreaming could fuel radicalization. Hasan, who was convicted of killing 13 people and injuring more than 30 at Fort Hood in 2009, is set to be executed by firing squad on Dec. 3. The Pentagon had previously confirmed the execution would be streamed, but Trump's reversal has ended that plan.

Survivors of the 2009 attack praised Trump's decision, saying it prevents the execution from becoming a spectacle or a propaganda tool for terrorist groups. Alonzo Lunsford, a victim of Hasan's attack, called the decision a "positive move" and emphasized that the execution should not be made public. Julia Wilson, another survivor, expressed concern that livestreaming could traumatize others and that the footage might be accessed by children online. Trump also noted that the immediate families of the victims will be allowed to view the execution in person.

9th Circuit Court Smacks Down California Law Unmasking ICE Agents For Violence-Hungry Leftists

A federal appellate court ruled on Wednesday that California’s No Vigilantes Act, which required federal law enforcement officers to display identification while operating in the state, violates the U.S. Constitution’s Supremacy Clause. The 9th Circuit Court of Appeals, in a unanimous decision, held that the law directly regulates federal operations without congressional authorization, making it void. The ruling reverses a lower court’s denial of a preliminary injunction and sends the case back to the lower court to enter an injunction prohibiting California from enforcing the law against federal officials.

The Trump administration had previously sought to block the law, arguing it infringes on federal authority. The court affirmed that the Trump administration is “certain to succeed” on the merits of its supremacy clause argument. The decision comes amid ongoing tensions between ICE agents and anti-ICE activists, including a shooting incident in Dallas where an individual with anti-ICE engravings opened fire on an ICE vehicle, killing two people.

Where the accounts differ

The New York Post and Axios do not mention this specific procedural outcome. The Federalist also notes that the law took effect on Jan. 1 and was part of California’s No Secret Police Act, which banned federal officers from wearing masks during operations. The other accounts do not specify the effective date or mention the No Secret Police Act. Houthis Launch Attacks on Saudi Infrastructure as Trump Considers U.S. Involvement

Iran-backed Houthis have intensified attacks on Saudi Arabia, targeting airports, oil facilities, and other infrastructure. At least 20 people were killed and 356 injured in Houthi attacks on Saudi airports between October 6 and October 11, according to the Saudi General Authority of Civil Aviation. The deadliest attack struck King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh on October 10, killing at least 12 people, including an American citizen, and injuring more than 300. The attacks have disrupted air travel and raised questions about Saudi Arabia’s ability to defend its territory. President Donald Trump has said the United States is considering joining Saudi Arabia’s strikes against the Houthis, though he has not confirmed the decision. The Houthis have warned that U.S. involvement would trigger a “swift and painful response” targeting American interests.

The conflict between the Houthis and Saudi Arabia dates back to the early 2000s, with tensions escalating in 2014 when the group seized Yemen’s capital, Sanaa. Saudi Arabia launched a military intervention in 2015 to restore the government, leading to a war that killed tens of thousands and caused a major humanitarian crisis. A 2022 truce reduced fighting, but the Houthis declared it over in July amid the U.S.-Iran war, resuming attacks on vessels in the Red Sea and eventually striking Saudi Arabia directly. The group has also demanded an end to Saudi military operations in Yemen and compensation for damage caused by the Saudi-led intervention.

Nine Major Banks ‘Denied, Debanked, and Discriminated Against’ Conservatives, Trump Admin Probe Finds

US regulator accuses nation's largest banks of debanking customers over political views

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) released a report alleging that nine major banks denied or restricted financial services to customers based on their political or religious beliefs. The report, issued under an executive order signed by President Donald Trump in 2025, found that between 2020 and September 2025, all nine banks had policies that could lead to discriminatory banking decisions. The report cited instances where banks closed accounts of merchants promoting President Trump and the Make America Great Again (MAGA) brand, and declined services to organizations with partisan leanings. The OCC stated that fair access to financial services is a mandate, not a mantra.

The report also highlighted that banks used subjective reputation-risk policies to evaluate customers, allowing employees to consider political affiliations and public controversy when making financial decisions. Some banks restricted services to lawful businesses involved in firearms manufacturing, coal mining, and other industries deemed to have reputational risks. The OCC warned that while banks have since changed their policies, there is no guarantee they will not return to past practices.

Where the accounts differ

Fox Business reports that the OCC found a payment processor owned by one bank closed the account of a merchant selling MAGA-branded merchandise in February 2021, and another bank declined services to a public education organization due to its partisan leanings. The Daily Wire states that the Biden administration pushed banks to target conservative and religious groups, and that the report found banks denied service to customers whose political or religious affiliations caused “reputation risk.” The Daily Caller notes that PNC declined a line of credit to a media company due to its owners’ conservative political affiliations, and that JPMorgan modified internal records to remove politically charged descriptions. The Federalist highlights that Bank of America canceled the account of the Timothy Two Project International in 2020, and that the OCC report is an indictment of the left’s cancel culture. The Free Press states that the OCC found that banks had policies in place between 2020 and 2025 that may have influenced their decisions to deny or limit access to financial services based on political or religious beliefs.

Sources Fox Business · The Daily Wire · The Daily Caller · The Federalist · The Free Press
