Ex-US Attorney will lead independent review of Christa Pike botched execution

Tennessee Gov. Bill Lee appointed former US attorney Ed Stanton to lead an independent investigation into the botched execution of death row inmate Christa Pike. The review follows the failed attempt to execute Pike on Wednesday, during which she received two doses of pentobarbital and survived. Pike, who was convicted of murdering her 19-year-old classmate Colleen Slemmer in 1995, remains unconscious and intubated in a Nashville hospital. The state halted all executions in Tennessee for nearly three years after the botched attempt, including the case of Oscar Smith, and resumed executions in February 2025 with a new single-dose protocol. Commissioner Frank Strada resigned after the failed execution, calling the review “entirely appropriate and necessary.”

The new lethal injection protocol, introduced by Strada, uses a single dose of pentobarbital instead of a three-drug cocktail. This change was prompted by a 2022 investigation into Tennessee’s lethal injection protocol, which found that drugs used in prior executions were not being tested properly. Pike’s attorneys claim she experienced intense pain during the injection and developed blisters and burns around the injection site, suggesting the IV may not have been properly connected. The state’s execution team reportedly used at least seven needles in Pike’s left arm during the attempt to establish an IV line.

Where the accounts differ

The New York Post and The Hill both report that Pike’s attorneys claim she experienced intense pain and developed blisters and burns around the injection site, suggesting the IV may not have been properly connected. The Washington Examiner and Axios do not mention these specific details. The Federalist and BBC do not discuss the medical details of Pike’s condition or the IV complications. The New York Post and The Hill attribute the claim about the IV complications to Pike’s attorneys, while the Washington Examiner and Axios do not specify who made the claim. The BBC provides more context about the victim, Colleen Slemmer, and includes quotes from a journalist who has covered many executions, while the other accounts do not mention this.

Sources New York Post · BBC · Washington Examiner · The Federalist · Axios · The Hill

Brazil's election pits Lula against Flávio Bolsonaro

Brazilians are voting in a presidential election on Sunday, with Luíz Inácio Lula da Silva, the left-wing incumbent, facing Flávio Bolsonaro, the son of former president Jair Bolsonaro. Lula, 80, is seeking a fourth term, while Bolsonaro, 45, is running as the standard-bearer of his father's political movement. Jair Bolsonaro, who served as president from 2019 to 2023, is currently serving a 27-year sentence for plotting a coup after losing the 2022 election. The election will determine whether Brazil continues under Lula's left-leaning government or returns to the Bolsonaro movement. Polls suggest a tight race, with both candidates hovering around 40% in the latest surveys, and a runoff likely if neither secures more than 50% of the vote.

The election has broader implications, particularly for U.S. influence in Latin America. The Trump administration has imposed tariffs on Brazilian goods, sanctioned a Supreme Court justice, and designated Brazil's two largest gangs as terrorist organizations. A Bolsonaro victory could align Brazil more closely with U.S. policies, including a hardline approach to crime and a focus on securing critical minerals. Lula, meanwhile, has emphasized Brazil's sovereignty and criticized the U.S.-led "war on drugs" for its impact on the Amazon and Brazil's democratic institutions.

Where the accounts differ

BBC reports that Flávio Bolsonaro used an AI-generated image of his father to endorse his candidacy, while The Daily Caller notes that Flávio met with Trump at the White House in May and pledged Brazil would join Trump's "Shield of the Americas" initiative if he wins. Newsweek and Axios both mention the U.S. sanctions on Brazilian Supreme Court Justice Alexandre de Moraes, but Newsweek adds that Trump ordered 50% tariffs on Brazilian goods in July 2025, while Axios states the tariffs were imposed in the summer of 2026. The Guardian and NPR both describe the election as a rematch of the 2022 contest, but The Guardian adds that Trump has been actively promoting right-wing candidates in Latin America, while NPR highlights the potential impact on Brazil's Supreme Court and environmental policy.

Sources BBC · Newsweek · The Guardian · NPR · Axios · The Daily Caller

Coast Guard finds debris from medical plane carrying 6 people

The U.S. Coast Guard confirmed finding debris off Nantucket, Massachusetts, from a medical transport plane that disappeared while flying from Bermuda to Boston. The plane, a Gulfstream G100, was carrying four Canadian and two Bermudian nationals. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) reported losing contact with the aircraft early Saturday, prompting a search. The Coast Guard deployed search and rescue assets, including planes and a helicopter, to locate the missing aircraft and any potential survivors.

The plane, operated by Latitude Air Ambulance, was en route to Boston Logan International Airport when it vanished. Flight data indicates the aircraft dropped rapidly in altitude before losing communication. An air traffic controller asked the pilot, "You're coming in blind? Is that what you said?" and later confirmed the pilot had declared an emergency. Emergency crews were warned to prepare for the plane's arrival due to reported electrical issues and loss of radio and radar.

Where the accounts differ

NPR and The Guardian both state the plane dropped at least 9,000 feet over two minutes before leveling off, while FOX News and CBS News report the plane plummeted from 24,000 feet to 11,000 feet in three minutes. The Guardian and CBS News mention the plane was set to land at Boston Logan International Airport at 1:30 a.m., while NPR and The Daily Wire do not specify a landing time. FOX News and The Guardian attribute the altitude drop to FlightAware data, while CBS News and BBC cite FlightAware as well. The Daily Wire and BBC mention the plane was flying at 24,000 feet before descending 13,000 feet in about two minutes. The Guardian and CBS News note the FAA issued a Search and Rescue Alert Notice at about 1:20 a.m., while NPR and The Daily Wire report the FAA alerted the Coast Guard at roughly 2 a.m. and 1 a.m., respectively.

Sources NPR · FOX News · The Daily Wire · The Guardian · CBS News · BBC

Trump, Cotton tussle over daylight savings after cell phone number leak

President Trump repeatedly criticized Sen. Tom Cotton (R-Ark.) over his stance on a daylight savings bill after posting the Republican Congress member’s cell phone number on social media. In a morning Truth Social post, the president encouraged his supporters to call the senator and urge him to back legislation that would make daylight savings time the permanent standard time. The House passed the Sunshine Protection Act of 2025 in July with broad bipartisan support, but the bill is stalled in the Senate. Trump accused Cotton of holding up the legislation and urged Arkansas voters to call the senator, citing the need for bipartisan support and the benefits of permanent daylight savings time.

Cotton responded with a statement on social media, reiterating his opposition to the bill. He argued that Congress has enacted permanent daylight savings time before but repealed it due to the negative effects of late sunrises in the winter. He emphasized that Arkansans do not want their children to go to school in the dark and that he would always put Arkansas first.

Where the accounts differ

French Students Protests Escalate Into Violence Across Schools

French high school students have escalated their protests over overcrowded classrooms, teacher shortages, and deteriorating school facilities into violent clashes with police and rioters. The unrest, which began in working-class areas near Paris, has spread to major cities across France, with reports of schools being set on fire and students using Molotov cocktails. Over 1,000 schools were affected by the protests, with more than 500 high schools shut down or disrupted. The violence has led to arrests, injuries to both students and staff, and government officials blaming the far-left France Unbowed (LFI) party for inciting the unrest.

The protests, initially sparked by student grievances over poor educational conditions, have grown into widespread riots. Students have blocked school entrances, set buildings on fire, and engaged in violent confrontations with law enforcement. The government has accused the LFI of orchestrating the violence, while student leaders and teachers unions have called for better funding and resources for schools. The situation has drawn national attention, with political figures and media outlets debating the causes and consequences of the unrest.

Where the accounts differ

The Daily Wire reports that over 560 high schools were completely shut down or disrupted, with more than 160 forced to lock their doors entirely. It also states that 2,000 agitators were arrested, with 90% being minors, and that 65 school staff and 170 students were injured. The Nation, however, mentions that nearly 2,000 people were arrested, with scores of schools closed by administrative order, but does not specify the number of injured. The BBC notes that the LFI is accused of inciting the protests, while the Nation attributes the spread of the movement to a combination of student anger and political factors. The Straight Arrow News states that more than 400 schools were closed Friday, while The Daily Wire reports over 500. The NPR account mentions 5,000 arrests, a figure not included in other reports. The FOX News account states that 2,000 people were arrested, aligning with The Daily Wire and the BBC, but differs in the number of injured and damaged schools.

Sources The Daily Wire · The Nation · BBC · Straight Arrow News · NPR · FOX News

Trump announces $90 checks for 20 million seniors to help cover Medicare premiums

President Trump announced Friday that over 20 million Medicare Part B enrollees will receive one-time payments of $90 to help cover their premiums. The White House fact sheet clarifies that the payment will be $90 per person, with most recipients receiving the money via direct deposit in early October. The funds are sourced from the Medicare Improvement Fund, which Trump labeled a "Slush Fund" used by Democrats to increase healthcare costs. The standard Part B premium in 2026 is $202.90, meaning the $90 payment will offset less than half of the monthly cost. Trump also mentioned that the Medicare Improvement Fund was established in 2008 for improvements to original Medicare’s fee-for-service program.

The administration stated that the payment is separate from other refund initiatives, including $500 checks for over 950,000 Americans who were allegedly overcharged for Obamacare. Trump emphasized that the Medicare checks are part of his effort to protect and preserve Medicare and Social Security, which he claims were on the verge of ruin under President Biden. He also reiterated his promise of a $5,000 "Trump Dividend" for all Americans if Republicans win the midterms.

Where the accounts differ

Axios reports the payment is $90, while Fox Business and Newsweek also mention $90, but Fox Business adds that Trump claims the funds come from a "Slush Fund" used by Democrats to pay for "Waste, Fraud, and Abuse for their Special Interest friends." Newsweek attributes the same characterization to Democrats. The New York Post also mentions the "Slush Fund" label but does not specify which party it attributes to. Axios notes that the $90 payment offsets less than half of the monthly premium, a detail also included in Newsweek. Fox Business and the New York Post mention the $5,000 "Trump Dividend" as a separate promise, while Axios and Newsweek reference it as well. The New York Post and Fox Business include Trump's claim that the Medicare Improvement Fund was used by Democrats to raise healthcare costs, which Axios and Newsweek attribute to Trump.

Sources Axios · Fox Business · Newsweek · New York Post

Feds charge shop clerk, son with selling illegal kratom after 2 Ole Miss deaths

Federal authorities have charged a store clerk and his son with selling an illegal kratom product following the deaths of two University of Mississippi students. Prosecutors stated that both deaths appear consistent with drug overdoses, though it remains unclear if kratom contributed. The investigation is ongoing, with toxicology reports still pending. One student, Aidan Hamilton, made multiple purchases from a vape, tobacco, and beer store in Oxford, Mississippi, according to court documents. An informant purchased kratom-based pills from the clerk, Yahya Mohamed Mosleh, who warned the informant about the risks. Both Mosleh and his son, Islam Yahya Mosleh, face one count of possession with intent to distribute a controlled substance. The DEA has banned three kratom-related compounds, one of which was found in products linked to the students.

The store where the kratom was sold is under investigation, with federal agents seizing kratom products from the location. The owner of the gas station, Ibrahim Aydah, denied that the pills found near one of the students came from his store, citing differences in packaging and ingredients. He claimed the pills found near the student had a price label and contained different ingredients than those he sells. The DEA confirmed that products listing "pseudoindoxyl" are banned under federal law.

Where the accounts differ

Additionally, FOX News reports that the owner of the gas station, Ibrahim Aydah, denied the pills came from his store, a claim not made by CBS or The Guardian.

Mass protests in Spain over housing crisis highlight systemic eviction threats endangering thousands' homes and livelihoods.

Spanish PM Sánchez loses key housing crisis vote after eviction of woman, 87

An 87-year-old woman, Maricarmen Abascal, was evicted from her Madrid apartment in September 2026 after failing to pay a rent increase. The incident sparked widespread protests across Spain, with demonstrators demanding an end to evictions, lower rents, and stronger tenant protections. The government introduced two decree-laws in response, but both were rejected by Congress. The rejection has intensified pressure on Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez to call a snap election before his term ends in 2027. Protesters have occupied central squares in Madrid and other cities, with some calling for a general strike over the housing crisis.

The protests have drawn attention to the broader issue of rising housing costs in Spain, with many residents struggling to afford rent. Some demonstrators argue that the government’s proposed measures are insufficient, while others believe the crisis has been exacerbated by policies favoring landlords. The eviction of Abascal, who had lived in her apartment since 1956, became a symbol of the housing struggles faced by many Spaniards, particularly the elderly and low-income families.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian reports that 70,000 people protested, while the Guardian also states that organizers claimed 500,000 attended. The BBC reports that the Tenants’ Union claimed 500,000 protesters, while authorities put the number at 70,000. The Christian Science Monitor does not specify a number but notes that “over a thousand people are now camped in Madrid’s most central square.” The Guardian and BBC both mention that the government introduced two decree-laws, but the Christian Science Monitor refers to them as “measures” without specifying they were decree-laws. The Guardian and BBC attribute the rejection of the first decree to a vote of 178 to 172, while the Christian Science Monitor does not specify the vote count.

Sources Christian Science Monitor · The Guardian · BBC
