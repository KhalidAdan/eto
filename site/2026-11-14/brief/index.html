Duffy, Mullin call for $50B air travel 'overhaul' in fiery ground stop briefing

The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) grounded all U.S. flights for nearly four hours on Friday due to a communications issue, according to multiple accounts. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin held a briefing after the FAA lifted the ground stop, calling for $50 billion in funding to overhaul the nation’s air traffic control system. Duffy stated the system is “incredibly old” and that the outage was caused by overloaded routers leading to inconsistent data. Mullin emphasized there is “zero evidence” of a cyber-attack and that the situation is a telecommunications outage. The FAA confirmed the airspace was reopened at around 8:15 p.m. EST, though normal operations are expected to take time to resume.

The ground stop, the second such event since the Sept. 11, 2001 terror attacks, affected over 10,218 flights, with more than 3,000 canceled, according to FlightAware. The FAA cited “extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety” for the ground stop. Some college football games were impacted, with teams scrambling to adjust travel plans, including switching to buses or altering kickoff times. The situation caused widespread chaos, with passengers stranded and airports in disarray.

Where the accounts differ

The Hill and The Guardian both state the FAA issued the ground stop at around 4 p.m. EST, while Just The News reports it was issued at 3:36 p.m. EST. The Hill and The Guardian mention the FAA’s SMART program as an AI tool, while The Guardian and Just The News note that the outage was not connected to this system. The Hill and The Guardian report the ground stop lasted nearly four hours, while Just The News states it was the second such event since the Sept. 11, 2001 attacks. The Hill and The Guardian mention the last widespread stoppage was in 2023 due to a contractor outage, while Just The News refers to the 2023 incident as a pilot notification system issue. The Hill and The Guardian report the ground stop was lifted at 8:15 p.m. EST, while Just The News states the FAA confirmed the airspace was open at that time. The Hill and The Guardian mention the FAA’s last widespread stoppage was on Sept. 11, 2001, while Just The News refers to it as a ground stop issued for the 9/11 terror attacks.

All passengers and crew accounted for after New Orleans boat crash in ‘miracle on the Mississippi’

A tanker collided with a river cruise ship docked on the Mississippi River in New Orleans on Friday afternoon, resulting in 32 people being taken to local hospitals. New Orleans Mayor Helena Moreno described the incident as a “miracle on the Mississippi,” noting that no one was killed or critically injured. The American Heritage, operated by American Cruise Lines, was struck by the Atlantic Asphalt tanker, which had lost control. The cruise ship eventually rolled onto its side, with some passengers and crew ending up in the river. Rescue efforts by multiple agencies, including the Coast Guard and Harbor Police, were credited with ensuring all individuals were accounted for.

The American Heritage had 197 passengers onboard when it was hit, according to Port of New Orleans press secretary Kimberly Curth. About two hours before the collision, the ship had docked to begin passenger disembarkation. Officials confirmed that the tanker lost steering control, though the exact cause remains under investigation. Video footage from the scene showed the tanker T-boning the side of the cruise ship, leading to the vessel rolling over. Witnesses described the event as surreal, with some people in the water and others on the remaining part of the ship.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian and CBS News also mention that 32 people were hospitalized, with none in critical condition, while The Daily Wire and Just The News report the same. Mississippi Couldn’t Stop Scott Colom’s Victory—But It Wasn’t for a Lack of Trying

Democrat Scott Colom won the U.S. Senate race in Mississippi, defeating Republican incumbent Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith by about 4,600 votes. Colom, the first elected Black senator from Mississippi in over 150 years, will be sworn in next year. The race, which took over a week to call, highlighted the state’s archaic voting system and the legal challenges that followed. Election officials will finalize remaining affidavit ballots, which were affected by a voter roll purge. Both campaigns are preparing for potential legal action, as Mississippi does not have laws for automatic vote recounts or runoff elections. Hyde-Smith’s campaign demanded that counties separate affidavit ballots from regular and absentee ballots, a move similar to one the Trump administration made in California.

Where the accounts differ

Slate and ABC News both note that Hyde-Smith’s campaign demanded counties separate affidavit ballots, but Slate attributes this to Mississippi law, while ABC News does not specify the legal basis. Axios and BBC both mention the historical significance of Colom’s win, but Axios provides specific county-by-county vote totals, while BBC focuses on the emotional impact and grassroots efforts. The Nation highlights the historical context by comparing Colom’s win to Hiram Revels’ 1870 election, a detail not included in the other accounts. NPR mentions the White House warning Democrats about future investigations, a point absent in the other outlets.

Sources Slate · Axios · The Nation · NPR · ABC News · BBC

Trump, Xi and Putin to hold first three-way summit in China

U.S. President Donald Trump, Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian leader Vladimir Putin are set to hold an unprecedented three-way meeting next week on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit in Shenzhen, China. The White House confirmed the meeting, which will focus on nuclear arms control and the Iran war, though no agreement is expected. Trump plans to avoid discussing Russia’s war in Ukraine during the meeting. Elon Musk will travel with Trump to China but will not attend the meeting. The summit has drawn comparisons to the 1945 Yalta Conference, though Russian officials have dismissed the parallel.

The meeting will be the first trilateral meeting between the leaders of the U.S., China and Russia. Trump is expected to discuss nuclear stockpiles and the Iran war with Xi and Putin, but not Ukraine. The New Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty (New START) between the U.S. and Russia expired in February, and Trump has rejected Russia’s proposal to extend it, demanding a new deal that includes China. The White House has described the meeting as the start of a conversation rather than a negotiation.

Democrat Eileen Laubacher defeats Republican Lauren Boebert in Colorado’s 4th Congressional District

Republican Rep. Lauren Boebert lost her re-election bid in Colorado’s 4th Congressional District to Democratic challenger Eileen Laubacher, according to the Associated Press. The race was extremely close, with Laubacher securing 48.8% of the vote compared to Boebert’s 47.9%, as 99% of ballots were counted. Libertarian candidate Douglas Mangeris received 3.3% of the vote. Laubacher, a retired Navy rear admiral, previously served on the Biden administration’s National Security Council. Boebert, who represented the district since 2024, was running for her fourth term in the House. The race marks another Democratic flip in a Republican stronghold, contributing to Democrats gaining control of both chambers of Congress. Laubacher’s victory follows a broader trend of Republican losses in midterm elections, with Democrats now holding 256 seats in the House compared to Republicans’ 169.

Laubacher, who moved to the district in 2024, ran as a common-sense candidate focused on affordability, ending corruption, and improving rural healthcare. She previously served as a Republican and later switched parties, citing dissatisfaction with the GOP. Boebert, a vocal Trump ally, faced multiple controversies during her tenure, including allegations of inappropriate conduct, ethics complaints, and comments about the 2020 election. Despite these issues, she occasionally criticized Trump, such as when she supported a bill to release the Epstein files, which led to Trump calling her a “carpetbagger” and “dumb.”

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian and CBS News also note that Laubacher changed her party affiliation in 2025, a detail not included in FOX News or The Daily Caller. The Daily Caller and The New York Post report that Boebert’s campaign lawyer claimed “unusual voting patterns” and expected a recount, which is not mentioned in other accounts. Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins wins race for Alaska governor, replaces a Republican

Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins won the race for Alaska governor, according to a race call by the Associated Press. He will replace outgoing Republican Gov. Mike Dunleavy, who has been in office since 2018. Kreiss-Tomkins, 37, will be the youngest governor in state history. The race was conducted under Alaska’s ranked-choice voting system, which requires a majority of votes to win. Kreiss-Tomkins defeated three Republican candidates, including one backed by President Trump. The state’s last Democratic governor was Tony Knowles, who left office in 2002. The AP race call came after over a week of counting votes, including those from remote areas. A separate AP race call indicated that a ballot measure to scrap the ranked-choice system failed, keeping it in place.

Kreiss-Tomkins, known by his initials JKT, made his "high-energy, bipartisan, problem-solving approach" a centerpiece of his campaign. He emphasized a willingness to work across party lines and described himself as a "man of the middle." He was endorsed by three former governors — a Democrat, a Republican and an independent — along with Republican Sen. Lisa Murkowski. The state faces challenges including rising costs, flat state funding, and a looming natural gas shortage. Voters have also been migrating out of the state for over a decade.

Where the accounts differ

NPR and Newsweek both state that Kreiss-Tomkins won with over 50 percent of the vote, avoiding a ranked-choice runoff. However, Newsweek reports that Kreiss-Tomkins secured 50.8 percent of the vote, while NPR does not specify the exact percentage. CBS News and the New York Post do not mention the exact vote share, though CBS notes that the AP called the race. The New York Post states that Kreiss-Tomkins finished first in August’s open primary, followed by a second Democrat, Tom Begich, who withdrew from the race. NPR and Newsweek do not mention Begich’s withdrawal. The New York Post also notes that Trump endorsed Bernadette Wilson, while Newsweek and CBS do not specify which candidates Trump endorsed. NPR and Newsweek both mention that the ranked-choice system was kept in place, but NPR adds that the measure to scrap it failed, while Newsweek does not.

Sources NPR · New York Post · Newsweek · CBS News

Israel Used a U.S.-Made Missile to Kill Six at Gaza’s Nasser Hospital

The Israeli airstrike on Nasser Hospital in Khan Younis, Gaza, killed six people, including surgeon Dr. Rizk Abu Shaaira, and wounded 52 others, according to Gaza health officials and Médecins Sans Frontières. The attack occurred on Wednesday evening, with victims including a disabled man and a patient of Abu Shaaira. The hospital’s emergency, surgery, radiology, and laboratory departments were closed following the strike, which damaged multiple floors and caused structural collapse in some areas. The Israeli military claimed it targeted a Hamas commander, though it did not provide evidence. The attack is the fourth on a hospital since the so-called ceasefire agreement in October 2025 and the seventh on the Nasser complex, including a double-tap strike in August 2025.

The strike occurred during a time when surgical patients were waiting for operations, according to Dr. Ayman Abu Ubaid, a wounded surgeon at the hospital. He described the attack as sudden and devastating, with thick smoke, fires, and casualties. The hospital director, Dr. Ahmed al-Fara, noted that Nasser Hospital is one of the last functioning healthcare facilities in Gaza, having been attacked seven times since the war began. The hospital has also been damaged in previous strikes, including one that killed five journalists in a double-tap attack last year.

Where the accounts differ

The Intercept reports that the Israeli military used a U.S.-made GBU-39/B missile, manufactured by Boeing, in the attack, and that the strike was the fourth on a hospital since the October 2025 ceasefire. It also mentions that the attack was the seventh on the Nasser complex, including a double-tap strike in August 2025. The Intercept attributes the claim about the U.S.-made missile to Feroze Sidhwa, who described it as active participation in the murder of healthcare workers. The Guardian does not mention the missile type or its manufacturer, nor does it reference the October 2025 ceasefire or the August 2025 double-tap strike. The Guardian also includes details about Seif Ghazi Abu Hatab, a patient killed in the strike, which The Intercept does not mention.
