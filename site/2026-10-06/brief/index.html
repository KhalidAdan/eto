Fort Hood shooter to be executed by firing squad - a first for US military since World War Two

The Pentagon has announced it will execute a former US Army major by firing squad, the first such military execution in more than 60 years. Nidal Malik Hasan fatally shot 13 unarmed US soldiers at Fort Hood in 2009, injuring another 32. Pentagon spokesperson Sean Parnell announced on social media on Monday that Secretary of War Pete Hegseth had made the recommendation and President Donald Trump approved it. The execution will mark the first military execution by firing squad since the end of World War Two. Hasan, a US Army psychiatrist from Virginia, killed 13 soldiers at the base in Texas in 2009 and later admitted in court he was the gunman and apologised for "any mistakes". He said during his court-martial in 2013 that he believed he was protecting Muslims and the Taliban in Afghanistan. The attack on 5 November 2009 was the deadliest ever non-combat attack on an American military base. The attack took place in a crowded medical building where deploying soldiers were awaiting check-ups and vaccines. Hasan is currently one of four inmates on the US military's death row. A military execution requires approval from the US president, according to military law.

Where the accounts differ

BBC states the execution will be the first since the end of World War Two, while The Hill says it will be the first since 1961. The Guardian and Newsweek do not specify the exact year of the last military execution by firing squad. Just The News and FOX News both mention 1961 as the last military execution by firing squad. The Guardian and Newsweek differ in the number of casualties, with the Guardian stating 12 soldiers and 30 wounded, while Newsweek says 13 dead and more than 30 wounded. FOX News attributes the claim that Hasan said "I shot to kill" to a Sanity Board report, while the other accounts do not mention this. The Guardian and Newsweek provide more detail about Hasan's background and mental state, while FOX News includes information about Hasan's alleged visit to a strip club and his actions during the attack.

Sources BBC · Just The News · The Guardian · Newsweek · The Hill · FOX News

Christa Pike survived failed execution attempt in Tennessee

Christa Pike, a convicted murderer, was scheduled for lethal injection in Tennessee on September 30, 2026. The execution was halted by a federal court stay just before it began, leading to a nine-hour wait at the prison. After the stay was lifted, Pike was taken to the death chamber, where she was administered two doses of pentobarbital. She remained conscious for part of the process, breathing heavily and snoring, before being taken to a hospital. Her lawyer, Stephen Ferrell, stated she remains critically ill and unconscious, with an unclear prognosis. Tennessee Governor Bill Lee suspended all executions for the rest of the year following the botched attempt.

Pike was sentenced to death in 1996 for the 1995 murder of Colleen Slemmer, a 19-year-old classmate. Her lawyers had previously warned that her medical condition, including a platelet disorder and small veins, made lethal injection a risky and potentially painful method. The state had previously botched an execution attempt in May 2026 with Tony Carruthers, leading to a suspension of executions. The Department of Correction's communications director, Dorinda Carter, announced the execution was proceeding after the stay was lifted.

Where the accounts differ

NPR reports that the execution was halted by a federal court stay and that the process took place in the death chamber with witnesses present. BBC and The Nation describe Pike as remaining unconscious and critically ill, with her lawyer stating the prognosis is unclear. The Nation adds that Pike's lawyers had warned about the risks of lethal injection due to her medical history. Straight Arrow News mentions that prison officials followed a protocol requiring a second dose if the first failed, leading to life-saving measures. Washington Examiner and New York Post note that Pike may not fully recover and that her condition remains critical. The Nation also highlights the broader context of botched executions in Tennessee and the state's history of failed attempts.

Jim Bakker, famed televangelist felled by scandal, dies at age 86

Jim Bakker, the televangelist who built a Christian broadcasting empire before it collapsed in scandal, has died at the age of 86. His second wife, Lori Bakker, announced his death on social media, stating he "went home to be with the Lord." Bakker and his first wife, Tammy Faye Bakker, became one of television's most recognized couples with their PTL show, which preached to millions of viewers that Christian faith and financial success were compatible. Bakker's world came crashing down in 1987 when he admitted to a sexual relationship with a secretary named Jessica Hahn, who accused him of abuse. Obama to campaign with fellow Democrat Abdul El-Sayed in key Michigan Senate race

Former President Barack Obama will campaign alongside Michigan Democratic Senate nominee Abdul El-Sayed in Detroit on October 24, according to multiple outlets. The event will also feature Democratic Secretary of State Jocelyn Benson, who is running for governor. The Senate race is considered one of the most competitive in the midterm cycle, with the outcome potentially determining control of the Senate. Obama has been selective in his campaign appearances since leaving office but has increased his public presence in the final stretch of high-stakes elections.

Obama’s involvement in the race follows a pattern of increased participation in recent midterms, including appearances in Detroit during the 2022 and 2024 elections. He has also recorded a series of campaign ads for Democratic candidates, which will be released in the coming weeks. The event is expected to draw large crowds and prominent guests, with some outlets noting that Obama’s presence could provide a significant boost to El-Sayed’s campaign, particularly in Detroit, where he remains highly popular.

Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor launches legal action against police over Epstein raids

Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor is challenging the legality of search warrants that led to police raids on his homes in February 2026. The legal action, which includes a judicial review, is being heard in a private high court hearing before Mr Justice Hilliard. The warrants were issued by a judge at the Central Criminal Court and allowed police to search his Sandringham estate residence and his former home, Royal Lodge. Mountbatten-Windsor was arrested on suspicion of misconduct in public office, following the release of documents from the U.S. Justice Department’s Epstein investigation. The arrest occurred on his 66th birthday, and he was released under investigation after being questioned for several hours. The legal challenge is part of a broader dispute over the validity of the warrants and the evidence obtained during the searches.

Thames Valley Police confirmed they are involved in legal proceedings related to the warrants but declined to comment further. The police investigation into misconduct in public office continues, with allegations that Mountbatten-Windsor shared sensitive information with Epstein during his tenure as the UK’s trade envoy. The legal action marks the first time a member of the royal family has challenged the lawfulness of criminal proceedings against them since the trial of Charles I in 1649.
