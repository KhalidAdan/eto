Hakeem Jeffries named House Speaker after Democrats secure historic majority

Hakeem Jeffries, a New York Democrat, was nominated as the next House Speaker by House Democrats, securing his position with a large majority. Jeffries, who previously worked as a corporate litigator, has emphasized his faith and community ties, particularly in Brooklyn. His leadership style is described as collaborative, focusing on listening to members before formulating strategy. Democrats have gained a significant majority in the House, with 258 seats, the largest since 1992, following a sweeping midterm victory. The party plans to use its majority to push for legislative action on issues like healthcare, affordability, and immigration, while also launching investigations into the Trump administration.

Where the accounts differ

NPR describes Jeffries as a strategic, deliberate politician with a focus on listening and avoiding ideological pigeonholing. The Hill highlights the challenges Jeffries faces with a large and diverse caucus, including balancing the interests of moderate and progressive members. CBS News and FOX News both note the historic nature of the Democratic majority, with CBS emphasizing the 258-seat tally and FOX mentioning the defeat of Virginia Foxx. The Daily Wire focuses on the diminished influence of the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) due to the large Democratic majority, noting only eight DSA-affiliated members in the House. The New York Post and The Hill both mention the DSA's potential impact on policy, but The New York Post does not reference the DSA's influence, while The Hill does.

Sources NPR · The Hill · New York Post · CBS News · FOX News · The Daily Wire

Russian warship sinks off Libya as suspected Ukrainian strikes take war beyond Black Sea

Ukraine claims to have launched a drone attack on a Russian warship docked at a naval base in Benghazi, Libya, resulting in the vessel's sinking. President Zelenskyy stated that seven Ukrainian naval drones were used in the operation, which also targeted a Russian cargo ship. Libyan officials confirmed an explosion at the naval base, with satellite images showing the warship capsizing and sinking. The vessel, the Merkury, was reportedly refueling and resupplying under a cooperation agreement with Libya's eastern-based government. Libyan authorities called for an international investigation into the attacks, emphasizing that Libya is not a party to the Russia-Ukraine war.

The attack on the Russian warship and a commercial ship off the coast of Benghazi is part of a broader pattern of Ukrainian long-range strikes against Russian assets. Zelenskyy described the operation as "exemplary," highlighting Ukraine's ability to conduct such attacks from distant locations. The UN mission in Libya expressed "serious concern" over the drone attacks, calling for an "immediate, independent and transparent investigation." Libyan officials also condemned the use of their territory for foreign conflicts, stating that Libya will not allow its ports or waters to be used as an arena for settling scores.

Where the accounts differ

FOX News reports that a second drone attack hit a commercial ship off the coast of Benghazi, with Libyan military officials attributing the attack to Ukrainian sea drones launched from a base in western Libya. NPR and Al Jazeera also mention the attack on the commercial ship, but Al Jazeera notes that the Libyan health ministry confirmed all crew members were rescued and in stable condition. CBS News and BBC do not specify the number of injured on the commercial ship, while The Guardian mentions that the number of injured and their nationalities are not known. Additionally, FOX News and The Guardian attribute the attack on the warship to Ukrainian drones, while BBC and CBS News do not explicitly state who launched the drones, though they cite Zelenskyy's claims.

Sources FOX News · NPR · Al Jazeera · CBS News · BBC · The Guardian

Trump, Putin, and Xi met in Shenzhen for a trilateral summit

President Donald Trump met with Russian President Vladimir Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping in Shenzhen, China, during the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) conference. The summit, described by some as historic, included a roundtable discussion among the three leaders. The meeting resulted in a four-point declaration focusing on peace, strategic stability, and further dialogue, though it lacked specific commitments on nuclear arms limits, verification, or Ukraine and Iran. Trump referred to Putin as a “friend” during the meeting, a characterization that drew both domestic and international criticism. The U.S. intelligence chief, Jay Clayton, defended Trump’s remarks, calling them “entirely appropriate” for building a personal rapport with the Russian leader.

The summit also drew attention for its implications on U.S. foreign policy. Military analyst Phillips O'Brien noted that the U.S. appears to be moving closer to Russia than to Ukraine or its European allies, suggesting that Ukraine's support will increasingly rely on Europe. O'Brien criticized the U.S. for hampering Ukraine's defense capabilities through actions like shooting down drones, which he said undermines Ukraine's ability to defend itself.

Where the accounts differ

The Washington Examiner and The Guardian both report that the summit produced no nuclear arms limits, no verification provisions, no negotiating timetable, and no agreement on Ukraine or Iran. The Daily Caller and The Dispatch, however, emphasize the lack of concrete commitments and the potential implications for U.S. foreign policy, with The Dispatch framing the meeting as a sign of America’s “slide into an amoral foreign policy.” The Al Jazeera and The Guardian accounts highlight the controversy over Trump’s characterization of Putin as a “friend,” with The Guardian noting that Clayton explicitly stated Putin is not America’s friend in Ukraine. The Daily Caller includes a quote from Senator John Kennedy describing Putin and Xi as “predators” who want to “eat America’s liver with some fava beans and a nice Chianti,” a characterization not present in other accounts.

US Army invokes emergency powers to detonate lead in Iowa

The US Army plans to detonate 68 barrels of lead azide, a toxic explosive, at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant. The operation is scheduled for Wednesday, November 25, 2026, to allow for optimal weather conditions and mitigation measures. The Army claims it must act under emergency powers to bypass a court order that temporarily halted the detonation. The court had issued a stay after environmental groups sued, arguing the Army failed to consider safer alternatives. The Army and its interagency partners say the detonation is necessary to prevent an uncontrolled explosion as temperatures are expected to drop below 30°F.

Environmental groups, including the Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club, have criticized the Army’s decision, arguing that the court order was issued to ensure transparency and public safety. They claim the Army did not provide sufficient notice to affected communities and that the risk of an uncontrolled explosion is not as imminent as the Army claims. The Army has advised people to avoid the area during the detonation and urged those who cannot leave to take precautions.

Where the accounts differ

The New York Post and CBS News both state the detonation is scheduled for Wednesday, November 25, 2026, while the Washington Examiner and BBC report the Army planned to act by Tuesday, November 24. The Guardian and BBC mention the Army had initially planned the detonation for Friday, November 20, but later moved it to Monday, November 23, before the court intervened. The Washington Examiner and BBC note the Army had previously postponed the plan due to public outcry, while the New York Post and CBS News attribute the delay to the court order. The Guardian and BBC also mention the Army had removed the doors to the bunker where the barrels were stored, making the explosives more vulnerable to temperature changes, a detail not included in the other accounts.

Sources New York Post · Washington Examiner · The Guardian · CBS News · BBC

Climate change talks fall apart in final hours over fossil fuels

International climate talks in Antalya, Turkey, collapsed on Sunday after a dispute over whether to explicitly mention fossil fuels in the final agreement. Delegates from nearly 200 countries failed to reach a consensus, with the European Union and several other nations rejecting a draft that did not include explicit language on phasing out fossil fuels. The talks, which ran two days longer than expected, became the longest climate conference in the history of these summits. The EU, represented by Ireland's Minister for Climate, Energy and the Environment Darragh O'Brien, walked out of the room in protest, citing the need to address fossil fuels as the main cause of global warming. The final agreement failed to include a commitment to transition away from fossil fuels, which make up roughly 73% of global emissions.

The failure to reach an agreement highlights deep divisions between nations. Some oil-producing countries like Saudi Arabia opposed language that would require a transition away from fossil fuels. The United Nations found that a best-case scenario of 1.5 degrees Celsius warming would require cutting emissions by 45% by 2035, which would necessitate a dramatic reduction in fossil fuel use. Meanwhile, Australia's climate and energy minister Chris Bowen stated that the transition away from fossil fuels is happening regardless of what the U.N. agreements say.

Where the accounts differ

NPR and CBS News both report that the EU, represented by Ireland's Darragh O'Brien, rejected the draft agreement for not mentioning fossil fuels. Fox Business adds that the draft omitted fossil fuels entirely and instead focused on carbon capture and climate finance, which was also a point of contention. BBC notes that while the central agreement failed, some other items on the agenda were agreed upon, including a just transition framework and a pledge to increase electricity's share of global energy use to 35% by 2035. Axios highlights the broader implications of the failure, including calls for reforming the COP process and the role of fossil fuels in climate change.

Sources NPR · CBS News · Fox Business · BBC · Axios

Trump orders contractors to check with White House over any congressional inquiries

President Donald Trump issued an executive order directing federal contractors and subcontractors to consult with the White House before responding to congressional inquiries. The order mandates that companies withhold communications deemed privileged by the administration and coordinate with the White House on "appropriate responses" to such requests. Non-compliance could result in contract termination or suspension of future federal work. The directive is part of the administration's strategy to resist anticipated aggressive oversight efforts from Democrats following their expected control of Congress in January. The White House has also instructed federal agencies to seek approval from its counsel before agreeing to congressional appearances.

White House Counsel Will Scharf informed agencies that some congressional investigations may amount to "blatant criminality," and he dismissed requests from Rep. Robert Garcia, the top Democrat on the House Oversight Committee, to preserve documents, arguing that Democrats are not yet in the majority and cannot compel compliance.

Where the accounts differ

The Guardian and NPR both report that the executive order requires contractors to consult the White House and withhold privileged communications. The Washington Examiner and FOX News also describe similar directives, though the Examiner's account is more limited in detail. The Guardian and NPR mention that the order is part of the administration's effort to resist Democratic oversight, while FOX News adds that the order is framed as pushback against lawmakers "harassing private companies" to obtain confidential information. The Guardian and NPR attribute the claim about "blatant criminality" to Scharf, while FOX News attributes it to the White House in general. The Examiner does not mention Scharf's comments or the "blatant criminality" characterization.

Rasmus Kieffer-Kristensen, head of Denmark’s national security and intelligence service, confirmed that the assassination attempt was planned by Russian intelligence services. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen expressed solidarity with Denmark and called for a new European security strategy to enhance preparedness and deterrence.

Environmental crimes: Israel blamed for toxic pollution in 99% of Gaza
