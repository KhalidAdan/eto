The ‘Cornell 7’ Fallout Is Infantilized Women And Villainized Men

Jane Doe, a former Cornell University student, alleges she was gang-raped by seven men at a Chi Phi fraternity house in October 2024. She claims she was intoxicated and under the influence of ketamine during the incident. Her lawsuit names the Chi Phi fraternity, the Tri Delta sorority, and Cornell University as defendants. The case has sparked widespread protests and debates over campus sexual misconduct and Title IX policies. Jane Doe’s lawyer, Thomas Giuffra, has argued that the responsibility for ensuring her safety rested with others, and that she concealed her intoxication from authority figures. In text messages obtained by CBS News, Jane Doe and two fraternity members discussed the incident, with one expressing regret over the events. Cornell University has since banned all fraternity parties and announced new policies to improve support for survivors of sexual assault.

Where the accounts differ

The Federalist reports that Jane Doe’s lawsuit does not claim she was forced, only that she wasn’t capable of taking responsibility for her actions because she got drunk. The Federalist attributes this to Jane Doe’s own contemporaneous account. Slate states that Jane Doe’s complaint describes a 360-degree failure on the part of Cornell and the Greek life it supports, including that she left her sorority house to bar hop before stumbling to the Chi Phi fraternity. The Nation claims that Jane Doe’s complaint to the Cornell University Police Department was minimized by the police, omitting the fact that she claimed to have been raped. CBS News reports that Jane Doe’s lawyer, Thomas Giuffra, described her messages as “classic symptoms of delayed recognition” and called it “a textbook response to sexual assault.” The Nation also states that the district attorney declined to bring charges after an investigation in 2024. The BBC reports that Cornell’s president said the school would allow New York State Police’s trauma-informed officers to investigate allegations of sexual assault on its campus. The Federalist and Slate differ on the nature of the incident, with the Federalist referring to it as a “gangbang” and Slate as a “gang rape.” The Nation and CBS News differ on the legal outcome, with the Nation stating that the district attorney declined to bring charges and CBS News stating that the district attorney declined to bring charges after an investigation in 2024.

Trump's Russia diesel deal and Ukraine's Starlink restrictions spark controversy

President Donald Trump announced a deal to import 3 million tons of Russian diesel over the coming months, aiming to lower U.S. fuel prices. The deal, which includes a temporary waiver of sanctions on Russian oil exports, has drawn criticism from lawmakers and analysts. Diesel prices in the U.S. have remained high, averaging $6.27 per gallon, with analysts noting that the deal would only provide a small fraction of the country's daily diesel consumption. Meanwhile, Ukraine reported restrictions on its use of Starlink and Starshield satellite services, which are critical for military operations. Ukrainian officials suspect the restrictions were intentional, possibly linked to Trump's public criticism of President Zelensky.

The U.S. Treasury Department temporarily waived sanctions on Russian diesel exports, allowing the country to sell the fuel globally. However, the deal has faced pushback from both Democrats and some Republicans, who argue it undermines sanctions and could benefit Russia's war efforts. Analysts suggest that the global diesel shortage, driven by conflicts in the Middle East and Ukraine, is a more significant factor in high prices than the Trump-Russia deal.

Where the accounts differ

NPR states that Trump claimed Russia would release 300,000 tons of diesel to the U.S. and global markets immediately, while Straight Arrow News reports that the deal includes 3 million tons of diesel over the coming months. Axios and The Hill describe the Starlink restrictions as a potential act of retaliation against Ukraine for attacking Russian refineries, while Washington Examiner links the restrictions to Trump's public criticism of Zelensky. The Hill and Axios attribute the Starlink restrictions to SpaceX, while The Hill also notes that Musk pointed the finger at the U.S. government for imposing limits on Starshield.

Houthis Launch Attacks on Saudi Airports, Sparking Escalation in Regional Conflict

The Houthis, an Iran-backed group, launched attacks on Saudi Arabia’s King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, killing at least 12 people, including an American citizen, and injuring over 300. The attacks disrupted air travel and raised concerns about Saudi Arabia’s ability to defend its territory. In response, Saudi Arabia launched strikes on a Yemeni oil depot and intercepted missiles aimed at its energy infrastructure, including the headquarters of Saudi Aramco. President Donald Trump warned the Houthis against further attacks and suggested the U.S. might consider military action. The conflict, which dates back to 2014, has seen escalating violence, with both sides targeting each other’s energy and military assets.

The Houthis have intensified their attacks, including strikes on Saudi airports and oil facilities, which they claim are in retaliation for Saudi air strikes in Yemen. Saudi Arabia has accused the Houthis of seeking to disrupt global energy supplies and has vowed to hold them accountable. The conflict has also drawn international attention, with the International Monetary Fund warning of potential negative economic spillovers. Meanwhile, Trump has criticized the Houthis for their actions and hinted at possible U.S. involvement, though he has not announced any specific military measures.

Where the accounts differ

Major U.S. Bank Hit With $1.21 Billion In Penalties After Ignoring Red Flags

The Treasury Department announced more than $1.21 billion in penalties against Bank of America for anti-money laundering and sanctions violations. The enforcement actions include the largest fine in the history of the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) and the third-largest penalty in Treasury history. According to Treasury, Bank of America failed to report more than $5 billion in suspicious transactions on time, including activity indicative of Chinese money laundering networks. The bank also processed hundreds of payments that appeared to violate U.S. sanctions targeting Iran, Russia, and other countries. Bank of America admitted to willfully violating the Bank Secrecy Act by failing to maintain an adequate anti-money laundering program, conduct required checks on accounts for foreign financial institutions, and file suspicious activity reports. The bank said it will record a $1.2 billion, non-tax-deductible charge in its third-quarter results, reducing net income by about 17 cents per share.

Treasury investigators found that the bank relied heavily on law enforcement inquiries and warnings from other financial institutions to identify suspicious activity instead of using its own systems to flag potential problems. The bank also allowed suspicious transactions to go uninvestigated for extended periods, delaying reports to authorities. Treasury said it failed to respond promptly to concerns about customers receiving payments from unknown third parties, including potential connections to narcotics trafficking proceeds. Key compliance staff lacked adequate training, and internal audits failed to identify longstanding problems later uncovered by regulators.

Where the accounts differ

The Daily Wire and Fox Business both mention the $1.21 billion total penalty, including the $750 million from the OCC and the $11.6 million from OFAC. The New York Post and Newsweek also report the $1.2 billion penalty, but Newsweek notes that the $750 million from the OCC is credited against the $1.2 billion penalty, not added to it. The BBC and Axios both state that the $1.2 billion penalty includes the $750 million from the OCC and the $11.6 million from OFAC. The Daily Wire and Fox Business mention the $1.21 billion total, while the New York Post and Newsweek refer to the $1.2 billion penalty. The Daily Wire and Fox Business also note that the $1.2 billion penalty includes the $750 million from the OCC and the $11.6 million from OFAC, while the New York Post and Newsweek do not specify the breakdown of the penalty.

Judge blocks Trump's White House ban targeting CNN, MS NOW and Politico

A federal judge ruled that President Trump cannot ban CNN, MS NOW and Politico from covering him at the White House, at least temporarily. The judge found that Trump's decision to revoke the press credentials of more than 70 journalists from these outlets likely violated their First Amendment and due process rights. The news organizations filed a lawsuit after the Trump administration denied them access, and the judge granted a temporary restraining order on Sept. 24. The ruling was issued after the Justice Department announced it would appeal the decision. The White House criticized the ruling, calling the outlets "Fake News" and claiming they are capable of reporting from outside the White House.

Consumer inflation rose faster than expected in September, CPI report shows

The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) reported that the consumer price index (CPI) increased 0.6% on a monthly basis in September, with an annual rate of 3.7%. Energy prices rose 5.2% monthly, contributing significantly to the inflation spike. Gasoline prices increased 9.1% from the prior month and are up 34.2% from a year ago. Core inflation, which excludes volatile energy and food prices, rose 0.3% monthly and 2.6% annually. The report highlights persistent inflation pressures, with energy costs and other factors driving price increases.

The report comes ahead of the November midterm elections, with rising consumer prices becoming a major issue for both political parties. The Federal Reserve raised interest rates for the first time since 2023 to combat inflation, lifting the target range to 3.75%-4.0%. Analysts suggest the Fed may need to continue raising rates as inflation remains above its 2% target.

Where the accounts differ

Fox Business and The Guardian both report that gasoline prices increased 9.1% from the prior month in September, while The Daily Wire and CBS News report the same figure. The Guardian and CBS News attribute the rise in energy prices to the U.S. war with Iran, while The Daily Wire also mentions the conflict with Iran as a cause. The Guardian and CBS News note that fuel oil prices increased 74% from last year, a detail not included in Fox Business or The Daily Wire. The Daily Wire and The Guardian mention Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's comments about inflation returning to the Fed's target after the Iran conflict ends, which is not included in Fox Business or CBS News. The Guardian and CBS News report that the September inflation rate is the highest before a midterm election since 1990, a claim not made by Fox Business or The Daily Wire.

Sources Fox Business · The Daily Wire · The Guardian · NPR · Axios · CBS News

Massive breach of Republican voter and donor data threatens election integrity and personal privacy for thousands.

Former Special Counsel Jack Smith obtained personal data on 639,000 Republican voters and donors, including MAGA hat buyers

Former Special Counsel Jack Smith and the Biden Justice Department collected personal information on over 639,000 Republican voters, donors, and supporters, including details such as names, addresses, and credit card information, according to a memo released by Senate Judiciary Committee Chairman Chuck Grassley. The data included information on individuals who purchased red MAGA hats and Trump memorabilia, including shipping addresses. Grassley criticized the collection of such data as "invasive" and beyond what was necessary for the investigation. Smith’s team also obtained information on Trump campaign products such as hats, t-shirts, mugs, and door mats.

The memo, obtained by multiple outlets, revealed that Smith’s office collected data on donors who contributed as little as $1, and that the information spanned from 2005 to 2021. Grassley stated that the data collection was part of an effort to "map out the entire Republican Party apparatus." Smith, during a Senate hearing, confirmed that his team acquired financial information but could not recall every subpoena issued.

Where the accounts differ

FOX News reports that Grassley’s memo includes a claim that Smith’s team obtained "spreadsheets containing Republican campaign donors, supporters, and detailed personal voter information in various formats, totaling over 639,000 individuals, at minimum," and that "over 148,000 rows are identified in the spreadsheets as donors." The New York Post and The Federalist both cite this same figure, but The Daily Caller reports a slightly higher number of 640,000. The Federalist also notes that the data included "last four digits of credit card numbers" and "donor analysis data (such as one-time versus recurring donations and the amount of the donation)," which are not mentioned in the other accounts. FOX News and The Daily Caller both mention that Smith’s team obtained information on the sales of red MAGA hats and their buyers, while The New York Post and The Federalist also include this detail. The Daily Caller adds that Smith’s team obtained "banking information" on thousands of donors, including "home addresses, bank account and routing numbers and signatures," which is not mentioned in the other accounts.

Sources FOX News · New York Post · The Federalist · The Daily Caller
