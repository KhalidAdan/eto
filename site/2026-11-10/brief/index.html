Europe to join U.S.-brokered Ukraine talks in Abu Dhabi, defying Kremlin objections

U.S.-mediated peace talks between Russia and Ukraine are set to resume in Abu Dhabi this week, with European representatives expected to participate despite Russian objections. France's foreign minister, Jean-Noël Barrot, confirmed France's involvement, though he did not specify who would represent the country. The talks, which will take place on Tuesday and Wednesday, are the first since February and follow a U.S. visit to Russia by envoy Steve Witkoff. The U.S. aims to address postwar security arrangements and de-escalation, with the Trump administration emphasizing the need for European involvement in any lasting peace deal. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has expressed concerns about the potential territorial concessions Ukraine may be required to make, warning of the risks involved.

The talks will include discussions on postwar security and humanitarian issues, with the U.S. seeking to involve European countries in shaping a new security framework. Russian delegation leader Vladimir Medinsky will again represent Moscow, alongside senior officials including Deputy Foreign Ministers Sergei Ryabkov and Mikhail Galuzin. The U.S. has also invited European allies to Abu Dhabi to hold separate discussions, aiming to integrate them into the broader security dialogue. Despite the U.S. optimism, analysts remain skeptical about the likelihood of significant progress, given the deepening divide over territorial control and security guarantees.

Supreme Court says border wall construction can continue in west Texas

The U.S. Supreme Court on Monday allowed the Trump administration to continue with its plans to construct hundreds of miles of border barrier and security infrastructure in west Texas. The decision came after the federal government filed an emergency request to lift a lower court's temporary halt on construction. The court ruled that the federal judge who issued the injunction did not have the authority to block the project, allowing the government to proceed with its plans. The Trump administration argued that the construction is necessary to secure the border and prevent illegal crossings, citing the need to address gaps in "border defenses."

The construction plans include physical walls, access roads, and surveillance systems in the Big Bend region, which spans over 500 miles and covers more than a quarter of the U.S.-Mexico border. The project has faced bipartisan opposition, with residents and environmental groups arguing that the construction threatens the natural landscape, wildlife, and local economies. The Center for Biological Diversity and other groups have challenged the plans, claiming they violate environmental protections and harm public lands.
