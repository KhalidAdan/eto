### Clarence Thomas calls foul on California ruling he says could turn citizens into government mouthpieces

The U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear a challenge to California’s requirement that licensed physicians complete continuing medical education (CME) courses including discussion of implicit bias. The case involves Dr. Azadeh Khatibi, a physician who argues the state compels her to convey a government-favored message in violation of the First Amendment. California maintains that while instructors must include discussion of implicit bias, they are not required to personally endorse the theory and may openly disagree with it. The Ninth Circuit previously ruled that CME courses constitute government speech due to California’s extensive regulation of the medical profession, meaning the state’s requirements are not subject to First Amendment scrutiny.

Justice Clarence Thomas, joined by Justice Samuel Alito, dissented from the Supreme Court’s decision not to take up the case. They argued that California’s implicit-bias-training requirement compels instructors to affirm the state’s view that implicit bias causes disparities in health outcomes, thereby violating First Amendment protections. Thomas warned that allowing the law to stand could have implications beyond the medical field, potentially enabling states to compel speech from private citizens.
