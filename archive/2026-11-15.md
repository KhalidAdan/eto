### SpaceX launches Starship test flight with partial success

SpaceX conducted its 15th test flight of the Starship rocket on Friday, November 13, 2026, from its Starbase facility in Texas. The mission lasted 22 hours, with the rocket reaching orbit and deploying 26 Starlink V3 satellites. The upper stage of the rocket, known as Starship, successfully returned to the launch site and was caught by mechanical arms on the launch gantry, marking a significant milestone. However, the lower stage, or Super Heavy booster, was diverted to a splashdown in the Gulf of Mexico due to a technical issue, where it exploded before impact. SpaceX had previously successfully caught the Super Heavy booster on three earlier test flights, but this was the first time the upper stage was captured after reaching orbit.

The flight was intended to demonstrate full reusability of the Starship system, with both stages returning to the launch site. While the upper stage was successfully retrieved, the lower stage's failure to be caught represents a setback. SpaceX plans to attempt a catch of the upper stage again in the near future, as part of its ongoing efforts to develop a fully reusable rocket system.

**Where the accounts differ**

CBS News reports that the technical problem preventing the return-to-launch-pad capture of the booster was not immediately known, while BBC and The Guardian attribute the diversion to a guidance, navigation, and control (GNC) system triggering the no-go due to the booster not being in the correct position. The Hill and NPR state that the booster was diverted due to a detected guidance problem, with NPR adding that the engines stopped firing too soon. BBC and The Guardian mention that Elon Musk noted the booster was "slightly low on fuel," a detail not included in CBS News or The Hill. The Daily Wire does not mention the technical problem or the fuel issue, focusing instead on the significance of the attempt.

### Judge temporarily blocks Trump from moving ahead on Washington public golf course redevelopment

A federal judge on Saturday issued a temporary restraining order blocking the Trump administration from closing East Potomac Park and the East Potomac Golf Course, which is located near the White House. The order, issued by U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes, delays any work on the redevelopment project until at least November 28, 2026. The administration had planned to close the site on November 15 to begin renovations. The plaintiffs, including the DC Preservation League and two local golfers, argued that the closure would irreparably harm the public interest and change the character of the accessible golf course. Trump had previously announced the closure date on social media, linking it to the renovation plans.

Reyes noted that Trump's public statements suggested the closure was final and that the National Park Service would close the park to implement the plan, which supported the plaintiffs' request for a stop-work order. The judge ruled that the government must keep the park and golf course open during the hours they were accessible to the public as of November 14, 2026, but did not require active management of the site. The Justice Department stated that the order does not prevent planning for a world-class golf course.

**Where the accounts differ**

NPR and New York Post both report that the judge issued a temporary restraining order blocking the closure of the park and golf course, while The Hill and Newsweek emphasize the judge's skepticism of the administration's safety arguments. The Hill and Newsweek note that the judge found the government's justifications for closure—such as safety concerns and lack of an operator—were likely pretextual. BBC and The Hill mention that the administration had cut down hundreds of trees without prior notice, which led to an earlier court order halting tree removal. ### U.S. loses measles elimination status

The United States has lost its measles elimination status, according to multiple international health organizations. The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) determined that sustained measles transmission has occurred for more than 12 months, marking the first time since 2000 that the U.S. has not met the elimination criteria. The decision follows a surge in measles cases, with over 5,400 confirmed infections reported in 2026, more than double the number from 2025. The majority of cases are in unvaccinated individuals, and public health officials emphasize that vaccination rates must remain above 95% to maintain herd immunity.

The loss of elimination status signifies that measles is now endemic in the U.S., spreading in pockets of unvaccinated communities. Pennsylvania has been the epicenter of the outbreak, with over 1,500 cases and six measles-associated deaths reported. The CDC notes that these numbers are likely undercounts due to limited testing and underreporting of mild cases. The Infectious Diseases Society of America called the loss of status a "tragedy," highlighting the preventable nature of the outbreak and the potential long-term health impacts on infants infected this year.
