### Christa Pike survived a botched execution in Tennessee

Christa Pike, a 50-year-old woman convicted of murdering her classmate Colleen Slemmer in 1995, survived a botched execution attempt in Tennessee on September 30. The execution, which involved two doses of the sedative pentobarbital, failed to kill her, leading to her hospitalization. Pike regained consciousness and was able to speak and walk by early October, according to her attorneys. The event has sparked national debate about the reliability and ethics of the death penalty. The Tennessee Department of Correction confirmed that no pending order exists for another execution attempt, though the possibility remains open.

Pike’s attorneys stated she was discharged from the hospital and returned to the Debra K. Johnson Rehabilitation Center in Nashville, where she has been housed for over 30 years. They described her as grateful to the medical team and first responders for their care. The state has ordered an investigation into the failed execution, and a judge has mandated the preservation of all evidence related to the incident. Pike’s case has drawn attention to the flaws in the lethal injection process, with experts highlighting the lack of medical expertise in administering the procedure.

### Panama declares state of emergency as aftershocks of 7.7- and 6.6-magnitude earthquakes shake communities

Panama’s government declared a state of emergency on Saturday following two powerful earthquakes that struck the country on Friday. The 7.7- and 6.6-magnitude quakes caused widespread damage across all 10 provinces and two Indigenous territories, with buildings collapsing and evacuations ordered. Seismologists recorded over 100 aftershocks, including several measuring 5.8, 5.5, and 5.2. The initial quake, centered about 10km (6 miles) southwest of Pitaloza Arriba in Herrera province, struck at a depth of 10km (6 miles). Officials reported 27 injured individuals, though no deaths were confirmed. Damage was concentrated in central provinces, with 420 homes and the same number of public schools affected, along with seven hotels, 13 churches, and nine bridges. In La Villa de Los Santos, a colonial-era community, homes and a historical Catholic church collapsed. The US Department of State mobilized a disaster response team, and the US military delivered 2,000 pounds of food to affected areas.

The earthquakes were felt in Panama City, where buildings shook and people evacuated into the streets. President José Raúl Mulino stated that no deaths or injuries had been documented as of Friday evening, though he warned that this could change. The US Pacific Tsunami Warning Center initially issued a tsunami alert but later canceled it. Mulino called for calm, emphasizing that the situation should not be used to cause alarm or disorder. The US military’s southern command deployed two helicopters to assist with relief efforts.

### Federal judge: DOJ cannot request states' voter lists for eligibility database

A federal judge ruled on Friday that the Justice Department’s nationwide effort to collect unredacted voter registration records from states to identify suspected non-U.S. citizens is unlawful. The decision, issued by U.S. District Judge Sparkle Sooknanan in Washington, blocked the Trump administration from continuing the program even in states that voluntarily cooperated. The judge found the department exceeded its legal authority and violated federal privacy laws by requesting sensitive voter information and sharing it with other agencies to check citizenship status. The ruling struck down the DOJ’s entire voter list maintenance policy, invalidating it nationwide. The decision followed multiple state-level challenges against the Justice Department’s rule. The lawsuit was brought by voting rights group Common Cause and four voters, who accused the administration of attempting to create a “sprawling new voter surveillance and purging apparatus” ahead of November’s midterm elections.

The program began last year after an executive order from President Trump directed federal officials to work with states to identify potential election law violations. The DOJ requested unredacted voter registration lists nationwide, seeking sensitive information including names, addresses, and partial Social Security numbers. At least 18 states complied, allowing the department to share their voter records with the Department of Homeland Security and check citizenship status through the federal Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements (SAVE) database. Participating states also agreed to remove voters identified as ineligible within 45 days of notification. The judge cited a 2006 Social Security Administration audit that found approximately 3.3 million U.S. citizens had been misidentified as noncitizens.

The Hill and The Guardian both state that at least 18 states complied with the DOJ’s request, while Straight Arrow News says about two dozen Republican-controlled states cooperated. The New York Post mentions that nearly all 50 states were requested to hand over their complete, unredacted voter rolls, with 18 mostly Republican-led states complying. The Guardian and The Hill note that the DOJ requested unredacted voter registration lists, while Straight Arrow News refers to the DOJ demanding voter rolls. The New York Post also mentions that the DOJ sued 30 Democratic-led and Republican states that refused to hand over their unredacted voter rolls, as well as Washington, DC, and has lost 26 of those cases so far, without winning one.
